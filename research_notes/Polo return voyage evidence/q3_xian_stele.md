# Q3: The Xi'an stele (781) and Marco Polo's "silence"

Scope note: the dates below refer to four different events, kept apart: **erection** (781), **burial** (unknown and inferred), **discovery** (c. 1623–1625, disputed), and **reports** (1625 onward). "Inspected" means I read the passage myself, in the local repo text or a downloaded full text. "Snippet" means I saw only search-engine summaries or metadata. "Reported" means I rely on another scholar's account.

**Recommendation for the retelling.** Polo cannot be said to have "missed" the stele. Its availability c. 1275–1290 is unknown, and every surviving discovery account says it came out of the ground. Burial at some point is therefore near-certain, but its date is a modern inference. The retelling should not present the omission as a "famous silence that later readers have weighed": I found no scholar who uses the stele's absence from Polo as evidence for or against his presence in China. The best options:

- **Preferred:** omit the point at Kenjanfu.
- **Alternative:** keep a single reception-history sentence, using the fallback wording given at the end of section 3.

The current manuscript has two problems. `book/vol-2/ch-06-pulisanghin-sindafu.md:33` and Note [10] call it a "famous silence", and the text states "rediscovered in 1625" without qualification. Registry `facts.md` F-32 says "Found by workmen in 1625 (Yule)". That is Yule's wording, but modern sources give 1623 or 1625. (No manuscript file was edited.)

## 1. What is known about the stele between 781 and its rediscovery? Burial versus assumption; who first asserted burial?

### Takeaway
Only two things are documented: the erection in 781, which is dated in the inscription itself, and the fact that it was dug out of the ground in the 1620s. Nothing documents its whereabouts from 781 to c. 1623–25. Burial is inferred from the circumstances of discovery and from the stone's good condition. The link to the 845 Huichang edict is a conjecture, which Yule attributes to Pauthier (1857–58). One later scholar reportedly allows a burial date anywhere from 845 to the 980s.

### Cited Findings
- **Erection, primary text via the editor (inspected).** The Chinese text dates the stone to the 2nd year of Jianzhong (Kienchung), which Gaubil reckoned as 4 Feb. 781. The Syriac gives 1092 of the Greeks (= 781). — Yule–Cordier, *The Book of Ser Marco Polo*, 3rd ed. (London 1903), vol. 2, Bk II ch. XLI, note 2, fn. [4], pp. ~27–29. Local copy: `book/refs/yule-vol2.txt:2737–2770`. [Gutenberg ed.](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636) The page range is inferred from internal cross-references: the chapter starts on p. 24, the cross engraving is "at p. 30", and Odoric's note cites "ii, pp. 25 seq.".
- **Burial conjecture (editor inference, inspected).** Yule writes that Pauthier "reasonably supposed" the monument "may have been buried in 845", the year of Wuzong's edict. Yule notes that the edict also ordered the foreign monks of Daqin to return to lay life. — same note, `book/refs/yule-vol2.txt:2823–2830`. Pauthier's works are cited there as *De l'authenticité de l'inscription nestorienne* (1857) and *L'inscription syro-chinoise de Si-ngan-fou* (1858).
  - I did not inspect Pauthier. Whether he was the first to propose burial is **not verified**. He is the earliest named proponent I found.
- **Discovery reports all say the stone was excavated (secondary, inspected).**
  - Saeki gives three discovery accounts. One is a Chinese epigrapher's: workmen digging a child's grave south of the Chongren si in Xi'an's western suburb found it "buried several feet deep". The others are a farmer ploughing and Moule's trench-digging near Zhouzhi.
  - Saeki concludes they all agree it was dug out of the ground and "had been buried, no doubt, for a long, long time". He takes its good condition as a sign of long burial.
  - Source: P. Y. Saeki, *The Nestorian Monument in China* (London: SPCK, 1916), pp. 17, 21–22. [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft). I searched and read the djvu full text.
- **Burial motive is asserted, not documented (inspected).** Saeki, pp. 89–90, says the stone "was buried by Christians who wished to preserve" it from Wuzong's destruction. He offers no evidence beyond the 845 context. — [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft)
- **Survival of the church after 845 (reported).** Moule (*JNCBRAS* 41, 1910, p. 79, as quoted by Saeki p. 22) argued that the original church at the capital "seems … to have survived that edict (of A.D. 845)". He used this as an argument that the stone was erected and found at Zhouzhi rather than in Xi'an. — Saeki 1916, p. 22 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft)
- **The 845 date is hedged in modern accounts (snippet).**
  - Wikipedia says the stone is "thought to have been buried in 845", citing Mungello 1989, p. 165, and "probably" buried in the Huichang persecution, citing McGrath 2021. — [Wikipedia, Xi'an Stele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi%27an_Stele)
  - A 1990 article in the *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society Hong Kong Branch* reportedly says the stone was probably buried by Xi'an's Christians "possibly as early as 845, possibly as late as the 980s". — [hkintexts RAS-1990 p. 73](https://hkintexts.histsyn.com/series/histsyn-rashkb-journal-engine/items/RAS-1990?p=73). The page returned 403, so I saw only a search snippet. Author and title are **not verified**.
- **"Forgotten" (editor framing, inspected).** Yule says the monument's discovery "a thousand years later disclosed their forgotten existence", meaning the Tang churches. — `book/refs/yule-vol2.txt:2533–2535`

### Inferences
- That the stone was buried before its discovery is well supported: every account says it was excavated. **When** it was buried is not documented. 845 is a plausible conjecture, not established fact, and a post-Tang date (10th c.) has been proposed.
- There is no positive evidence that the stone stood above ground c. 1275–90. All the discovery evidence points toward it being underground, but this is an inference. The safe phrasing is "probably buried long before Polo's time".
- I found no evidence that it was destroyed or moved. "Obscurity" without burial is not supported by any source.

### Gaps
- I did not inspect Pauthier 1857/1858, Havret *La stèle chrétienne de Si-ngan-fou* (vol. 2, 1897, the history of the monument), Pelliot/Forte 1996, Keevak 2008, Deeg, or Nicolini-Zani. So I cannot say who first asserted burial before Pauthier, or how Pelliot/Forte date it.
- I could not read the 1990 JRASHKB article (403).

## 2. Discovery date and circumstances: 1625 certain, or 1623/1625? What do Semedo, Trigault, Kircher, Li Zhizao and Zhang Gengyou say?

### Takeaway
1625 is not certain. Here are the earliest and best-attested witnesses:
- Li Zhizao's colophon (12 June 1625) says the stone was dug out "recently".
- Trigault's papers say "this year, 1625".
- Emmanuel Diaz (1644) gives the 3rd year of Tianqi, i.e. 1623.

Saeki (1916) and Moule settle on early 1625, and some modern sources give 1623 or a range (1623–25; Wikipedia even gives 1621–25). The place is also disputed: Xi'an's western suburb near the Chongren si, Zhouzhi, or somewhere in between. Yule's "accidentally found in 1625 by some workmen … in the Chang-ngan suburb" is the 1903 consensus, not a settled fact.

### Cited Findings
- **Yule–Cordier 1903 (inspected).** The stone "was accidentally found in 1625 by some workmen who were digging in the Chang-ngan suburb of the city of Singanfu".
  - Source: vol. 2, Bk II ch. XLI, n. 2, fn. [4]; `book/refs/yule-vol2.txt:2702–2706`.
  - Yule refers to *Cathay* p. xcii seqq. and p. clxxxi seqq. for a fuller account. I did not inspect *Cathay* 1866.
  - Cordier adds that Havret's *La stèle chrétienne* parts 1–2 appeared at Shanghai in 1895 and 1897, and that Havret died on 29 Sept. 1901 before part 3. (Lines 2813–2820.)
- **Li Zhizao (primary, in translation; inspected via Saeki).** His colophon of 12 June 1625 says Zhang Gengyou of Qiyang sent him a rubbing with the words "recently in Ch'ang-an, they dug out a stone". — Saeki 1916, pp. 17–18 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft)
- **Xu Guangqi, 1627 (inspected via Saeki).** He likewise places the dig "in Ch'ang-an". — Saeki p. 18
- **Emmanuel Diaz (primary, via Saeki).** In his *Jingjiao bei song zheng quan* (1644), Diaz dates the discovery to the 3rd year of Tianqi (1623), "at the base of a ruined wall in Kuan-chung", with workmen digging by official order. — Saeki pp. 18, 19
- **Trigault (reported).** Saeki quotes from Trigault's diary, at second hand: "This year, 1625, outside the country-town of Chou-chih … a stone was discovered".
  - Saeki says Trigault saw the stone at the Jinsheng si in October 1625.
  - A footnote credits A. C. Moule for the point that six statements give 1625, one "certainly" and another "probably" by Trigault. One of them reads *hoc anno 1625 inventi*.
  - Moule's own conclusion, quoted there: "Early in the year 1625, perhaps about the beginning of March", near Zhouzhi.
  - Source: Saeki pp. 16–21 [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft)
- **Saeki's own conclusion (inspected).** The date "so far no one has ascertained … exactly". It is "generally supposed" to be 1625. The stone was found before March 1625 and after 1620, which he infers from Aleni's ignorance of it on his 1620 visit to Shaanxi. — Saeki pp. 19–21. A Chinese epigrapher, "Mr. Ch'ien", placed it between 1573 and 1620 (Saeki p. 20).
- **Place (inspected).** Saeki lists four theories:
  - Sanyuan (Martini);
  - the western suburb of Xi'an near the Chongren si (the Chinese epigrapher's version: a grave for the child of an official);
  - Zhouzhi (Trigault, Moule);
  - a spot between the two (Saeki's own view).
  - Source: Saeki pp. 16–19.
- **Modern sources disagree (snippet/metadata).**
  - Childers, *Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage*, "Xi'an", has Jesuits finding it "in 1623 or 1625". — [GEDSH](https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/Xian)
  - Lieu, "Christians and Manichaeans on the Silk Road", *European Review* 33.4 (2025), gives 1623, "in or near the village of Zhouzhi". — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/97ED83314CFBD8DFFA438F3CE7DDE415/core-reader)
  - Wikipedia gives "between 1621 and 1625", citing Ma Tianji (2025, *International Journal of Asian Christianity*) for "most researchers" and an ongoing debate. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi%27an_Stele)
- **Semedo (reported).** He was the first European to see the stone, between 1625 and 1628. Mungello, following Legge, prefers 1628. Semedo's account in *Imperio de la China* (1641/42) has workers reporting the find to the governor, who set it on a pedestal under a roof. — [Wikipedia, citing Mungello pp. 168–169](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi%27an_Stele). I did not inspect Semedo.
- **Kircher (reported).** *China monumentis … illustrata* (1667) was the first European publication of the full Chinese and Syriac text with a Latin translation. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi%27an_Stele). Yule also cites Kircher for more Syriac names than appear on rubbings (`yule-vol2.txt:2791–2793`).
- **Keevak (metadata).** *The Story of a Stele: China's Nestorian Monument and Its Reception in the West, 1625–1916* (Hong Kong UP, 2008). The title takes 1625 as its starting date. — [HKU repository](https://digitalrepository.lib.hku.hk/catalog/tb09jj77f)

### Inferences
- The safest wording is "unearthed in the 1620s (c. 1623–25)". Alternatively: "known to Jesuits and Chinese Christians by 1625". The earliest firmly dated document is Li Zhizao's colophon of June 1625. Bare "1625" or "rediscovered in 1625" overstates certainty. Diaz's 1623 is the main rival, and modern authorities split between the two.
- "Found by workmen" is common to most accounts. "Accidentally" and the exact place are disputed.

### Gaps
- I did not inspect Havret vol. 2 (1897), Pelliot/Forte 1996, Keevak 2008, Semedo, Trigault's own text, Kircher, or the Chinese original of Li Zhizao's colophon. The Trigault and Diaz statements come at second hand via Saeki and Moule.
- I did not inspect Moule's *Christians in China before the Year 1550* (1930); no open full text was found. Nor did I inspect Moule's JNCBRAS 41 (1910) article.

## 3. Have scholars used its absence from Polo as an argument? History of the argument versus whether he could have seen it

### Takeaway
I found **no** scholar who uses the stele's absence from Polo as evidence for or against his presence in China. Yule–Cordier does not; Wood-style skeptical arguments, as recorded in the repo, rely on other omissions. The nearest arguments run the other way:
- Rockhill, via Cordier, notes that the Uighur monks Markos and Sauma (c. 1276) also never mention Chang'an. Rockhill uses this to question Chang'an as a Nestorian see in Yuan times, not to judge Polo.
- A 1990 JRASHKB article reportedly argues that Yuan-era Christians did not know of the tablet, so it "had been long underground" when Polo arrived. That article treats Polo's non-mention as non-evidence.

Calling the omission a "famous silence that later readers have weighed" is therefore unsupported.

### Cited Findings
- **Polo's own text at Kenjanfu (primary, Yule's translation; inspected).** It mentions no Christians at Kenjanfu: "The people are Idolaters" (said twice). — Yule 1903, vol. 2, Bk II ch. XLI, p. ~24; `book/refs/yule-vol2.txt:2340–2373`. I checked only Yule's composite text. Other redactions (F, Z, Ramusio) were not checked.
- **Rockhill via Cordier (inspected).** Rockhill finds it "strange" that Mar Yahballaha (Markos) and Rabban Sauma, travelling west c. 1276, mention Tangut (Ningxia) as a Nestorian centre but "do not once refer to Hsi-anfu or Chang-an". He reasons that had it been an episcopal see, "one would think that these pilgrims would have visited it". — Yule–Cordier vol. 2, n. 2, fn. [4], citing Rockhill, *Rubruck*, p. 157 n., and Chabot, *Mar Jabalaha*, p. 21; `book/refs/yule-vol2.txt:2776–2787`.
  - This is the argument currently in ch-06 Note [10]. It concerns the strength of the Christian community at Xi'an, not Polo.
- **Yule's framing (inspected).** Yule presents the stele as information about Xi'an's history and treats the controversies as about its authenticity: "We have no room to speak of the controversies raised by this stone". He makes no inference from Polo's silence. — `yule-vol2.txt:2793–2795`
- **Reverse inference (snippet only).** The JRASHKB 1990 article reportedly says Christians in Yuan China "knew nothing of it" and seemingly believed they were the first to bring the Gospel to China. It concludes "we can safely conclude that when Marco Polo arrived in China in 1279 the Sian tablet had been long underground". — [hkintexts RAS-1990 p. 73](https://hkintexts.histsyn.com/series/histsyn-rashkb-journal-engine/items/RAS-1990?p=73). Seen only as a search-engine summary; the page returned 403.
- **Recent work linking Mongol-era Christianity to the Polo debate (inspected via fetch).** Lieu (2025) asks whether Mongol-era Christianity bears on the historicity of Polo's visit. He argues from the Fuzhou "Christians" episode and the 1313 Mar Solomon tombstone, and does not mention the stele in connection with Polo. — [Lieu 2025, European Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/97ED83314CFBD8DFFA438F3CE7DDE415/core-reader)
- **The stele's reception was about authenticity (snippet/metadata).** The Western debate (Kircher; Voltaire and Protestant charges of Jesuit forgery; later rehabilitation) concerned whether the stone was forged, not Polo. — [Lieu 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/97ED83314CFBD8DFFA438F3CE7DDE415/core-reader); [Keevak, review via Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/708473/summary)
- **The repo's own audit agrees (inspected).** `book/plans/critique/vol-3.md:94–96` (M3) and registry F-32 (`book/plans/registry/facts.md:53`) already classed the stele as a false "miss" and cut it from the III-12 essay. II-06 (`book/vol-2/ch-06-pulisanghin-sindafu.md:33`, Note [10] at :71) still calls it a "famous silence".

### Inferences
- **History of the argument.** At most, the stele's absence from Polo has been raised in passing, or used in reverse: Yuan Christians, the Rabban Sauma narrative and Polo all ignore it, which suggests it was out of sight. I found no evidence it ever carried weight in the "did Polo go to China" debate. A retelling that calls it "famous" or "weighed by readers" invents a reception.
- **Could he have seen it?** Probably not, but this is not provable. Excavation in the 1620s, the apparent ignorance of Yuan Christians, and the silence of Markos and Sauma all point the same way. None of them dates the burial.

### Fallback wording (narrowest safe)
- **To omit:** simply drop the aside. This is preferred and matches the III-12 fix.
- **To keep as reception history:**
  - "Some modern readers have asked why the Book says nothing of the Christian stele of 781, now in Xi'an's Forest of Stelae."
  - "But the stone was dug out of the ground only in the 1620s (c. 1623–25), and it had probably lain buried for centuries. Polo's silence about it tells us nothing either way."
- **Do not say:** "rediscovered in 1625" (unhedged); "buried in 845" (as fact); "famous silence"; "later readers have weighed it". Do not imply Xi'an's Christian community was strong in Polo's day.

### Gaps
- I did not inspect Keevak 2008, Deeg, or Nicolini-Zani, so I cannot rule out that one of them discusses Polo's silence. Wood 1995 was not checked for a stele mention. The repo's prior notes suggest she does not use it, but that is unverified.

## 4. Evidence independent of the stele for Christians at Xi'an / Jingzhao / Kenjanfu in Polo's period

### Takeaway
I found the evidence for a Christian community at Xi'an itself c. 1275–1290 to be weak or absent:
- Polo calls Kenjanfu's people idolaters.
- Odoric's "Kansan" notice mentions no Christians.
- Markos and Sauma pass it over, as Rockhill noted.
- I found no Yuan source placing Christians at Fengyuan/Jingzhao.

Yuan Christianity is well attested elsewhere: Tangut/Ningxia, Tenduc/Öngüt lands, Zhenjiang (Mar Sargis), Dadu, Quanzhou. The 1289 Chongfu si was a central office in the capital, not a Xi'an institution. Prince Ananda of Anxi is associated with Islam (and Buddhist/Daoist patronage), not Christianity. Any "Ongut/Ananda Christian" link in the brief appears unfounded.

### Cited Findings
- **Polo, Kenjanfu (inspected).** "The people are all Idolaters" and "the people are Idolaters". The chapter's material is Mangalai's palace, silk and arms. — Yule 1903 vol. 2 Bk II ch. XLI; `book/refs/yule-vol2.txt:2347, 2362`
- **Mangala and Ananda (editor, inspected).** Mangala died in 1280 (17th year of Zhiyuan), leaving Anxi to his eldest son Ananda. Kublai then suppressed Anxi's administrative autonomy. — Cordier, quoting *Yuan-shi lei pien*, Yule vol. 2, n. 4; `yule-vol2.txt:2671–2683`
- **Ananda's religion (snippet).** He is described as converting to Islam (c. 1295), with a 2021 *Central Asiatic Journal* article qualifying claims that he persecuted other faiths. No Christian association was found. — [Wikipedia, Ananda (King of Anxi)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananda_(King_of_Anxi)); [Association for Iranian Studies listing](https://www.associationforiranianstudies.org/content/conversion-islam-mongol-ruled-hexi-china-life-and-times-prince-ananda)
- **Odoric's Kansan (inspected).** Odoric calls it the second-best and most populous province, and notes chestnuts and rhubarb. Yule identifies it with Polo's Kenjanfu/Si-ngan fu. Neither text nor note mentions Christians there. — *Cathay and the Way Thither*, new ed. Cordier (Hakluyt Society, 1913), vol. 2 (Odoric), §§ on Kansan; `book/refs/odoric-yule-cordier-1913.txt:13930–13980` (index: "Kansan … 246")
- **Markos and Sauma (inspected via Cordier).** They omit Chang'an while naming Tangut as a Nestorian centre. — `yule-vol2.txt:2776–2787`
- **Where Polo does place Yuan Christians (Yule, quoted by Saeki; inspected).** Samarkand, Yarkand, Chingintalas, Suzhou, Ganzhou, all of Tangut, Tenduc, Manchuria, Yunnan, and Zhenjiang, where Mar Sergius built two churches. Yule adds that Polo gives few definite statements for China proper. — Saeki 1916, p. 90 n. [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft). Xi'an is not in the list.
- **Yelikewen and organisation (snippet).** *Yelikewen* (Mongol *erke'ün*) is the Yuan term for Christians, attested in Chinese sources c. 1252–1315 according to Devéria. Devéria also says the term does not occur in the Xi'an inscription. — Yule 1903 vol. 1, `book/refs/yule-vol1.txt:28308–28316` (inspected); [ARI WPS 63, Mongol Responses to Christianity in China](https://ari.nus.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/wps06_063.pdf) (snippet). The 1289 Chongfu si is recalled from general knowledge and was **not verified** this session.
- **Yuan-era Xi'an Christian material (negative search).** English and Chinese searches (也里可温 陕西 奉元路 安西王) found no source placing Christians at Fengyuan/Xi'an in the Yuan period. — [aisixiang (Chen Yuan, 元也里可温教考 discussion)](https://www.aisixiang.com/data/175999.html); [GEDSH, China, Syriac Christianity in](https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/China-Syriac-Christianity-in)
- **Post-845 continuity is unclear (inspected).** Saeki (p. 90): no Chinese book mentions the Church after 845. Yule (quoted there): nothing more is known until the Mongol era, when a "new wave" of Turkic and Mongol converts appears. — [Saeki 1916](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft)

### Inferences
- There is no positive evidence that Polo encountered Christians at Xi'an. His own text gives no Christian community there. The retelling should not suggest he passed over a thriving local church or its monument.
- The Yuan-era Christian presence that matters for Polo lies in Tangut, Tenduc, Zhenjiang and the capital, and the retelling can make that point at those chapters instead.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the 1289 Chongfu si, Mar Sargis's Zhenjiang record (*Zhishun Zhenjiang zhi*), or any Shaanxi Yuan stone inscription naming *yelikewen*. Chen Yuan's *Yuan Yelikewen jiao kao* (1917) and Moule 1930 are the places to check.
- I did not check other Polo redactions (F, Z, Ramusio) for any Christian remark at Kenjanfu.
- The brief pairs "Ongut" with "Prince Ananda of Anxi". These are separate: the Öngüt are Christian (Tenduc, Prince George), whereas Ananda is Muslim. I found no evidence linking Ananda to Christianity.
