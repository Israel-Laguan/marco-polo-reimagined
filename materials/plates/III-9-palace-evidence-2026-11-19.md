The [court-farewell plate](/home/anthony/code/marco-polo-reimagined/book/media/plates/III-9-court-farewell-no-fleet.png) works as an imagined late-13th-century Yuan court. Its red timber, painted interiors, tiled roofs, and dressed stone have historical support. The weakest element is the view as a whole: a broad, uniformly paved court, continuous carved white railings, and a prominent double-eaved hall combine to evoke the later Forbidden City. The evidence supports the individual building traditions more securely than it supports that particular arrangement. **Neither Dadu nor Shangdu can be established as the site of the farewell.**

## What the palace evidence supports

**Dadu, present-day Beijing.** West of Longzong Gate, Palace Museum excavators found Yuan rammed-earth foundations and buried column-footing structures, plus gray ceramic and green-glazed tile fragments and small pieces of red wall plaster. They proposed that the foundations belonged to a gallery around Daming Hall. That attribution is provisional, and the exposed brick pavements in this trench belong to later Ming and Qing phases. The Imperial Workshops excavation also found Yuan foundations and deposits of architectural fragments, including glazed ridge ornaments, dragon-patterned eaves fittings, and molded bricks. Its plan of the whole Yuan palace is a published reconstruction. [Longzong Gate excavation report, pp. 100–103 and 108–110, figs. 7–9](https://img.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2018/06/01/u5b10bfd083109.pdf#page=8); [Imperial Workshops report, pp. 183–188, figs. 2–7](https://img.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/file/2025/12/22/1766400343NFoVsZqXp218985.pdf).

Two later descriptions give the finishes more substance. **Late-Yuan** Tao Zongyi describes cinnabar-red columns, gilded painting, glazed roof fittings, patterned stone floors, and furnished imperial couches. **Early-Ming** Xiao Xun describes the former Yuan Daming Hall with bracket work and white stone railings carved with dragons and phoenixes. Tao describes vermilion railings in his account of the hall. Polo’s account, composed around **1298** and recalling Kublai’s reign, also describes a balustraded terrace and a brightly colored roof at Cambaluc, though the terrace passage is bracketed in the Yule–Cordier edition. Together these texts make lavish finishes and carved railings credible. They leave the illustrated finials, panels, and placement open. [Tao, *Nancun chuogeng lu*, juan 21, “Palace Regulations,” pp. 245–246 in the 1998 edition](https://www.shidianguji.com/book/SBCK438/chapter/SBCK438_26); [Xiao, *Record of the Former Yuan Palace*, Daming Hall passage](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/元故宮遺錄); [Polo, Book II, ch. X, PDF pp. 506–508](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=506).

**Shangdu, or Xanadu.** The summer capital had a walled palace precinct, large permanent buildings, and a surrounding landscape used for hunting and encampment. Building Base 1 is generally identified with Da’an Pavilion. Its Yuan lower platform was only partly exposed, and the upper remains include a post-Yuan temple phase. At Building Base 2, generally identified with Muqing Pavilion, there are stone column bases, brick-paved surfaces and approaches, colored glazed fittings, and carved stone pieces. Those finds establish the materials and scale. Wang Guixiang’s published elevations and perspectives of Da’an are modern reconstructions. Polo’s account of a gilded, painted permanent palace alongside a dismantlable cane pavilion is consistent with a court that used both kinds of architecture. [Nara National Research Institute report, pp. 67–68, fig. 46](https://sitereports.nabunken.go.jp/files/attach/57/57061/138317_1_%E5%A4%AA%E6%A5%B5%E6%AE%BF%E3%83%BB%E5%90%AB%E5%85%83%E6%AE%BF%E3%83%BB%E6%98%8E%E5%A0%82%E3%81%A8%E5%A4%A7%E6%A5%B5%E6%AE%BF%EF%BC%8D%E5%94%90%E4%BB%A3%E9%83%BD%E5%9F%8E%E4%B8%AD%E6%9E%A2%E9%83%A8%E3%81%AE%E5%B1%95%E9%96%8B%E3%81%A8%E3%81%9D%E3%81%AE%E6%84%8F%E7%BE%A9.pdf); [Shangdu nomination dossier, excavation photographs, PDF pp. 339 and 345–352](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1389.pdf#page=339); [Wang, abridged republication, figs. 17–20](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_18780888); [Polo, Book I, ch. LXI, PDF pp. 453–454](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=453).

**Images and furnishings.** Ma Lin’s signed Southern Song *Evening Outing by Torchlight* gives a pre-Yuan comparison for tiled double eaves and covered walks around a planted courtyard. The National Palace Museum dates *Khubilai Khan Hunting* to **1280**. It is a court-period reference for Kublai and his entourage, with a hunting setting. Wang Zhenpeng’s *Dragon Boat Regatta*, dated **1310–1325**, gives a later Yuan comparison for architectural forms in ink and portrays a Northern Song subject. For furnishings, William of Rubruck describes Möngke seated on a couch in a dwelling lined with cloth of gold in **1254**, and also describes Möngke’s permanent palace at Karakorum. Polo’s gold-lined, elephant-borne hunting chamber supplies a precedent for Kublai’s portable luxury. The plate’s hand-carried litter and scalloped canopy remain conjectural. [Ma Lin, NPM catalogue, vol. 29, pp. 238–241](https://digitalarchive.npm.gov.tw/Collection/Detail/14534?dep=P); [*Khubilai Khan Hunting*, NPM object 745](https://digitalarchive.npm.gov.tw/Collection/Detail/745?dep=P); [*Dragon Boat Regatta*, DIA 64.75](https://dia.org/collection/dragon-boat-regatta/64512); [Rubruck, Rockhill translation, first audience and Karakorum palace sections](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html); [Polo, Book II, ch. XX, PDF p. 536](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=536).

## What can be established about the farewell

- **The embassy is independently corroborated.** A Yuan memorial from the eighth lunar month of **1290**, preserved through the *Jingshi dadian* in the **15th-century** *Yongle dadian*, recalls a third-month order to send Uladai, Abishqa, and Khoja to Arghun by way of Maʿbar. It records a decision about rations for the travelling party and gives no departure date. The Chinese entry names neither Cocachin nor the Polos. In the **early-14th-century** *Jāmiʿ al-Tawārīkh*, Rashid al-Din records Khoja and the bride’s arrival at Abhar, where Ghazan took her as his wife after Arghun’s death. His account names no Polo. [*Yongle dadian*, juan 19418, “Postal Stations,” eighth month, day 17](https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/book/YLDD00001/chapter/1loja0544gnr3); [de Rachewiltz, pp. 48–53](https://altaica.ru/LIBRARY/rachewiltz/Rachewiltz_Marco%20Polo%20went%20to%20China%201997.pdf#page=9); [Jackson, pp. 98–99 and n. 87](https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/Jackson%2520Marco%2520Polo.pdf).
- **The Polos’ participation is credible but less securely documented.** Their own account places all three on the voyage. Its match with the Chinese record on the three envoys is strong circumstantial evidence. Jackson leaves open whether they sailed with the embassy or held an official role in it. Maffeo’s **1310** will independently records the family’s possession of Mongol gold tablets, without fixing who issued them or when. [Polo, Prologue, chs. XVII–XVIII, PDF pp. 223–225](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=223); [Jackson, pp. 86 and 98–100](https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/Jackson%2520Marco%2520Polo.pdf).
- **The depicted scene has no established venue.** The prologue recounts permission to leave, the grant of **two** gold tablets to the Polos, and the party’s leave-taking. It gives no city, building, courtyard, seating arrangement, or account of a single ceremony involving everyone shown. The *Yuan shi*, compiled in **1369–70**, records Kublai’s movements toward Shangdu in both 1290 and 1291, but supplies no date for the audience. An early-1291 sailing is a scholarly inference. Even a secure sailing date from Quanzhou would locate the embarkation alone. The BnF’s tablet-giving miniature was painted in Paris around **1410–1412** and supplies no Yuan setting evidence. [Polo, Prologue, ch. XVIII, PDF pp. 224–225](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=224); [*Yuan shi*, juan 16, Zhiyuan years 27–28](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/元史/卷016); [de Rachewiltz, pp. 48–51](https://altaica.ru/LIBRARY/rachewiltz/Rachewiltz_Marco%20Polo%20went%20to%20China%201997.pdf#page=9); [BnF, Français 2810, fol. 3v](https://expositions.bnf.fr/livres/polo/grand/fr_2810_003v.htm).

I could inspect the Chinese memorial in transcription, the Shangdu dossier photographs, and the cited Palace Museum reports. I could not inspect Wei Jian’s full 2008 excavation monograph, Cleaves’s full 1976 article, or the Persian passage in a critical edition. The Persian details above follow de Rachewiltz and Jackson. [Wei Jian, catalogue record](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA86356833); [Cleaves, pp. 181–203](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2718743).

## Feature-by-feature verdict

| Visible feature | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Red columns on pale bases | **Supported as a type.** Tao explicitly describes red columns at Dadu. Shangdu preserves stone bases. The exact red, painted surface pattern, and base profiles in the plate are unverified. [Tao, juan 21](https://www.shidianguji.com/book/SBCK438/chapter/SBCK438_26); [Shangdu excavation photos, PDF pp. 345–348](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1389.pdf#page=345). |
| Dense blue-and-gold brackets | **Supported as a construction tradition, indeterminate in detail.** Interlocking timber bracket sets predate the Yuan. The painted outlines give too little information to date the assembly or recover its structural profile. [Li Jie, *Yingzao fashi*, printed 1103, juan 4, bracket diagrams](https://textbookofarchitecture.com/source/yingzao-fashi). |
| Painted beams and gold scrolls | **Plausible but unverified in this form.** Painted and gilded finishes have textual support at both capitals. The repeating blue-and-gold scheme and exact motifs are artistic choices. [Tao, juan 21](https://www.shidianguji.com/book/SBCK438/chapter/SBCK438_26); [Polo, Book I, ch. LXI, PDF p. 453](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=453). |
| Blue-gray tiled roofs and ridge tips | **Tiling and colored glaze are supported at both sites.** Excavated fragments include turquoise, green, and yellow glaze and molded end fittings. The distant slopes could read as gray ceramic or subdued glaze. Their color scheme and complete ridge profiles are indeterminate. [Tile analysis, p. 193, figs. 1–4](https://img.dpm.org.cn/Uploads/File/2020/04/15/u5e96e1c084d84.pdf#page=2); [Muqing finds, PDF p. 349](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1389.pdf#page=349). |
| Distant halls and double eaves | **Plausible as forms, indeterminate as a view.** Both capitals had multiple halls, and double eaves predate 1291. Copying the standing Hall of Supreme Harmony would be **anachronistic**. It was founded under the Ming and the current building is a Qing reconstruction. The silhouette alone cannot establish such a copy. [Ma Lin, NPM catalogue](https://digitalarchive.npm.gov.tw/Collection/Detail/14534?dep=P); [Palace Museum, Hall of Supreme Harmony](https://intl.dpm.org.cn/hallsinfo/374.html?_wap=1). |
| White carved balustrades | **Supported in kind at Dadu by later testimony.** Shangdu’s carved marble shows comparable workmanship. The published fragments do not establish the plate’s long rail runs, repeated relief panels, or rounded finials. [Xiao, Daming Hall passage](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/元故宮遺錄); [Muqing carved stone, PDF p. 351](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1389.pdf#page=351). |
| Pale paving and open courtyard | **Supported in principle, unverified in extent and material.** Shangdu has excavated brick paving and a forecourt at Base 2. Dadu texts describe paved palace surfaces and courts. The plate’s continuous pale grid could be brick or stone, and its extent and ornamental planting are conjectural. [Shangdu, PDF p. 347](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/1389.pdf#page=347); [Tao, juan 21](https://www.shidianguji.com/book/SBCK438/chapter/SBCK438_26). |
| Canopied litter on a raised platform | **Plausible but unverified.** Portable luxury, gold textiles, and raised seating fit Mongol court practice. The hand-carried litter, its textile canopy, and its use during this farewell are undocumented. [Rubruck, first audience with Möngke](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html); [Polo, Book II, ch. XX, PDF p. 536](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=536). |
| Blank gold tablets | **Supported as a narrative motif.** Polo describes two gold passes for the three Polos. A surviving late-13th-century *paiza* bears an inscription. The many identical blank faces in the plate are an illustration convention. [Polo, Prologue, ch. XVIII, PDF p. 224](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/Marco-Polo-Volume-1.pdf#page=224); [Met, *Safe Conduct Pass*, 1993.256](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39624). |

## Image-revision prompt

> Revise the setting as an imagined farewell at Kublai Khan’s court around 1291, in an unnamed Yuan palace precinct. Preserve the composition, character identities, and all existing period dress. Keep Cocachin’s unmarried braids and cap exactly as shown. Frame the group beneath a red timber gallery on plain stone bases, with clearly articulated load-bearing bracket sets and localized painted and gilded ornament. Beyond it, show part of one tiled hall through the bays, with a few muted green or turquoise eaves fittings. Use gray brick paving, a low platform, and a short length of simple stone railing at the steps. Simplify the litter canopy into a removable patterned textile over a lacquered frame and retain the cushions. Keep the fleet offstage. Preserve the blank tablets and keep the entire image free of writing, pseudo-script, seals, stamps, and captions.
