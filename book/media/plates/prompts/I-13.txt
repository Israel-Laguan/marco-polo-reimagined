Paint a new early Yuan court handscroll passage in the manner of Liu Guandao’s Khubilai Khan Hunting of 1280 and the Yuan imperial portrait album. Use fine, even dark ink contours, flat mineral washes, small precise strokes for cloth and hair, restrained cheeks, calm three-quarter human figures and profile horses. On one continuous pale warm silk ground use a lightly elevated viewpoint, a shallow stage of overlapping figures, low sparse grasses and gently receding landscape. Gentle even ageing throughout. This is Yuan figure painting, with the intimate scale and restrained colour of the hunting scroll. Avoid a later Ming or Qing palace scene, fantasy landscape and thick theatrical shading.

The court leaves the summer park at Chandu, Shangdu, about 1280, in a representative departure following Polo’s account. In the foreground two human officiants perform the libation. A Mongol ritual officiant kneels, and with a plain shallow bowl scatters a small arc of off-white mare’s milk that ends on the earth directly in front of him. He wears a simple layered ochre steppe robe and soft cloth cap. A separately identifiable Tibetan lama in an unadorned maroon monastic robe with shaven head steadies a small plain bowl beside him. They are adult people calmly carrying out a dignified ritual. Their equipment is deliberately plain. The small white stream of milk visibly falls to the earth, without glow or supernatural effect.

Just behind them, three distinct pure white adult mares wait calmly, with complete naturally drawn eyes, hooves, heads and anatomy, stocky small steppe horses in the manner of the Yuan hunting scroll. Use a simple progression in profile and three-quarter view with clean spaces between legs. Give their coats slight pale ivory and grey contours so white reads as real horses against the silk. Lead ropes are gathered loose and touch the earth. No large herd, riders or black horses.

Kublai, aged about sixty-five, observes the rite from a plain low portable seat already made ready for travel. He has a broad round face, fair-to-ruddy cheeks, dark eyes, a thin moustache and a little beard, a heavy build, and braids at his ears. He wears the white outdoor fur coat flecked with black tufts, dark fur collar and cuffs, over a red robe with subtle gold pattern, red-topped small rounded cap and boots. He is recognisably the same man as in the approved court study, here wearing the outdoor clothes visible in its mounted view. Keep him seated, with no horse attached to his seat. Near him stands one unnamed married Mongol court woman in a wide red-orange robe with a patterned crossed-collar band and pearl pendants. Her tall red boqta rises in a narrow column and flares slightly to a flat top, concealing her hair. She has no distinctive royal facial likeness.

At the other end of the same open ground, two adult court attendants are dismantling the temporary cane-and-silk palace. Their Mongol C1 clothes have a long crossed-collar robe, contrasting short outer sleeves over long narrow sleeves, a small round brimmed hat with a knob, and compact looped braids. One gathers neatly bundled cane poles and the other releases a silk stay. The low open pavilion has a shallow rounded roof with visibly split cane shingles and a gap exposing light cane ribs where part has been taken down. It stands directly on the turf, without masonry foundations. Light reddish-gilt portable columns under the eaves bear clearly legible small carved golden dragons wound around the posts and facing up to the beams. Several slender ivory and muted red silk guy-cords slope out from the framework to small ground stakes, with a clear gap between cord and post. This is a partly dismantled round or gently curved pavilion, with the exact footprint left indistinct and with no definitive archaeological reconstruction. Far back in faint ink, one quiet low brick-and-stone-faced wall with a plain dark-roofed timber hall behind it marks Chandu. Keep that distant permanent hall and wall low and small, plainly separate from the foreground temporary pavilion. Cloth and cane bundles signal departure. Count only six people on the image: the two adult officiants, Kublai, the one court woman, and the two attendants. No additional people, deities or ghosts.
