# Chapter I-2
## The House of Polo and the Elder Voyage

> *“The Great Kaan hath never seen any Latins, and he hath a great desire so to do.”*
> — Marco Polo, Prologue, ch. 3 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Venetian records, not the Book, sketch the house Niccolò and Maffeo Polo came from. [1] They were merchants of the kind commerce made substantial without ever making famous. There was a base at Constantinople (Istanbul), a trading house in Soldaia (Sudak) on the Crimean shore, and wills and property notices that survive because men of business stamped their names on paper. Tradition pushes the line back to one Andrea Polo of the parish of San Felice. [2] He was said to be father of three sons. Marco the Elder kept the Constantinople foothold and the Soldaia house; Niccolò, second of the brothers, was father of the traveller; and Maffeo was Niccolò’s partner in the ventures that follow. [3] They dealt in jewels, cloth, credit and the judgement of markets, and in the mid-thirteenth century those markets ran east along the Greater Sea.

In the year that the best reckoning settles at 1260, when Baldwin still wore the Latin crown at Constantinople, the two brothers sat there above their wares in a city whose empire was already a shadow. Within a year Michael Palaeologus would take it. [4] Men the Prologue calls *“singularly noble, wise, and provident”* took counsel to cross the Greater Sea on a venture of trade. They laid in a store of jewels and made the crossing to Soldaia, the Crimean port the Orientals called Súdák. [5]

— A quick note on the dates: the manuscripts read 1250 for this crossing, a figure that cannot stand beside the return of 1269 and a son of fifteen. Yule corrected it to about 1260, and that is the working chronology here. [6]

Having stayed a while at Soldaia, they judged their venture worth extending and set out inland, until they came to the court of a Tartar prince: Barca (Berke), third son of Juji and the first Muslim sovereign of his Chinghizide line. His residences were at Sarai, on the Volga’s lower courses, and at Bolghar (Bolgar), a city of Volga Bulgaria. [7] The book remembers him as liberal and courteous beyond the common run of Tartar princes. He received the brothers with honour, accepted the jewels they presented, and caused them to receive at least twice their value — an exchange that reads as gift as much as purchase. [8] For a twelvemonth they remained.

Then war broke the road behind them. Between Barca, lord of the Tartars of the Ponent, and Alaü (Hulagu), lord of the Tartars of the Levant, great hosts mustered in the summer of 1262. Though slaughter fell on both sides, the outcome the Book gives is backwards: Alaü’s army, under his son Abaga (Abaqa), was routed when Barca rallied and was driven back across the frozen Terek in the winter of 1263. [9] No man could travel the western road without peril of capture. Forward, the way still lay open. That war is among the better-corroborated beats in the Prologue, for Rashid al-Din and Polo’s own later war chapters agree that the cousins came to blows then.

Quitting Bolghar, the brothers made for Ucaca (Ukek), a town on the Volga that marked the extremity of the Ponent’s kingdom toward the Russian hills. [10] Thence, departing again, they passed the great River Tigris — the Volga under a classical name gone astray. Then came a desert that ran, at the Book’s count, for seventeen days; they found neither town nor village, only the tents of Tartars at pasture with their cattle. [11]

After the desert they came to a very great and noble city, Bocara (Bukhara), whose territory belonged to a king named Barac (Baraq) of the house of Sigatay (Chagatai). The Prologue, with a merchant’s geography rather than a geographer’s map, calls Bocara the best city in all Persia — loose by modern borders. Hayton likewise folds Bocara into a Persian horizon, and before the Mongol conquest those cities were widely felt to belong to the Persian cultural sphere. The praise is reach, not cartography. [12] There the brothers found themselves trapped between wars and suspicions, able neither to proceed farther nor to turn back, and in that city they abode three years. Three years is a long sojourn for men who had left Venice on a trading chance — warehouses, local partnerships, the slow learning of tongues and prices. The round number is literary duration as well as an itinerary claim. [13]

While they sojourned there, envoys bound from Alaü’s Levant to the court of the Great Kaan, Lord of all the Tartars, came through Bocara. The sight of two European merchants astonished them — Latins in that part of the earth, never beheld before. *“If ye will take our counsel”*, they urged, *“great honour and profit shall come thereof”*. The Kaan, they said, had never yet seen any Latin and burned to do so. If the brothers would ride in their company to his court, he would welcome them warmly, treat them with honour and liberality, and they would travel in perfect security. [14] The brothers asked to be shown how. Arrangements were made, and they set out in the envoys’ company, journeying a whole year northward and north-eastward before they reached the Prince’s court.

Of the marvels of that road the Prologue deliberately says nothing. Messer Mark, who later saw them likewise, would give full account in the Book that follows, so the elder voyage stays half in shadow. It is a corridor of silence through which the reader is hurried toward the audience that remade the family’s fate. The invitation itself is plausible; a possible link to Sartak’s return party of 1265 is conjecture, not proof. [15]

The audience itself is the set-piece. The Great Kaan received the two Venetians with honour and hospitality and open pleasure, and plied them with questions — how the emperors of the West kept their dignity, dispensed justice and took the field; how their kings and princes did likewise. Then he asked how the Pope and the Church stood, what was done at Rome, and what customs the Latins kept. The brothers answered everything truthfully, plainly and in order — possible, the book asserts, because they by then knew the Tartar tongue. That fluency is claimed without ceremony, as if it were simply one more tool in the merchant’s chest, though which Turkic or Mongol idiom it was is never specified. [16]

Cublay (Kublai) was greatly pleased. He took it into his head to send the brothers to the Pope, with one of his barons for company; they replied that they would gladly execute his commands. He summoned a baron named Cogatal and bade him make ready. Letters were indited in the Tartar tongue and entrusted to the two brothers and to that baron. The contents, as the Prologue reports them, ran to this purport: the Pope was asked to send as many as a hundred learned Christians — intelligent men, masters of the Seven Arts, ready to enter public controversy. They were to prove to idolaters and every other kind of folk that the teaching of Christ outshone all others. And if they proved it, the Kaan and all under him would become Christians and the Church’s liegemen. Finally he charged his envoys to bring back holy oil from the Lamp that burns at our Lord’s Sepulchre. [17]

— No Mongol original of that letter survives. The request for learned men and for oil of the Sepulchre fits Chinghizide curiosity about skilled foreigners and Latin Holy Land piety. Whether Cublay personally framed a promise to baptise the empire, or whether the brothers — or Rustichello — polished the memory for Christian readers, remains open. Of all the letter’s clauses the promise to convert is the flimsiest; Yule already doubted purely religious motives. [18]

When the Prince had charged them with all his commission, he gave them a Tablet of Gold — the *paiza* of Mongol administration. It was inscribed with an order that the three ambassadors be supplied with everything needful wherever they passed: horses, escorts, whatever they should require. Such tablets were real instruments of imperial logistics; Yuan archaeology and Persian historians corroborate the *paiza* as safe-conduct and requisition, making it one of the more institutionally solid marvels of the opening narrative. [19]

Days out on the road, the Tartar baron fell sick and could not ride. He halted at a certain city, and the brothers judged it best to leave him and carry out their commission — with which he was well content. At every halt thereafter they were honourably supplied with whatever they required; thanks, the book insists, to that Tablet of Authority from the Lord. [20]

The road ran three years to the port of Ayas (Yumurtalık) on the Gulf of Scanderoon, in Lesser Hermenia (Cilician Armenia), because they could not always proceed — stopped at times by snow, heavy rains, or great torrents. Ayas was fast becoming one of the great hinges between caravans out of Tauris (Tabriz) and the galleys of the Italian republics. From Ayas they came to Acre, arriving there in the month of April in the year 1269. There they learned that the Pope was dead — Clement IV, though the oldest French texts leave the name blank, as if memory had failed the dictation. [21]

They went to a certain wise churchman, the Legate for the kingdom of Egypt, a man of great authority: Tedaldo (Theobald) of Piacenza, Archdeacon of Liège, whom fortune would shortly raise higher still. He was greatly surprised when he heard their errand, and deemed the thing of great honour and advantage for all Christendom. *“Ye see that the Pope is dead”*, he told them, *“wherefore ye must needs have patience until a new Pope be made.”* [22]

Seeing that what the Legate said was just, they answered that while a Pope was a-making they might as well go to Venice and visit their households. They set out from Acre for Negropont (Euboea), and from Negropont they continued their voyage to Venice. When Messer Niccolò stepped ashore he found his wife dead and a son of fifteen waiting for him. The boy was named Marco — the boy this Book tells of, born by the received reckoning in 1254. [23]

— Whether Niccolò saw that son as an infant before riding east, or left before the child was born and met him for the first time in 1269, the oldest French texts do not insist. Only Pipino’s later Latin claimed the first sight of a son, and Yule was inclined to reject its pathos. This retelling keeps the early French understatement. [24]

What the Book does insist on is simpler and more human. The house had lost a wife and mother; the father had gained a fine lad of fifteen; and the two brothers abode at Venice a couple of years, tarrying until a Pope should be made. That is the older, quieter homecoming. The unrecognised kinsmen, the Tartar coat, the great banquet and the jewels ripped from the lining belong to a later, more theatrical return — Ramusio’s embroidery for the final homecoming of 1295, which modern criticism has set aside. [25]

The Kaan still waited for learned men and for oil of the Sepulchre; Christendom still waited for a Pope. Between those two waits stood the House of Polo, ledgers thickened by Barca’s gifts and Bocara’s three years, its future leaning toward a second departure — one that would take the boy Marco into the Book’s own centre. But first the cardinals had to make a Pope, and at Viterbo they were proving slow; the brothers, tarrying on the lagoon, would not wait much longer.

## Notes

[1] ✓ The records are Venetian: Marco the Elder’s will (1280) bequeaths his Soldaia house to the Franciscans there [@yc, Prol. 1 n. 2], and Yule’s Introduction assembles the family’s base at Constantinople and its Soldaia business from wills and property notices. The house that made the name famous, in the parish of San Giovanni Grisostomo, is attested only after the return (S15-F9).

[2] ~ The Andrea of San Felice genealogy is Ramusio tradition, not an archival fact: Ramusio said that older records named Andrea as father of the three brothers, and Tanase reports that no surviving record of him has been found [-@tanase2025educazione] (S15-F11).

[3] ~ The division of the brothers’ roles fills in the same tradition where the Book is silent. On Maffeo the documents are ambiguous: Gullino reports that his wife Marta died before him [-@gullino2015matteo], Jacoby reads his last testament as showing no surviving wife or offspring [-@jacoby2006relatives], and Bruno 2023 claims two natural daughters [-@bruno2023quietanza]. The question stays open, and the testament’s year is itself disputed (F-17, S-13).

[4] ~ The year 1260 is Yule’s correction of the manuscripts’ “1250”, discussed in the date aside below; it fits Baldwin’s reign, which ran to his expulsion in 1261, when Michael Palaeologus took the city [@yc, Prol. 1 nn. 1–2].

[5] ~ Prologue tradition [@yc, Prol. 1]: the brothers lay in a store of jewels and crossed the Greater Sea to Soldaia. The port was a real hinge — Greek memory, Mongol pressure, Venetian factory by mid-century [@yc, Prol. 1 n. 2].

[6] ✗ / ~ Manuscript “1250” for the crossing cannot stand beside the 1269 return and Marco aged fifteen. Yule’s corrected date of about 1260 is the working chronology; Stephen G. Haw [-@haw2006marco, ch. 3] dates the first journey by outside reigns instead: at Soldaia before 1260, held at Bukhara under the Chaghadaid Barak (r. 1266–71) until 1266 or early 1267, and at Khubilai’s court about 1267.

[7] ~ Barca (Berke), third son of Juji and the first Muslim sovereign of his line, is historical, with his courts at Sarai and Bolghar attested. Yule dates the brothers’ arrival at Sarai to 1261, a year before the war closed the road west [@yc, Prol. 2 nn. 1–3].

[8] ~ The doubled-value exchange of jewels is book tradition, the royal largesse the Prologue likes to report [@yc, Prol. 2]. The twelvemonth stay is its round companion.

[9] ✓ / ✗ The war is solidly dated — it began in the summer of 1262 and ended about eight months later, as Rashid al-Din and Polo’s own later war chapters both witness [@yc, Prol. 2 n. 3] — but the winner the Book gives is backwards. Alaü’s army under Abaga was routed when Barca rallied, and driven back across the frozen Terek, so that Yule calls Polo’s account “anything but an accurate” one [@yc, 4.25 n. 3]. (F-01)

[10] ~ Ucaca (Ukek, near modern Saratov) marked the Ponent’s frontier toward Russia, as Abulfeda’s “extremity of the empire of the Barka Tartars” indicates and Ibn Batuta’s one-day distance from the Russian hills confirms [@yc, Prol. 2 n. 4].

[11] ~ The “Tigris” is the Volga under a classical name, as Pauthier showed and as the monk Pascal of Vittoria also calls it in 1338 [@yc, Prol. 2 n. 5]. The seventeen days fits one stretch of desert, not the whole crossing.

[12] ~ / ✗ “Bocara the best city in all Persia” is merchant geography rather than a map: Hayton likewise folds it into a Persian horizon, and Vámbéry notes the Persian cultural sphere of those cities before Chinghiz’s conquest; read as modern political geography it fails [@yc, Prol. 3 n. 1]. Barac is Borrak Khan, great-grandson of Sigatay (Chagatai) and sovereign of the Chaghadaid ulus from 1264 to 1270.

[13] ~ Three years’ sojourn at Bocara sits on the Prologue’s fondness for round durations — one, three, three — as much as on any itinerary record. Borrak’s reign dates only partly overlap the stretch the brothers say they spent there.

[14] ~ The meeting with the Great Kaan’s envoys at Bocara and the year-long ride north and north-east are the Prologue’s own narrative, plausible in outline and rounded in its duration [@yc, Prol. 3–4]. The marvels of that road the text deliberately defers — Marco promises them in the Book that follows.

[15] ~ The envoys’ identity is not recoverable from the Book. Yule suggested they may have been the Great Kaan’s own men returning from Hulagu’s court, and that Sartak’s return party of 1265 could have been theirs — a suggestive identification, not proof [@yc, Prol. 3 n. 2].

[16] ~ The audience is Prologue theatre in outline and plausible in substance: the questions about emperors, kings, the Pope and Latin customs track Cublay’s known curiosity, and years on the steppe make the brothers’ fluency credible even though the idiom is never named [@yc, Prol. 5–6].

[17] ? The embassy letter survives in no Mongol original. The request for a hundred learned Christians, masters of the Seven Arts, and for oil of the Sepulchre comports with Chinghizide interest in skilled foreigners and with Latin Holy Land piety; the exact wording and the baptism pledge remain contested [@yc, Prol. 7 nn. 1–3].

[18] ? / ✗ The promise that Cublay and his people would embrace the Law of Christ if the disputants prevailed is conversion rhetoric rather than a recorded policy pledge. Yule doubted purely religious motives [@yc, Prol. 7 n. 3], while Allsen sets the request for learned foreigners in a court culture that collected expertise across Eurasia [-@allsen2001culture, ch. 1]; Arghun’s and Öljeitü’s later letters to Philip the Fair show the diplomatic form without proving these clauses.

[19] ✓ The golden tablet (*paiza*) was a real instrument of imperial logistics, attested in Yuan archaeology and across Persian administrative writing [@yc, Prol. 8]. The Book’s report of its order — supplies, horses, escorts for the three ambassadors — matches the genre.

[20] ~ Cogatal’s illness and abandonment is an uncorroborated personal detail, harmless as book tradition [@yc, Prol. 8].

[21] ✓ / ~ The return date is firm — Acre, April 1269, the See vacant after Clement IV — while the even three years is a Prologue rounding [@yc, Prol. 8–9]. The dead Pope’s name is blank in the oldest French texts; the Crusca and Ramusio supply Clement IV [@yc, Prol. 9 n. 1].

[22] ~ The consultation with Tedaldo Visconti (later Gregory X) at Acre is widely accepted; his precise legatine title varies by manuscript, “legate for the whole kingdom of Egypt” beside “personage of great authority” [@yc, Prol. 9 nn. 1–2].

[23] ✓ / ~ The homecoming beat — wife dead, a son named Marco aged fifteen — is core early tradition, consistent with the return of 1269 [@yc, Prol. 9]. The received 1254 birthdate is itself inferred from that fifteen, not independently documented (S-15/T1).

[24] ✗ / ? Pipino-only: the claim that Niccolò left Venice before the child was born and met him for the first time in 1269 rests on Pipino’s Latin and its dependents; the oldest French texts report only that the mother was dead and the son was fifteen. Yule was inclined to reject the melodramatic first sight of a son [@yc, Prol. 9 n. 3].

[25] ✗ Ramusio-only. The unrecognised kinsmen, the Tartar coat, the banquet of crimson robes and the jewels cut from the lining belong to Ramusio’s account of the final return of c. 1295, filtered through Gasparo Malipiero’s neighbourhood legend; they are not facts of 1269 and are not used as such here. Modern criticism treats them as later embroidery [@yc, Prol. 9].
