# Chapter I-5
## Mausul, Baudas, and the Mountain That Moved

> *“Now I will tell you of a great miracle that befell at Baudas, wrought by God on behalf of the Christians.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 6 (Yule–Cordier)


---

See [the map of the outbound road, I-3](ch-03-second-setting-forth.md#map-of-the-outbound-road).

Beyond the south-eastern frontier of Greater Hermenia (Greater Armenia), the Book finds cloth worth carrying a very long way. It calls the country Mausul (Mosul) and uses much the same name for both the traders and the fabrics they take to market [1]. What goes on the packhorse? First the goods you can sell in the next town, then the goods worth taking several towns farther. The Mosolins in Polo’s account deal in pearls and spices as well as silk and cloth of gold. One name, which a buyer might have heard in a shop a long way west of here, gives the Book a way into the country.

Turn the bale over a moment. There is a question of what the stuff is made of. Polo speaks of silk and gold. Pelliot wondered whether those words had strayed up from the description of what the merchants carry a line later [2]. A buyer would certainly want a closer look. There is another cloth to reckon with here too. The Venetian V version names Mus and Merdin, where people turn abundant cotton into buckram and other fabric, and puts the trading towns under Mongol rule [3]. Around Mausul, the Mongols had already put an end to the old government. The city surrendered in July or August 1262, and its ruler al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl was taken and later killed [4].

The Book’s next business is to find who lives here. Alongside the Muslim Arabi it names Nestorian and Jacobite Christians. It gives their Jatolic, or Catholicos, the business of sending bishops and other clergy to distant places, as the Pope does in the West [5].

Consider what a merchant looking for a congregation might make of that. India sounds quite a way to send a bishop until you have sent a cargo there and are wondering who might look in on your nephew. The Book sizes up these churches by their conformity to Rome. Its own account, in the distance it allows a churchman to travel, gives them a reach a Roman reader might never have imagined.

In the high country Polo sets the Kurds. He knows there are Muslims and Christians among them and tells his reader to watch out for robbers [6]. The traveller with the heavy cargo and the man watching it from above the road have rather different ideas of what makes a good afternoon. The Book is looking up from among the bales. When it follows the river south, there is a great deal of business to be done without climbing a hill at all.

A merchant in Baudas (Baghdad) can send his goods down the Tigris. Below the city comes Bastra (Basra), where Polo draws attention to the date groves [7]. In the Book it takes about eighteen days from Baudas to get to Kisi (Kish) and enter the Indian Sea. A skipper would have to reckon with a considerable reach of the Gulf between the river mouth and that island. The Book has given us the names a trader wants for a first enquiry and left out a stretch of water a skipper would want to know about [8].

Give the man who is just setting out a choice between the dates and the cloth. The dates come with a notable name and a nearer market. For the cloth he has to hope he finds a buyer with money in his purse. And what a buyer from far away can go home and ask for depends on whether he has remembered what the dealer called what he showed him.

There are more things to load upstream. Baudas supplies silk and gold cloth under names a buyer might remember: *nasich*, *nac*, and cramoisy. There are fabrics patterned with birds and beasts [9]. The name of the city travelled too. A cloth of the kind that took its name from Baudas could go over the important person at a ceremony. In time the canopy itself took the name baldachin [10]. A man craning to see a procession in Italy might have been standing under the far end of the same trade as the man counting packages into a boat on the Tigris.

---

To explain what happened to the Calif, the Book takes us into his treasury. Baudas was the seat of the Abbasid line, and in 1258 Alaü (Hulagu) took it. The Book dates the capture to 1255 [11]. It gives the city more than a hundred thousand horsemen and infantry on top of that. How could such a place fall? It flings open the door of a tower full of treasure [12].

Have a look at the two men the story sets before it. One is the ruler who kept the gold. The other is the ruler who brought the army. Alaü asks what use a pile like this is if you won't spend it on the men who could defend the city. The Calif has no answer. He can eat his gold, then, says Alaü. He shuts the treasury on him without food or water. Four days pass before the Book opens the door again, and the Calif is dead [13].

Different accounts leave him in different circumstances. Some give him a confrontation over his wealth and send him out again to be executed. Others make the gold the only thing there is to eat until he dies. The question of which story circulated where is easier to get purchase on than the question of what killed him. Here is what the relevant passages let us say about the end of the man later readers know as al-Mustaʿṣim [14].

| Account | What happens to the Calif | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Polo | Shut in with his riches and deprived of food and drink until he dies. | ~ Yule’s Book I, ch. 6 |
| Hayton | Confronted over his riches and dies in confinement without food or drink. | ~ Translated passage quoted by Neggaz |
| Rashīd al-Dīn | An episode of starvation and a subsequent execution at Waqf. | ~ Neggaz’s discussion and Yule’s account of the execution |
| Ibn al-Furāt | A taunt involving dishes of gold, followed by execution in a carpet. | ~ Le Strange’s account as quoted by Cordier |
| Persian account attributed to Ṭūsī | Treated to a rebuke and a golden tray. His death is reported with its manner unspecified. | ? Attribution debated, translation quoted by Neggaz |

Read down that list and listen for the part that would survive being told to someone across a dinner table. The Calif who has made his money and can do nothing with it is easy to remember. It matters to him whether the door opens again. To a listener who is already thinking of the fine speech about the treasure, the ending after the door shuts may feel as though it has been decided.

Some of the real spoils went east and some were secured by Alaü, according to the sources Yule sets beside the tale [15]. The Book is more concerned with who holds power once the Calif is dead. It brings the office to an end there and then. It did end at Baudas. Another Abbasid line held the title in Cairo [16]. By the time the younger Polo might have come this way, neither the defence of the city nor the opening of the treasury could have met his eyes.

---

Now set a hill in front of Baudas and take the listener up it far enough to see the city. Here lives the Calif in the next tale. He is bent on the conversion or death of the Christians under his rule. In one manuscript tradition the date is 1275, after the fall of the city, and in Ramusio it is 1225 [17]. What the man on the throne has got hold of is the Gospel’s promise about faith the size of a mustard seed. He summons the Christians and asks if they believe it. When they say they do, he points to the hill and gives them ten days to move it, change their religion or die [18].

The congregations have eight days and nights of prayer before the story gives them anywhere to turn. An angel appears to a bishop and tells him to look for a cobbler with one eye [19]. Imagine that bishop coming into the workshop. The man he has to persuade to take charge of the whole affair has been sitting there making shoes, dividing his earnings between his needs and his devotions. The Book tells the story of one of his customers. He had taken the measure of her foot and found himself thinking about more than the shoe. When she left, he put out his own eye with the awl he used to stitch his leather [20]. For the Book, that is how thoroughly he takes the Gospel at its word.

The cobbler says the bishop has too high an opinion of him. The Christians go on asking until he says he will do what they want [21]. On the appointed morning the people go to Mass and come out with a cross at the head of the procession. Polo makes them more than a hundred thousand, with singing and weeping as they cross the plain. Someone in that crowd who cannot see past the cross will know the Calif has brought armed men only when the procession comes to a halt [22].

The bishop gives his blessing. The cobbler goes down on his knees before the cross and stretches out his hands. In the Book’s telling one can see the hill lift and move all the way to the place the Calif pointed out [23]. The consequence for the man who made the challenge comes to light much later. He is said to have been baptised in secret. Only when he dies do the people preparing him for burial find the little cross at his neck, and lay him apart from the other califs [24].

Ramusio adds the yearly celebration among the Nestorian and Jacobite Christians, with a fast the night before [25]. In that version you can go straight from the procession in the story to a congregation keeping the day. Anyone arriving at the feast for the first time will want to know what it commemorates. Start from the fast and the story can be told the other way round, as an account of how all the people who come to church acquired one more day to remember.

— Move the listener a little way round the hill. Some Latin copies put the miracle near Tauris (Tabriz), and Yule heard of a comparable tale on the other side of the desert, in Egypt. A hill and a man who can pray are wonderfully portable. They give us a fair way of asking where Polo heard his story, though we still lack his answer [26].

The Book leaves the story with an accusation against Muslims everywhere. The accusation belongs to its telling and shows how readily it turns a victory in a tale into a judgement on the people it has been describing [27]. The two stories also ask different things of a traveller. A merchant who was never there during the siege could have seen what it left behind or asked someone how they got their shop back open. A traveller who went to see the hill would still need someone to point to the ground it had stood on before.

Whether the Polos went to Mausul and Baudas themselves remains open [28]. The goods are things a man could see in another market. If you want to catch up with them now, try the merchants arriving in Tauris, where the cloth has birds of gold and there are stones to be bought by the handful.

## Notes

[1] ✓ Polo derives the merchants and the cloth from Mausul. Pelliot finds forms of the name of the people of the city in papal lists of 1244, 1258 and 1291 [@yc, 1.5 n. 3; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Mosul”].

[2] ~ Yule connects the word for the cloth to muslin. Pelliot suspects the fibre and metal in the description of the cloth have been copied from the next line, on the goods the merchants sell [@yc, 1.5 n. 3; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Mosul”].

[3] ✓ Shared by the V witness and Ramusio. Yule brackets the passage, and V also names Mus and Merdin and describes their cotton, buckram and trade under the Mongol king [@yc, 1.5 n. 5; @simion2019devisement, 13.9].

[4] ✓ The surrender belongs to July–August 1262, a year later than Yule gives. Yürekli reports the capture and subsequent death of al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl, whose precise date and manner of death remain open [@yurekli2013musul; @jackson2017mongols; @yc, 1.5 n. 1].

[5] ~ The name Jatolic stands for Catholicos. Yule describes the reach of the Church of the East across Asia, while Polo compresses the distinctions among Eastern churches and explains them to a Latin Christian audience [@yc, 1.5 n. 2].

[6] ~ Yule compares Polo with Ricold of Montecroce on the Kurds and on the dangers to travellers. The description of a whole people as robbers is Polo’s and Ricold’s generalisation [@yc, 1.5 n. 4].

[7] ✓ Bastra stood near the head of the Gulf and was known for its dates. Yule compares medieval notices of its exports and groves [@yc, 1.6 n. 3].

[8] ✗ Polo’s reckoning leaves the impression that the river leads straight to Kisi. Yule points out how much of the Gulf a ship would have to traverse to reach the island [@yc, 1.6 n. 2].

[9] ✓ Yule compares the textile names with the forms listed by Pegolotti and other writers. He also cites evidence on the long trade in gold cloth patterned with animals [@yc, 1.6 n. 4].

[10] ✓ Yule traces “baldachin” through a name for the cloth of Baudas. The name subsequently passed to the canopy the cloth served to make [@yc, 1.6 n. 4].

[11] ✓ The sack of the Abbasid capital took place in February 1258. Yule’s translation keeps the Book’s 1255 and Cordier supplies the historical date [@yc, 1.6 nn. 1, 5].

[12] ~ The Book gives the garrison more than a hundred thousand horsemen and has Alaü discover exceptional riches in a tower. The number and single store of fabulous wealth belong to Polo’s siege story [@yc, 1.6].

[13] ~ Polo makes the Calif’s inability to feed himself with his treasure the answer to his failure to spend it on the defence. Yule records related tellings in Hayton and other writers [@yc, 1.6 n. 7].

[14] ? Neggaz quotes Hayton and the account attributed to Ṭūsī, discusses Rashīd al-Dīn’s starvation episode and execution, and reads the recurrence of the treasure taunt across versions. Cordier quotes Le Strange’s report of Ibn al-Furāt, whose account is later, and gives the execution at Waqf in Yule’s account of Rashīd. Confinement and execution can occur in the same narrative [@neggaz2020deaths; @yc, 1.6 n. 7].

[15] ✓ Yule’s note records one portion of the plunder sent away and another placed in safekeeping towards Lake Urmia. A division of the spoils is a separate claim from the treasure tower tale [@yc, 1.6 n. 6].

[16] ✗ The Book ends the caliphate everywhere with the taking of Baudas. Yule notes a surviving Abbasid caliphate under Mamluk rule in Cairo [@yc, 1.6 n. 8].

[17] ? The date 1275 is in the Geographic Text and Pauthier, and 1225 is Ramusio’s reading. Cordier lists two caliphs for 1225, while the city’s fall in 1258 rules out 1275 for a reigning Abbasid in Baudas [@yc, 1.7 n. 1].

[18] ~ The mustard seed is the Gospel image the Calif takes up for his ultimatum. The ten days and the three choices are given in the miracle story [@yc, 1.7].

[19] ~ The community spends eight days and nights praying in Polo’s telling. The answer is delivered in the bishop’s vision, which recurs before he tells the Christians about it [@yc, 1.8–9].

[20] ~ The cobbler’s gifts, fasting and Mass shape his reputation in the story. He acts on a saying about the offending eye in Matthew 5:29 when he uses the awl [@yc, 1.8].

[21] ~ The persuasion of the cobbler is a separate stage of the legend. His doubts about his own goodness come before his agreement to pray [@yc, 1.9].

[22] ~ The early Mass, high count, cross and armed men make up the Book’s account of the procession. The stated number belongs to the tale [@yc, 1.10].

[23] ~ In the legend the cobbler is blessed by the bishop and prays before the cross. The hill moves with both communities there to see it [@yc, 1.10].

[24] ~ Polo has the Calif converted in secret. The cross becomes known at his death and explains the separate burial within the logic of the story [@yc, 1.10].

[25] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule quotes the description of the continued observance with the fast on the eve of the feast [@yc, 1.10 n. 1].

[26] ? Yule traces the relocation to Latin copies and to Gravina between Tauris and Nakhchivan. He compares Gregory Thaumaturgus, reports of travelling mountains near Mecca, and a tale heard about the Egyptian Muqaṭṭam. Neggaz also makes the comparison with the Egyptian story. The route by which any of these tales reached the Book remains open [@yc, 1.10 n. 1; @neggaz2020deaths].

[27] ✗ The Book uses the Christian victory to support a general claim that Islam commands hostility to Christians. That is the polemic in this telling of the miracle [@yc, 1.10].

[28] ? The account shows knowledge of city names, merchandise and stories on the caravan routes. Neither it nor the comparisons here establishes a personal visit, and the date of the conquest rules out the younger Polo seeing it [@yc, 1.5–10].
