# Chapter I-5
## Mausul, Baudas, and the Mountain That Moved

> *“Now I will tell you of a great miracle that befell at Baudas, wrought by God on behalf of the Christians.”*
> — Book I, Of Baudas (Yule–Cordier)

---

Marco Polo starts by narrating that south-eastward of Greater Hermenia the Book turns toward the Mesopotamian plain and names a kingdom that, on Armenia’s frontier, opens the road to the heart of Islam. That kingdom is Mausul — Mosul (al-Mawṣil) — and it is, the text declares without hesitation, a very great kingdom, inhabited by several kinds of people whom it now undertakes to describe. [1]

First there are the Arabi, worshippers of Mahommet. Then there are Christians of two great Oriental communions, Nestorians and Jacobites. These have a Patriarch whom they call the Jatolic — *Játhalík*, the Catholicos — and this Patriarch creates archbishops and abbots and prelates of every degree, and sends them into every quarter: to India, to Baudas, or even to Cathay, just as the Pope of Rome does in the Latin countries. For you must know, the Book insists, that though Christians are very numerous in those eastern lands, they are almost all Jacobites and Nestorians — Christians indeed, yet not in the fashion enjoined by Rome, for they come short in several points of the Faith. The observation is a Latin’s; the geography beneath it is sound. From the districts of Mosul, Tekrit, and the Jazīrah the Jacobite church kept its strength; the Nestorian patriarchate, in the century of the Polos, still reached with bishops and metropolitans from Jerusalem toward Peking. What the Venetian merchant saw — or was told — was not a handful of schismatics on a frontier, but a parallel Christendom whose hierarchy travelled with the caravan. [2]

All the cloths of gold and silk that are called *Mosolins* are made in this country; and those great merchants called *Mosolins*, who carry for sale such quantities of spicery and pearls and cloths of silk and gold, are also from this kingdom. The word has shifted its meaning since the Book was dictated: later ages would apply *muslin* to a fine cotton; here it names rich silk-and-gold stuffs, and perhaps also a class of traders whose name clung to the city like a brand-mark. Near this province, some texts add, lies another called Mus and Merdin — Mush and Mardin — producing immense cotton from which they weave buckram and other cloth; the people are craftsmen and traders, and all subject to the Tartar King. Thus the Book’s eye, even when it moralises, never quite leaves the factor’s ledger. [3]

There is yet another race who inhabit the mountains in that quarter, and are called Curds — Kurds. Some of them are Christians and some Saracens; but they are an evil generation, whose delight it is to plunder merchants. Ricold of Montecroce would write of Kurdish ferocity in the same temper; Mas‘udi had already reckoned certain Jacobite highlanders among the Kurds. The Book’s ethnographic chill sits, as it did with the Turcomans of Asia Minor, beside accurate notice of a people who controlled the high roads and taxed the caravan with steel when they chose. So Mausul is drawn: Arabi of the plain, Nestorians and Jacobites under their Jatolic, Mosolin weavers and merchants, Kurdish highlanders, and cotton towns under Tartar suzerainty — a frontier kingdom already, in historical fact, dissolved as an Atabeg state after Hulagu’s campaigns, yet kept alive in the Book’s geography as a named gate between Armenia and Baudas. [4]

Baudas — Baghdad (Baghdād) — is a great city which used to be the seat of the Calif of all the Saracens in the world, just as Rome is the seat of the Pope of all the Christians. A very great river flows through it, and by this you can descend to the Sea of India. Merchants with their goods travel this way; they descend some eighteen days from Baudas and come to a certain city called Kisi — Kish (Kais / Qays) — where they enter the Indian Sea. On the river, as you go from Baudas toward Kisi, stands a great city called Bastra — Basra (al-Baṣra) — surrounded by woods in which grow the best dates in the world. [5]

— The hydrography is abbreviated: three-fourths of the Persian Gulf intervene between the Tigris mouth and Kish; yet the commercial spine is true enough for a merchant’s sketch — Baghdad to Basra’s date-groves, then the Gulf ports that fed India’s horse-trade and spice return. Polo never calls Kish an island, which may mean he never saw it. [6] Anyway, next he mentions what they weave in Baudas.

In Baudas they weave many kinds of silk stuffs and gold brocades: *nasich* and *nac*, and cramoisy, and many another beautiful tissue richly wrought with figures of beasts and birds. From Baudas, or Baldac, certain of these rich silk-and-gold brocades took the name *baldachini* — baudekins — whence, by a later Italian turn, the canopy itself came to be called a baldachin. The Book’s textile vocabulary is among its firmest technical notices: animal-patterned gold brocade was a Mesopotamian and Persian speciality long before and long after the Mongols; Pegolotti and the Yuan dress ordinances still echo *nakh* and *nasīj*. It is the noblest and greatest city in all those regions — or was, until the day the Tartars came. [7]

Now it came to pass, says the Book, in the year of Christ 1255 — the date is a year or three early — that the Lord of the Tartars of the Levant, whose name was Alaü — Alau (Hulagu / Hülegü) — brother to the Great Kaan then reigning, gathered a mighty host and came up against Baudas and took it by storm. It was a great enterprise: for in Baudas there were more than a hundred thousand horse, besides foot. And when Alaü had taken the place he found therein a tower of the Califs full of gold and silver and other treasure — the greatest accumulation in one spot that ever was known. When he beheld that heap he was astonished, and summoning the Calif — al-Musta‘ṣim Billāh, last of the Abbasids in Baghdad — he said: “Calif, tell me now why thou hast gathered such a huge treasure? What didst thou mean to do therewith? Knewest thou not that I was thine enemy, and that I was coming against thee with so great a host to cast thee forth of thine heritage? Wherefore didst thou not take of thy gear and employ it in paying knights and soldiers to defend thee and thy city?” [8]

The Calif wist not what to answer, and said never a word. So the Prince continued: “Now then, Calif, since I see what a love thou hast borne thy treasure, I will e’en give it thee to eat!” He shut the Calif up in the Treasure Tower, and bade that neither meat nor drink should be given him, saying, “Now, Calif, eat of thy treasure as much as thou wilt, since thou art so fond of it; for never shalt thou have aught else to eat!” So the Calif lingered in the tower four days, and then died like a dog. Truly his treasure would have been of more service to him had he bestowed it upon men who would have defended his kingdom and his people. Howbeit, since that time, there has been never another Calif, either at Baudas or anywhere else — a Latin overstatement, for shadow-caliphates would linger in Cairo, yet true enough of Baghdad’s Abbasid throne, which ended in February of 1258. [9]

— The tale is told as exemplum: avarice punished, treasure that would not buy steel, a prince who fed a hoarder on his own gold. Rashid al-Din and the Arabic chroniclers tell another death — carpet and horses’ hooves, or hunger and a dish of gold set mockingly before the prisoner — but the moral is the same coin minted for Christian and Muslim hearers alike. Polo moralises; he does not invent the taunt out of nothing. “Brother to the Great Kaan now reigning” fits Hulagu–Möngke/Kublai kinship as Franks understood Levantine Tartar lordship. Treasure was real — part sent to Möngke; part stored, per Chinese and Persian notices, toward Urmia. [10] Anyway, the Book has done with history. “Now,” it says, “I will tell you of a great miracle that befell at Baudas, wrought by God on behalf of the Christians.”

I will tell you then, the dictation continues, this great marvel that occurred between Baudas and Mausul. It was in the year of Christ — here the manuscripts disagree: some write 1275, which cannot have been intended for a living Abbasid challenge; Ramusio has 1225 — that there was a Calif at Baudas who bore a great hatred to Christians, and was taken up day and night with the thought how he might either bring those in his kingdom over to his own faith, or procure them all to be slain. He took counsel daily with the devotees and priests of his law. And indeed, the Book adds with the blunt prejudice of a Genoa prison and a Levant road, it is a fact that the whole body of Saracens throughout the world are always most malignantly disposed towards the whole body of Christians. [11]

Now it happened that the Calif, with those shrewd priests of his, got hold of that passage in the Gospel which says that if a Christian had faith as a grain of mustard seed, and should bid a mountain be removed, it would be removed. And such indeed is the truth — so the narrator affirms, without irony. When they had got hold of this text they were delighted, for it seemed the very thing whereby either to force all the Christians to change their faith or to bring destruction upon them all. The Calif therefore called together all the Christians in his territories, who were extremely numerous. He showed them the Gospel and made them read the text. When they had read it he asked if that was the truth. They answered that it assuredly was so. “Well,” said the Calif, “since you say that it is the truth, I will give you a choice. Among such a number of you there must surely be this small amount of faith; so you must either move that mountain there” — and he pointed to a mountain in the neighbourhood — “or you shall die an ill death; unless you choose to eschew death by all becoming Saracens and adopting our Holy Law. To this end I give you a respite of ten days; if the thing be not done by that time, ye shall die or become Saracens.” And when he had said this he dismissed them. [12]

The Christians on hearing what the Calif had said were in great dismay, but they lifted all their hopes to God, their Creator. All the wisest among them took counsel — bishops and priests among them — and found no resource except to turn to Him from whom all good things do come. So they were gathered together in prayer, men and women, for eight days and eight nights. And whilst they were thus engaged it was revealed in a vision by a Holy Angel of Heaven to a certain Bishop who was a very good Christian, that he should desire a certain Christian Cobler, who had but one eye, to pray to God; and that God in His goodness would grant such prayer because of the Cobler’s holy life.

Now I must tell you what manner of man this Cobler was. He led a life of great uprightness and chastity; he fasted and kept from all sin; he went daily to church to hear Mass, and gave daily a portion of his gains to God. And the way he came to have but one eye was this. It happened one day that a certain woman came to him to have a pair of shoes made, and she showed him her foot that he might take her measure. She had a very beautiful foot and leg; and the Cobler, in taking her measure, was conscious of sinful thoughts. He had often heard it said in the Holy Evangel that if thine eye offend thee, pluck it out and cast it from thee, rather than sin. So, as soon as the woman had departed, he took the awl that he used in stitching, and drove it into his eye and destroyed it. And this is the way he came to lose his eye. So you can judge what a holy, just, and righteous man he was. [13]

When this vision had visited the Bishop several times, he related the whole matter to the Christians, and they agreed with one consent to call the Cobler before them. They told him it was their wish that he should pray, and that God had promised to accomplish the matter by his means. On hearing their request he made many excuses, declaring that he was not at all so good a man as they represented. But they persisted with so much sweetness that at last he said he would not tarry, but do what they desired.

And when the appointed day was come, all the Christians got up early — men and women, small and great, more than a hundred thousand persons — and went to church and heard the Holy Mass. After Mass they went forth together in a great procession to the plain in front of the mountain, carrying the precious cross before them, loudly singing and greatly weeping as they went. There they found the Calif with all his Saracen host armed to slay them if they would not change their faith; for the Saracens believed not in the least that God would grant such favour to the Christians. These latter stood indeed in great fear and doubt, but nevertheless they rested their hope on their God Jesus Christ.

So the Cobler received the Bishop’s benison, and then threw himself on his knees before the Holy Cross, and stretched out his hands towards Heaven, and made this prayer: “Blessed Lord God Almighty, I pray Thee by Thy goodness that Thou wilt grant this grace unto Thy people, insomuch that they perish not, nor Thy faith be cast down, nor abused nor flouted. Not that I am in the least worthy to prefer such request unto Thee; but for Thy great power and mercy I beseech Thee to hear this prayer from me Thy servant full of sin.”

And when he had ended this his prayer to God the Sovereign Father and Giver of all grace, and whilst the Calif and all the Saracens and other people there were looking on, the mountain rose out of its place and moved to the spot which the Calif had pointed out. And when the Calif and all his Saracens beheld, they stood amazed at the wonderful miracle that God had wrought for the Christians, insomuch that a great number of the Saracens became Christians. And even the Calif caused himself to be baptised in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost, Amen, and became a Christian, but in secret. Howbeit, when he died they found a little cross hung round his neck; and therefore the Saracens would not bury him with the other Califs, but put him in a place apart. The Christians exulted greatly at this most holy miracle, and returned to their homes full of joy, giving thanks to their Creator for that which He had done. [14]

And now you have heard in what wise took place this great miracle. And marvel not, the Book concludes in its hardest Levantine register, that the Saracens hate the Christians; for the law that Mahommet gave them commands them to do all the mischief in their power to all other descriptions of people, and especially to Christians. See then what an evil law and what naughty commandments they have! But in such fashion the Saracens act, throughout the world. Ramusio’s text softens the close with a liturgical afterglow: from that time forward all the Nestorian and Jacobite Christians maintained a solemn celebration of the day, keeping a fast also on the eve thereof. [15]

— The mountain-miracle is a Christian legend cycle the Book insists is true. Dates in the manuscripts are impossible or arbitrary. Chronological mismatch with Polo’s route is decisive for *visit*: the Polos’ second outbound (c. 1271+) cannot witness an Abbasid-era ordeal; the sack of 1258 already ends the Caliphate the legend needs. Parallels run wide — Gregory Thaumaturgus; Muslim “mountains from Mecca”; Egyptian cobbler–Muqaṭṭam analogues; some Latin copies and a sixteenth-century missionary place the scene toward Tauris or Nakhchivan. A retelling should voice the wonder without sneering and without endorsing. The closing anti-Saracen tirade is Book and Rustichello Levantine commonplaces, not a fair reading of law or practice. [16]

Now I have told you something of Baudas, the narrator says, stepping back from wonder into itinerary. I could easily have told you first of the affairs and the customs of the people there. But it would be too long a business, looking to the great and strange things that I have got to tell you, as you will find detailed in this Book. So now I will tell you of the noble city of Tauris.

Thus Chapter I-5 holds two registers in one hand: the merchant’s Mausul and Baudas — Jatolic, Mosolins, Kurds, dates of Bastra, silks of *nac* and baldachin, the river road toward Kish — and the romance of gold eaten in a tower and a mountain that answered a cobbler’s prayer. Whether Marco stood in the ruins of the Calif’s city, or compiled the sack from Ilkhanid talk and the miracle from a Christian legend already old on the road between Mosul and Baghdad, the Book does not say. It only insists, in the voice that dictated to Rustichello, that the thing is true — and then, with a factor’s shrug, turns toward Tabriz.

## Notes

[1] ✓ Mausul on Armenia’s SE frontier; mixed Arabi, Nestorians, Jacobites. Standard historical geography. Book I.v. “Very great kingdom” is Polo’s habitual inflation — Atabeg Mosul had effectively ended after Badruddīn Lūlū’s line fell to the Mongols (Malik Ṣāliḥ tortured to death 1261).

[2] ✓ / ~ Jatolic (Catholicos) appointing prelates to India, Baudas, Cathay. ✓ Nestorian/Jacobite hierarchies and Asian reach; ~ “just like the Pope” as analogy. ◇ Oriental Christians “come short” of Roman Faith — Latin confessional framing (Nestorians’ two-Persons stereotype; Jacobites’ Monophysitism), not ethnography.

[3] ✓ / ~ *Mosolins*: rich silk-and-gold cloths made at Mosul. ✓ Mosul textile fame; ~ exact medieval referent of *mosolin* debated (silk vs later cotton). ? / ◇ *Mosolins* as a class of great spice/pearl merchants — sparse parallels (Innocent IV’s 1244 *Mosolini*); unclear. ✓ / ~ Mus & Merdin (Mush, Mardin): cotton, buckram; Tartar subjects.

[4] ✓ / ~ Kurds: mixed Christian/Saracen; plunder merchants. ✓ highland pastoral control and mixed confession notices (Mas‘udi); ~ “evil generation” stereotype (Ricold).

[5] ✓ Baudas = Calif’s seat, analogised to Rome. Abbasid Baghdad until 1258. ✓ / ✗ River route ~18 days toward Kisi; Bastra date woods. ✓ Basra dates and Gulf trade spine; ✗ implication that Tigris runs to Kish (Gulf elided). ✓ Kisi as Indian-Sea entrepôt (Kish/Kais) — major 12th–14th-c. Gulf port; Polo likely unvisited.

[6] ✗ Hydrography compressed: Gulf gap between Tigris mouth and Kish elided. Merchant sketch, not survey.

[7] ✓ *Nasich*, *nac*, cramoisy; animal-figure gold brocades; baldachin etymology. Pegolotti, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, Rubruck, Yuan *nakh/nasīj*; *baldachino* from Baldac. Animal-pattern gold brocade is a long Near Eastern technical tradition (Makrizi’s *ṭard waḥsh*).

[8] ✓ / ~ Alaü (Hulagu) storms Baudas; vast garrison & treasure. ✓ historical sack 5–10 Feb. 1258 and huge booty; ~ “100,000 horse” as epic round number; text date 1255 early. Hulagu brother to the Great Kaan then reigning — Frank understanding of Levantine Tartar lordship.

[9] ◇ / ~ Calif starved in treasure tower / forced to “eat” gold. Widely circulated Christian exemplum (Hayton, Ricold, Pachymeres; Sanuto’s molten-gold variant is Crassus-transfer). Muslim sources share the taunt, differ on killing method: Rashid al-Din — death at Wakf, 14 Ṣafar 656 / 20 Feb. 1258; later writers — carpet and trampling (Mongol taboo on shedding royal blood); Ibn al-Furāt — hunger, dishes of gold, then sacks and trampling. ✗ / ~ “Never another Calif” after Baudas — ✗ Cairo Abbasid shadow-caliphate; ~ true of Baghdad’s line.

[10] ✓ Treasure real (part to Möngke; part stored toward Urmia per Chinese and Persian notices). Polo moralises a taunt already circulating; he does not invent it from nothing.

[11] ◇ Mountain-miracle frame: Calif’s hatred; MS dates 1275 (G.T./Pauthier) or 1225 (Ramusio) — impossible or arbitrary for an Abbasid-era ordeal after 1258. Closing claim that “all Saracens” hate Christians is polemical commonplace.

[12] ◇ Mustard-seed Gospel challenge; ten-day respite; force conversion or death. Legend setup; Book voice affirms the Gospel text as truth without irony.

[13] ◇ One-eyed cobbler: self-blinding after impure thought at a woman’s foot — literalising Matt. 5:29. Hagiographic motif of extreme chastity.

[14] ◇ Procession of 100,000+; mountain moves; mass conversions; Calif secretly baptised; cross found at death; separate burial. Christian legend cycle; chronological mismatch with Polo’s travel decisive against autopsy. Parallels: Gregory Thaumaturgus; Muslim Mecca-mountain tales; Egyptian Muqaṭṭam analogues; some MSS relocate toward Tauris/Nakhchivan (Yule’s “Mountain Miracle” note).

[15] ◇ / ✗ Closing tirade that Islamic law commands universal mischief vs Christians. Polemical commonplace; not a fair reading of law or practice. ~ Ramusio’s commemorative feast — later colour.

[16] ? Whole Mausul–Baudas block personally traversed by Marco. Gazetteer flags both as *reported_only*. Pegolotti-type roads and some missionary itineraries did run Sivas–Mardin–Mosul–Baghdad; whether the Polos personally lingered is open. Textile and date notices smell of mercantile intelligence; the miracle cycle and moralised sack smell of received tale.
