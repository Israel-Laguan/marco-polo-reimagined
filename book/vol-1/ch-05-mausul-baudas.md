# Chapter I-5
## Mausul, Baudas, and the Mountain That Moved

> *“Now I will tell you of a great miracle that befell at Baudas, wrought by God on behalf of the Christians.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 6 (Yule–Cordier)


---

Cross the south-eastern frontier of Greater Hermenia (Greater Armenia) and you meet a cloth whose name travelled farther than most merchants ever did. Polo calls the country Mausul (Mosul) and gives much the same name to its traders — the Mosolins — and to the fine fabrics they send to market [1]. Packhorses carry the local cottons to the next town, while the Mosolins' richer wares — pearls, spices, silk, and cloth of gold — travel much farther afield across the Levant. One word heard in a shop a long way west gives the Book its way into the region, and a Venetian listener knows at once what sort of goods are on the table.

See [the map of the outbound road, I-3](ch-03-second-setting-forth.md#map-of-the-outbound-road).

Turn the bale over a moment. What is the stuff actually made of? Polo speaks of silk and gold, though Pelliot suspected those words slipped up from the line below, where the merchants' other wares are listed [2]. A buyer would want a closer look at the weave before paying a premium. The Venetian V witness and Ramusio add Mus and Merdin, where craftsmen turn abundant cotton into buckram and other cloths under Mongol rule [3]. Around Mausul itself, the old government was already gone: the city surrendered in July or August 1262, and its ruler al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl was captured and later killed [4].

That political shift matters on the road. A merchant arriving with silver or spices does not merely ask what the local looms produce; he asks whose tax-collectors sit at the gate and whether the ruler in the citadel can keep the passes open. Around Mausul, Mongol governors now answered those questions.

Alongside the Muslim Arabi, Polo finds Nestorian and Jacobite Christians living under a Jatolic, or Catholicos, who sends bishops and clergy to distant sees — even to India and Cathay — much as the Pope does in the West [5]. For a Venetian merchant who has sent a cargo down the Gulf and wonders who might look in on his nephew, a church that reaches as far as his ships is worth noting, even if Polo measures those eastern congregations by how closely they match Rome.

Up in the hills live the Kurds — both Muslims and Christians — whom Polo warns travellers to watch as robbers [6]. A merchant trudging beside loaded mules through a narrow defile has one view of the passes; the hillmen watching from the ridge above have another. Follow the river south instead, and the trade opens out.

At Baudas (Baghdad), goods go straight onto the Tigris, where barges and river-craft carry them toward the sea. Downstream lies Bastra (Basra), famed for its palm groves and the finest dates in the world [7], and from Baudas Polo reckons about eighteen days to Kisi (Kish) and the Indian Sea. A skipper would have to reckon with a long stretch of the Gulf between the river-mouth and that island; Polo gives a trader the port names he needs for a first enquiry and skips the open water in between [8].

Even so, a merchant loading a boat on the Tigris has plenty to tempt him: silk and cloth of gold under names buyers remember — *nasich*, *nac*, and cramoisy — woven with figures of birds and beasts [9]. The city's name travelled with its looms. When a fine Baudas cloth was hung over a great personage in ceremony, the canopy itself took the name *baldachin* [10]. Someone craning at a procession in Italy might be standing beneath the far end of the same trade as the factor tallying bales onto a river-barge.

Pause at the riverside for a moment to hear what those names meant to a Venetian buyer. *Nasich* and *nac* were silk brocades shot through with gold thread, stiff enough to catch torchlight in a palace hall; *cramoisy* was silk steeped in crimson dye. Packing them into oiled bales for a river voyage was a gamble against damp and river pirates alike, yet the profit on a single chest could pay for the whole crew's wages.

---

To explain how Baudas fell, Polo takes us straight into the Calif's treasury. Alaü (Hulagu) took the Abbasid capital in 1258 — though the Book dates the capture to 1255 [11] — and Polo credits the city with more than a hundred thousand horsemen plus infantry besides, as if to make its fall all the more astonishing. How could such a host lose? The story flings open the door of a tower crammed to the rafters with gold, silver, and precious stones [12].

Look at the two men standing before it: the ruler who hoarded the metal, and the conqueror who brought the army. Alaü asks what good a mountain of treasure is if you will not spend a penny of it to pay soldiers who might defend your walls. The Calif has no answer. Then he can eat his gold, says Alaü, and shuts the heavy treasury door on him without food or water. Four days later the Book opens the door again, and the Calif lies dead among his heaps of coin [13].

It is pure Rustichello theatre — a moral fable staged inside a locked vault, where every coin mocks the man who refused to spend it. Yet behind the drama lies a real historical question that chroniclers across the Middle East argued over for generations.

Different accounts leave him in different circumstances. Some give him a confrontation over his wealth and send him out again to be executed. Others make the gold the only thing there is to eat until he dies. The question of which story circulated where is easier to get purchase on than the question of what killed him. Here is what the relevant passages let us say about the end of the man later readers know as al-Mustaʿṣim [14].

| Account | What happens to the Calif | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Polo | Shut in with his riches and deprived of food and drink until he dies. | ~ Yule’s Book I, ch. 6 |
| Hayton | Confronted over his riches and dies in confinement without food or drink. | ~ Translated passage quoted by Neggaz |
| Rashīd al-Dīn | An episode of starvation and a subsequent execution at Waqf. | ~ Neggaz’s discussion and Yule’s account of the execution |
| Ibn al-Furāt | A taunt involving dishes of gold, followed by execution in a carpet. | ~ Le Strange’s account as quoted by Cordier |
| Persian account attributed to Ṭūsī | Treated to a rebuke and a golden tray. His death is reported with its manner unspecified. | ? Attribution debated, translation quoted by Neggaz |

Read down that list and listen for the part that survives being told across a dinner table. A Calif who hoarded his gold and could do nothing with it when the Mongols arrived is easy to remember. Once Alaü delivers that fine speech at the treasury door, the ending feels almost decided before the bolts are drawn.

Some of the real spoils went east to the Great Kaan and some were secured by Alaü, according to the sources Yule sets beside the tale [15]. Polo cares less about the treasury inventory than about who holds power once the Calif is dead. In his telling, the caliphate ends there and then. It did end at Baudas, though another Abbasid line kept the title alive in Cairo under Mamluk protection [16]. By the time the younger Marco could have passed this way, neither the siege nor the opening of the tower could have met his eyes. Everything he reports here comes from the stories still circulating along the caravan routes.

---

Now set a hill in front of Baudas and climb it far enough to see the city below. Here lives the Calif of the Book's next tale, bent on the conversion or death of his Christian subjects. One manuscript tradition dates the scene to 1275, seventeen years after the city fell; Ramusio sets it in 1225 [17]. What this Calif has seized upon is the Gospel promise of *“faith as a grain of mustard seed”*. He summons the Christians before him, asks if they believe those words are true, and points to the hill outside the walls. They have ten days to move it, he declares, or they must convert to his law or die [18].

Eight days and nights the congregations pray in their churches before help appears. Men, women, and children fast and weep together while the deadline narrows. Then an angel tells a bishop in a vision to seek out a one-eyed cobbler [19]. Picture the bishop stepping into that modest workshop, where leather scraps and wooden lasts lie beside the bench. The man he must persuade to save the city divides his daily earnings between his own table and the poor, fasts, and hears Mass. Once, while fitting a woman's shoe, he felt a stray desire; after she left, he took his stitching awl and put out his own eye [20]. For the Book, here is a man who takes the Gospel literally.

There is nothing grand about the setting Rustichello gives him: no marble basilica, no gilded altar, only a cobbler's stool, a sharp awl, and a man who gives half his day's copper coins to the poor before locking his shutter. If the Calif thought he was challenging the learned bishops of Baudas, the story sends him to the humblest tradesman in the lane.

The cobbler protests that he is unworthy and that the bishop thinks too highly of him, until the Christians' pleading finally wears him down [21]. On the tenth morning they hear Mass together and march out across the plain behind a lifted cross — more than a hundred thousand strong, Polo says, weeping and singing hymns as they go. Those at the back of the column cannot see the Calif's waiting troops drawn up in battle order until the whole procession halts before the hill [22].

The bishop gives his blessing. The cobbler drops to his knees and stretches his hands toward heaven. At his prayer, the hill lifts and moves to the spot the Calif named, while the startled sovereign and his court look on in terror [23]. The sequel comes out only at the ruler's death: secretly baptised, he is found wearing a little cross at his neck when his body is prepared for burial, and is laid apart from the other califs [24].

Ramusio adds that the Nestorian and Jacobite Christians still keep the day with an annual feast and a vigil fast the night before, not even touching water until the liturgy ends [25]. Walk into that service for the first time and you would ask what it commemorates; start from the fast, and the miracle explains how the congregation gained another day on its calendar.

— Move a little way round the hill. Some Latin copies place the miracle near Tauris (Tabriz), and Yule heard of a comparable tale across the desert in Egypt, at the hill of Muqaṭṭam. A hill and a man who can pray are wonderfully portable; they give us a fair way of asking where Polo heard his story, though we still lack his answer [26].

The Book closes the tale with a sweeping charge against Muslims everywhere, turning a story of Christian deliverance into a polemic against the peoples it has been describing [27]. Yet the two Baudas tales ask very different things of a traveller. A merchant who missed the siege could still see broken walls or ask how shops reopened. A traveller looking for the moved hill still needs someone to point to the empty ground where it once stood.

Whether the Polos ever visited Mausul and Baudas in person remains open [28]. The goods are things a merchant could handle in another market. If you want to catch up with them now, try the dealers arriving in Tauris, where the cloth gleams with birds of gold and gemstones are bought by the handful.

## Notes

[1] ✓ Polo derives the merchants and the cloth from Mausul. Pelliot finds forms of the name of the people of the city in papal lists of 1244, 1258 and 1291 [@yc, 1.5 n. 3; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Mosul”].

[2] ~ Yule connects the word for the cloth to muslin. Pelliot suspects the fibre and metal in the description of the cloth have been copied from the next line, on the goods the merchants sell [@yc, 1.5 n. 3; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Mosul”].

[3] ✓ Shared by the V witness and Ramusio. Yule brackets the passage, and V also names Mus and Merdin and describes their cotton, buckram and trade under the Mongol king [@yc, 1.5 n. 5; @simion2019devisement, 13.9].

[4] ✓ The surrender belongs to July–August 1262, a year later than Yule gives. Yürekli reports the capture and subsequent death of al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl, whose precise date and manner of death remain open [@yurekli2013musul; @jackson2017mongols; @yc, 1.5 n. 1].

[5] ~ The name Jatolic stands for Catholicos. Yule describes the reach of the Church of the East across Asia, while Polo compresses the distinctions among Eastern churches and explains them to a Latin Christian audience [@yc, 1.5 n. 2].

[6] ~ Yule compares Polo with Ricold of Montecroce on the Kurds and on the dangers to travellers. The description of a whole people as robbers is Polo’s and Ricold’s generalisation [@yc, 1.5 n. 4].

[7] ✓ Bastra stood near the head of the Gulf and was known for its dates. Yule compares medieval notices of its exports and groves [@yc, 1.6 n. 3].

[8] ✗ Polo’s reckoning leaves the impression that the river leads straight to Kisi. Yule points out how much of the Gulf a ship would have to traverse to reach the island [@yc, 1.6 n. 2].

[9] ✓ Yule compares the textile names with the forms listed by Pegolotti and other writers. He also cites evidence on the long trade in gold cloth patterned with animals [@yc, 1.6 n. 4].

[10] ✓ Yule traces “baldachin” through a name for the cloth of Baudas. The name subsequently passed to the canopy the cloth served to make [@yc, 1.6 n. 4].

[11] ✓ The sack of the Abbasid capital took place in February 1258. Yule’s translation keeps the Book’s 1255 and Cordier supplies the historical date [@yc, 1.6 nn. 1, 5].

[12] ~ The Book gives the garrison more than a hundred thousand horsemen and has Alaü discover exceptional riches in a tower. The number and single store of fabulous wealth belong to Polo’s siege story [@yc, 1.6].

[13] ~ Polo makes the Calif’s inability to feed himself with his treasure the answer to his failure to spend it on the defence. Yule records related tellings in Hayton and other writers [@yc, 1.6 n. 7].

[14] ? Neggaz quotes Hayton and the account attributed to Ṭūsī, discusses Rashīd al-Dīn’s starvation episode and execution, and reads the recurrence of the treasure taunt across versions. Cordier quotes Le Strange’s report of Ibn al-Furāt, whose account is later, and gives the execution at Waqf in Yule’s account of Rashīd. Confinement and execution can occur in the same narrative [@neggaz2020deaths; @yc, 1.6 n. 7].

[15] ✓ Yule’s note records one portion of the plunder sent away and another placed in safekeeping towards Lake Urmia. A division of the spoils is a separate claim from the treasure tower tale [@yc, 1.6 n. 6].

[16] ✗ The Book ends the caliphate everywhere with the taking of Baudas. Yule notes a surviving Abbasid caliphate under Mamluk rule in Cairo [@yc, 1.6 n. 8].

[17] ? The date 1275 is in the Geographic Text and Pauthier, and 1225 is Ramusio’s reading. Cordier lists two caliphs for 1225, while the city’s fall in 1258 rules out 1275 for a reigning Abbasid in Baudas [@yc, 1.7 n. 1].

[18] ~ The mustard seed is the Gospel image the Calif takes up for his ultimatum. The ten days and the three choices are given in the miracle story [@yc, 1.7].

[19] ~ The community spends eight days and nights praying in Polo’s telling. The answer is delivered in the bishop’s vision, which recurs before he tells the Christians about it [@yc, 1.8–9].

[20] ~ The cobbler’s gifts, fasting and Mass shape his reputation in the story. He acts on a saying about the offending eye in Matthew 5:29 when he uses the awl [@yc, 1.8].

[21] ~ The persuasion of the cobbler is a separate stage of the legend. His doubts about his own goodness come before his agreement to pray [@yc, 1.9].

[22] ~ The early Mass, high count, cross and armed men make up the Book’s account of the procession. The stated number belongs to the tale [@yc, 1.10].

[23] ~ In the legend the cobbler is blessed by the bishop and prays before the cross. The hill moves with both communities there to see it [@yc, 1.10].

[24] ~ Polo has the Calif converted in secret. The cross becomes known at his death and explains the separate burial within the logic of the story [@yc, 1.10].

[25] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule quotes the description of the continued observance with the fast on the eve of the feast [@yc, 1.10 n. 1].

[26] ? Yule traces the relocation to Latin copies and to Gravina between Tauris and Nakhchivan. He compares Gregory Thaumaturgus, reports of travelling mountains near Mecca, and a tale heard about the Egyptian Muqaṭṭam. Neggaz also makes the comparison with the Egyptian story. The route by which any of these tales reached the Book remains open [@yc, 1.10 n. 1; @neggaz2020deaths].

[27] ✗ The Book uses the Christian victory to support a general claim that Islam commands hostility to Christians. That is the polemic in this telling of the miracle [@yc, 1.10].

[28] ? The account shows knowledge of city names, merchandise and stories on the caravan routes. Neither it nor the comparisons here establishes a personal visit, and the date of the conquest rules out the younger Polo seeing it [@yc, 1.5–10].
