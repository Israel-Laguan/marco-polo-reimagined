# Chapter I-11
## Charchan, Lop, Tangut, Camul: Desert Voices and the Edge of Cathay

> *“Even in the day-time one hears those spirits talking. And sometimes you shall hear the sound of a variety of musical instruments, and still more commonly the sound of drums.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 39 (Yule–Cordier)


---

A river near Pein (probably Uzun-tatïr, near Keriya) can give a man a stone worth taking a long way to sell. At Charchan (Cherchen) the rivers still bring down what the Book calls jasper and chalcedony, with good prices for the stones in Cathay (North China). From here the water itself becomes a thing worth carrying, if it is fit to drink [1]. People who know where it is can get away from an army, the Book says. Take your family and the animals two or three days into the sands. The people following you have to find the same water, and the wind can cover your tracks [2].

The march to Lop (Charklik) is five days over sand by Polo’s reckoning, and the water turns sweet when you get there. A man planning to cross the desert will spend a week giving himself and his animals time to recover, and lay in food for a month [3]. What is missing on the road is the chance to buy anything someone forgot. In the Book the crossing is a month at the narrowest point. Its first water can supply fifty or a hundred people with their animals. The traveller takes a day and a night to reach it. He may be travelling with somebody who knows where to look, yet the size of the party makes a difference to how much help he can expect from the discovery. Those people have twenty-eight places with good water and four where it is brackish [4]. To the man who needs the next of those wells it matters how far ahead the others in his party have got.

Picture it at night. A man on the road gets a little way behind the others and then hears someone call him by name. It’s the sound he has wanted to hear. Think how he would go about finding the others if he were ahead, where he might reasonably stop and wait for them to come up. Put him behind, in the dark, and the best thing he can think to do is hurry, following whatever sign reaching his ears will let him choose a direction. By the time he realises there is nobody in front of him, he can have come too far from his companions to get back. In the Book he has heard spirits. The story has voices and music even by day, and says the sound they make most often is drums [5]. In Ramusio the straggler hears a whole party riding by. He follows and finds himself lost at daybreak [6]. The desert is a year’s ride in its full length in the telling. It is no great comfort to this man to know that the others took it at its narrowest point.

— The earlier pilgrim accounts give other people occasion to dread voices and spirits in desert country. It is worth listening for what the travellers need to know in the story before asking how far the sound can travel: the man behind the others needs to tell which direction they’ve gone in [7].

Ramusio gives the party something sensible to do before setting out. Fasten bells to the animals’ necks, stick together and put up a sign at the place you sleep to show the direction to take next time you start [8]. The bell tells somebody who can hear it where to look. A sign tells somebody who can see it where to go. It’s worth attending to both, in a story that starts out by putting a man behind his companions in the dark.

---

After the crossing the Book brings us to Sachiu (Dunhuang) in Tangut. Buddhists, Nestorian Christians and Muslims are here in Polo’s account. He puts the Buddhist inhabitants to work on the land and describes their houses of worship [9]. Near the city, the caves at Mogao show what could be made for Buddhist worship over centuries. The Book leaves us without a visit to a particular cave [10].

A family who wants a blessing for its children has the sheep to bring with it at the feast. It has been fattened for the occasion. The family has it cooked and puts the meat out before the image while prayers are said. After that it can go home and serve the relations [11]. Somebody may have been feeding the sheep for quite a while. The people at the feast have waited for the right day, and the children can see it go from a thing they helped raise to a thing set down where they are accustomed to looking when the family prays. There are practical questions to settle when everyone is to get a share. Ramusio gives the priests the head, feet, entrails, skin and some of the meat [12].

The Book would have all the people it calls idolaters burn their dead. It does know how to describe the preparations at a particular funeral. Cloth is hung on a wooden shelter the procession will pass, and food and wine are set out there. At the burning, figures cut out to look like men, horses, camels and money go into the flames, to furnish the dead with what they have been shown in miniature [13]. People in the procession have a destination in view for both the body they are carrying and the things they put beside it.

Some families have to keep a body for months before the burning in the Book’s account. Ramusio gives the astrologers the birth date and lets them choose the day. He also describes the joints of a coffin being sealed [14]. The Book makes it a thick painted coffin, with spices and camphor inside. Here is another thing a merchant could carry a great way and have a customer for. There is an occasion on which it would be quite easy to find out why that customer wanted it. The family sets meals out each day. If a soothsayer tells them the door is unlucky, there are people in the Book who break a hole in the wall so as to take the coffin out by another way [15]. It is a very particular detail to find in an account that takes a custom it describes here and recommends it as a way of understanding people a long way from this town.

---

Camul (Hami) is an oasis between deserts. It has things to sell a traveller who is hungry and the Book makes it a place of dancing and singing [16]. Its account of hospitality goes farther. Polo claims the host will put his wife at the visitor’s disposal and leave until the man has gone. Mongou (Möngke) prohibits the custom in the story [17]. Pipino and Ramusio describe him ordering public lodgings for travellers [18]. Ramusio gives three lean years afterwards. The people blame the ban and make a costly appeal, until the Kaan lets them go on as before [19]. The tale tells us a great deal about what a Latin reader was expected to think of both husband and wife. Whether Marco stood in the town himself remains open.

Ask about a different thing a man could have heard in these parts. The Book locates a substance called salamander in a mountain in Chingintalas. It puts veins of metal in the same place. The province itself is unfixed [20]. Polo’s acquaintance Zurficar tells him he spent three years obtaining the material for the Kaan. We now call the mineral fibre asbestos. The word that would have reached Polo’s first listeners gives us a clue to why the matter deserved a story. To a man who thinks he knows how a salamander lives, it takes a man with a piece of the thing he can hold to get him to attend to how you would make it into a napkin. The fibres, once separated from the earth, can be spun into a cloth which the Book says comes out of the fire cleaner than it went in [21]. A napkin can give a man in Europe something quite different to imagine from an animal able to live in a fire. The Book says the Kaan even sent one to Rome to wrap a holy relic. That gift is unverified [22].

---

Back on the route east lies Sukchur (Jiuquan), where Polo makes rhubarb a purchase worth travelling to the mountains for [23]. In Zelada and Ramusio, watch which plants a beast takes on the way. The texts give a plant that makes animals lose their hooves and have the cattle of the country keep clear of it. Ramusio makes a rule of it for the traveller: take only the cattle of the country there [24]. Local people can tell someone what to leave alone in a pasture quite as readily as what to take away in a bundle. The green thing the man with a taste for trading wants to see laid out in front of him has a different value from the green thing a person has to know well enough to keep his animal away from it. Polo’s report puts the two on the same mountain road, close enough to make a man stop before he leaves his beasts alone to graze in the hills.

Near this stretch of road is the pass at Jiayuguan. The surviving pass was begun by the Ming in 1372, a century after Polo’s passage. What stood at that spot in the 1270s is uncertain [25]. The Book never names a Great Wall. Wood makes something of the silence even while acknowledging the later date of the familiar Wall. She thinks earlier earthen defences ought to have been visible. De Rachewiltz’s answer puts more weight on the difference between those works and what a modern visitor would expect a traveller to name [26].

In Campichu (Zhangye), put your head inside one of the houses Polo sets aside for worship. There are three Christian churches in his account, and many Buddhist houses [27]. He has figures of wood, clay and stone, polished and covered in gold. Among them are great images lying down, with other figures set around them. A reclining Buddha survives in the city [28]. The scale is the sort of thing a man can find time to describe even if he has been given very little time to look.

The Book notices five days in the month when people should abstain from killing animals and eating meat [29]. Polo also describes people having more than one wife according to what they can afford, with particular standing for the first wife [30]. How long a visitor had to find out the rules would matter here. The Book has Marco and Maffeo stay for an entire year in Campichu, with uncertainty in the versions over who was there and on what business [31].

When it is time to go, turn north with what you can carry towards Etzina (Khara-Khoto), where the next calculation will be how long the provisions must last.
