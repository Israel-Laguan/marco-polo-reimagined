# Chapter I-11
## Charchan, Lop, Tangut, Camul: Desert Voices and the Edge of Cathay

> *“Even in the day-time one hears those spirits talking. And sometimes you shall hear the sound of a variety of musical instruments, and still more commonly the sound of drums.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 39 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Marco Polo starts by narrating that from Pein and the jade rivers of Cotan’s fringe the road turns east again into sand. Charchan—Cherchen (Qiemo)—is a province of Great Turkey lying between north-east and east. The people worship Mahommet. There are numerous towns and villages, and the chief city of the kingdom bears the province’s name. Rivers here likewise bring down Jasper and Chalcedony—jade again, carried for sale into Cathay, where the stone fetches great prices. The whole country is sandy, and so is the road all the way from Pein; much of the water is bitter and bad, though at certain places you do find sweet. When an army passes through the land, he says, the people escape with wives, children, and cattle two or three days into the sandy waste; knowing the spots where water is to be had, they live and keep their beasts alive, whilst discovery is impossible, for the wind immediately blows the sand over their track. [1]

Quitting Charchan, according to the book, you ride some five days through the sands, finding none but bitter water, and then come to a place where the water is sweet. At the end of those five days stands Lop—the City of Lop (near modern Charklik / Ruoqiang)—a large town at the entrance of the Great Desert, belonging to the Great Kaan, its people worshippers of Mahommet. Here travellers repose before the crossing. Such as propose to enter the Desert take a week’s rest to refresh themselves and their cattle, then make ready a month’s supply for man and beast. On quitting the city they enter the waste. [2]

The length of this Desert, Polo narrates, is so great that ’tis said a year and more would be needed to ride from one end of it to the other. Where its breadth is least, it takes a month to cross. It is all hills and valleys of sand, and not a thing to eat is found upon it. Yet after riding a day and a night you find fresh water—enough, mayhap, for fifty or a hundred persons with their beasts, but not for more. Across the Desert you will find water in like manner: in some twenty-eight places good water in no great quantity, and in four places brackish. Beasts there are none, for there is nought for them to eat. The “year and more” is book hyperbole; the month at least for the narrow crossing and the thin chain of wells are the working geography. [3]

But there is a marvellous thing related of this Desert. When travellers move by night, and one of them chances to lag behind or fall asleep, and then tries to regain his company, he will hear spirits talking, and will suppose them to be his comrades. Sometimes the spirits will call him by name; and thus a traveller may be led astray so that he never finds his party, and in this way many have perished. Sometimes the stray will hear as it were the tramp and hum of a great cavalcade away from the true line of road, and taking that sound for his own company will follow it; when day breaks he finds that a cheat has been put upon him and that he is in an ill plight. Even in the day-time one hears those spirits talking. And sometimes you shall hear musical instruments of many kinds, and still more commonly the sound of drums. [4]

— Now, those voices need a brief aside. Chinese pilgrims and later travellers tell the same desert-goblin stories for this waste and for others; Fa-hian and Hiuen Tsang already knew demon speech and bone trails in the flowing sands. The drums and music are a different class of marvel: singing sands are real acoustics near Shachau and in many deserts of the world. So the book’s spirits are folklore universal dressed as Lop geography—and the exact road past Lob-nor has been argued for a century by explorers who found the lakes themselves wandering. [5] Anyway, next he mentions how the Desert is crossed in practice.

Hence it is customary, he says, for travellers to keep close together; all the animals have bells at their necks so that they cannot easily go astray; and at sleeping-time a signal is set up to show the direction of the next march. So thus the Desert is crossed.

After you have travelled thirty days through that wilderness you come to a city called Sachiu—Shachau (Dunhuang)—lying between north-east and east. It belongs to the Great Kaan and stands in a province called Tangut. The people are for the most part Idolaters—Buddhists, in the book’s habitual language—though there are also some Nestorian Christians and some Saracens. The Idolaters have a peculiar language; they are no great traders but live by agriculture. They keep many abbeys and minsters full of idols of sundry fashions, to which they pay great honour, worshipping and sacrificing with much ceremony. Such as have children will feed up a sheep in honour of the idol, and at the New Year or on the idol’s feast-day they take the children and the sheep into the idol’s presence; the sheep is slaughtered and cooked, presented again with reverence, left before the image whilst offices and prayers are recited for a blessing on the children—and, if you will believe them, the idol feeds on the meat set before it. Afterwards the flesh is carried home and eaten in festivity with the kindred; the idol-priests receive head, feet, entrails, skin, and a share of the meat; the bones that remain are stored carefully in a hutch. [6]

And you must know, says the book with Venetian confidence, that all the Idolaters in the world burn their dead. When a body is carried to the burning, the kinsfolk build a wooden house on the way, draped with cloths of silk and gold; when the corpse passes they halt and set before it wine and meat and other eatables, assured that the defunct will be received with like attentions in the other world. All the minstrelsy of the town goes playing before the body; and at the burning-place the kin are prepared with figures cut of parchment and paper in the shape of men and horses and camels, and with round pieces of paper like gold coins, and all these they burn along with the corpse, saying that in the other world the dead will be provided with slaves and cattle and money in proportion to the paper burnt. They never burn until astrologers, told the year and day and hour of the deceased’s birth, have declared under what constellation he was born and named the lucky day for the fire; so that the body is sometimes kept six months, more or less, before it is burnt. The coffin is made a good span in thickness, carefully joined and daintily painted, filled with camphor and spices against corruption, the joints stopped with pitch and lime, and covered with fine cloth. Every day whilst the body is kept they set a table before the dead with food, holding that the soul comes and eats and drinks. And worse still—so the Latin reader is meant to feel—sometimes the soothsayers say it is not good luck to carry the corpse out by the door; then a hole must be broken in the wall, and the body drawn out that way to the burning. These, the book assures you, are the practices of all the Idolaters of those countries. [7]

— That “all Idolaters” is a merchant’s generalisation. Cremation, paper money for the dead, astrologer-timed funerals, and the wall-exit instead of the door are real Chinese and Buddhist customs first met here on the Hexi road; Polo stretches them into a world-rule for every Buddhist he will ever name. The door-superstition itself has parallels from China to Umbria. [8] Anyway, from Tangut’s threshold the itinerary digresses, as it did for Samarcan, toward a province that does not lie on the main eastward spine.

Camul—Hami (Kumul)—is a province which in former days was a kingdom. It contains numerous towns and villages; the chief city bears the name Camul. The province lies between two deserts: on one side the Great Desert of Lop, on the other a small desert of three days’ journey. The people are all Idolaters and have a peculiar language. They live by the fruits of the earth, which they have in plenty and dispose of to travellers. They are a people who take things very easily, minding nothing but playing and singing and dancing and enjoying themselves. [9]

And it is the truth—so the book insists where Latin ears will redden—that if a foreigner comes to lodge in one of these houses, the host is delighted and desires his wife to put herself entirely at the guest’s disposal, whilst he himself gets out of the way and returns not until the stranger has departed. The guest may stay and enjoy the wife’s society as long as he lists; the husband has no shame in the matter but considers it an honour; and all the men of this province are made wittols of by their wives in this way. The women themselves are fair and wanton. In the reign of Mangu Kaan—Möngke—as lord of the province he heard of this custom and sent an order commanding them under grievous penalties to do so no more, but to provide public hostelries for travellers. For about three years they obeyed; then they found their lands no longer fruitful and mishaps befalling them. So they collected a grand present and sent it to their Lord, praying him to let them retain the custom inherited from their ancestors; for by that usage their gods bestowed the good things they possessed, and without it they saw not how they could continue to exist. The Prince’s reply was: Since ye must needs keep your shame, keep it then—and he left them at liberty to maintain their naughty custom. And they always have kept it up, and do so still. [10]

— A quick note on Camul: the oasis and its easy living are sound; Marco’s personal visit is doubtful, because the province sits off the main Cathay road as a digression. Hung Hao and other Inner Asian notices had already reported similar hospitality among Uighurs and neighbours; ethnographic parallels exist without making the Latin heat less sensational. Möngke’s ban and the three-year crop failure are book anecdote. [11] Anyway, another digression, between north-west and north, names Chingintalas.

Chingintalas, he says, is a province at the verge of the Desert, sixteen days’ journey in extent, belonging to the Great Kaan, with numerous towns and villages and three races: Idolaters, Saracens, and some Nestorian Christians. At its northern extremity stands a mountain with excellent veins of steel and ondanique; and in the same mountain is a vein of the substance from which Salamander is made. For the real truth is that the Salamander is no beast, as they allege in our part of the world, but a substance found in the earth. Everybody must be aware that it can be no animal’s nature to live in fire, seeing that every animal is composed of the four elements. Messer Marco had a Turkish acquaintance named Zurficar—Zu’lfikār—a clever fellow, who related how he had lived three years in that region on the Great Kaan’s behalf to procure those Salamanders. They dug until they found a certain vein; the substance was crushed and divided into fibres of wool, dried, pounded in a great copper mortar, washed free of earth, spun, and made into napkins. When first made the napkins are not very white; put into the fire for a while they come out white as snow, and whenever they become dirty they are bleached again by fire. This, and nought else, is the truth about the Salamander; any other account is fabulous nonsense. And they have at Rome a napkin of this stuff which the Grand Kaan sent to the Pope to wrap the Holy Sudarium of Jesus Christ. [12]

— That demystification is one of Polo’s famous hits: asbestos spun and fire-bleached, not a mythical fire-beast. The province name itself is untraced—Chi-kin, Kemkemjút, Kobdo candidates all argued—and the Rome napkin linked to Kublai is unverified medieval colour; asbestos relics existed, the Vatican connection to the Great Kaan does not. [13] Anyway, leaving those northern digressions, the book resumes the road between north-east and east.

From the province last spoken of—understood as Sachiu’s Tangut verge—you ride ten days finding no human dwelling, or next to none. At the end of those ten days you come to Sukchur—Suhchau (Jiuquan)—a province of numerous towns and villages; the chief city is called Sukchu. The people are partly Christians and partly Idolaters, all subject to the Great Kaan. The great general province to which these belong is Tangut. Over all the mountains of this country rhubarb is found in great abundance, and thither merchants come to buy it and carry it thence all over the world. Travellers dare not visit those mountains with any cattle but those of the country, for a certain plant grows there so poisonous that cattle which eat it lose their hoofs; the cattle of the country know it and eschew it. The people live by agriculture and have not much trade; they are of a brown complexion, and the whole province is healthy. [14]

Campichu—Kanchau (Zhangye)—is also a city of Tangut, and a very great and noble one: indeed the capital and seat of government of the whole province. The people are Idolaters, Saracens, and Christians; the Christians have three very fine churches in the city, whilst the Idolaters have many minsters and abbeys after their fashion, with an enormous number of idols small and great—certain of the latter a good ten paces in stature, of wood, clay, or stone, highly polished and covered with gold. The great idols lie at length, and round about them are other figures of considerable size as if adoring and paying homage. Among the Idolaters are religious recluses who lead a more virtuous life than the rest: they abstain from lechery, though they do not regard it as a deadly sin; howbeit if any sin against nature they condemn him to death. They have an ecclesiastical calendar as we have; five days in the month they observe particularly, and on those days they would on no account slaughter any animal or eat flesh, keeping greater abstinence altogether than on other days. Among these people a man may take thirty wives, more or less, if he can afford it, each having wives in proportion to his wealth; the first wife is always held highest; men endow their wives with cattle, slaves, and money according to ability; if a man dislikes any wife he turns her off and takes another; they take to wife their cousins and their fathers’ widows (always excepting the man’s own mother), holding no sin many things that we think grievous, and in short—so the book moralises—they live like beasts. [15]

Messer Maffeo and Messer Marco Polo dwelt a whole year in this city when on a mission—so some texts say; others fold Niccolò into the sojourn and call it business rather than legation. The year is book tradition, not independently confirmed. [16] Now the book will leave Campichu and tell of other provinces toward the north, for it is going to take the reader a sixty days’ journey in that direction—first to Etzina and then into the Tartar heartland of Caracoron, where the next movement of Book I begins.

Thus the Venetian itinerary has brought the reader from jade country across the singing sands to Tangut’s Buddhist cities and the Gansu corridor that is the edge of Cathay. The Great Wall of China is never named—though Suhchau sits just within its extreme north-west angle, and the Hexi road that Campichu commands is precisely the corridor the Wall was meant to guard. That silence will become, centuries later, a dispute as loud as the Desert’s drums: Frances Wood and others have treated it as cumulative doubt; de Rachewiltz, Haw, Larner, and Vogel reply that a merchant itinerary need not chorograph every landmark a later European expected. [17] For the present the book hears only idols, rhubarb, asbestos white as snow, and the hospitality that scandalised Latin Christendom; the Wall waits unspoken beyond the next ridge of sand.
