# Chapter I-13
## Chandu: Bamboo Palace, White Horses, Bacsi and Sensin

> *“Now let us have done with this subject, and let me tell you of the great state and wonderful magnificence of the Great Lord of Lords; I mean that great Prince who is the Sovereign of the Tartars, CUBLAY by name.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 61 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Three days north and east from Chagannor (Chaghan Nor)’s crane-hunting marshes, the summer park rises out of the grass. Welcome at last to Chandu (Shangdu, the Prologue’s Kemenfu), built by Cublay (Kublai)—closing the long overland road from Venice at the very court where young Marco first knelt before the throne [1]. Even today, ruined earthworks and stone foundations trace the rings of its enclosure. Start from the outer park wall, sixteen miles around, enclosing meadows, clear streams, fountains, and stocked game to keep the imperial falcons fed [2]. A sixteen-mile ring around one sovereign’s summer pleasure draws a sharp line between the court and the outside world. A rider skirting that wall for hours would never guess what was happening inside, or how much country had been set aside for one household’s sport.

See [the map of the desert and Tangut road, I-11](ch-11-lop-tangut.md#map-of-the-desert-and-tangut-road).

Step indoors to the marble and stone palace, whose rooms stand *"all gilt and painted"* with beasts, birds, flowering trees, and human figures executed with rare skill. The fit between the Book’s description and the excavated three-walled city gives strong reason to believe Marco walked those galleries himself [3]. Inside, you see what court painters were hired to dazzle, and how much gold leaf an imperial treasury could lay onto plaster and timber. To understand what happens outdoors, follow the falconers across the turf.

More than two hundred gerfalcons sit in the mews alone, and Cublay inspects them once a week [4]. Keeping two hundred birds in trim means quiet, steady work while the Kaan is away. The moment his banner appears on the road, the pace quickens so he can watch them fed and flown whenever the mood takes him. Every perch, hood, and meat-block has to be spotless when the emperor steps through the door, because a keeper’s whole season hangs on one afternoon’s inspection.

Sometimes Cublay rides out across the park with a tame leopard perched behind his saddle, loosing it at deer or wild game while his hawks strike overhead [5]. A guest watches an emperor’s afternoon sport. A keeper counts how long the stocked deer will hold out, and how many grooms and beaters it takes to keep the coverts in order. Step back from the mews to the outer park wall, and you begin to see how every pleasure inside that sixteen-mile ring depends on men whose names never reach the Book. Even a quiet morning’s hawking rests on a small army of unseen hands.

---

A second palace stands inside a grove in the park, built to rise and vanish with the seasons. Gilded dragons coil up each timber column, bracing head and claws against the roof-beam [6]. Ask a carpenter about the roof itself. Thick canes three palms around and ten to fifteen paces long are split down the joints into twin half-round tiles and varnished to shed summer rain [7]. Getting such canes hauled intact is feat enough. Split into gutters, they are nailed fast against summer gales and guyed down by more than two hundred silk cords [8]. Look from a taut silk cord up to a dragon’s raised claws, and you see engineering and imperial theatre working hand in hand. Contemporary court poets likewise sang of wind tugging brocade cords on a palm-fibre tower at Chandu [9]—even if a famous later "Palm Hall" stood in a garden at Cambaluc (Beijing) [10]. A timber hall pitched like a nomad tent and gilded like a throne room sums up Cublay’s double world in one building.

![Diagram C16. Conjectural section of the Chandu cane palace roof, after Polo’s description: split half-cane tiles, silk tie cords, and gilded dragon columns. *(Diagram: this edition, after Polo.)*](../media/diagrams/C16-cane-palace-roof.svg){#fig-c16-cane-palace-roof}

By the Book’s calendar, Cublay spends June, July, and August at Chandu to escape the lowland heat. Then on the twenty-eighth of the August moon, the cane palace comes down [11]. Three cool months are what all that carpentry buys. When the court rides south, the stone palace stays behind while crews unnail tiles, coil two hundred silk ropes, and number every post for next summer. Twin imperial highways lined with staging palaces wait to receive the marching household between the hills and Cambaluc [12]. Every post-house along the route has to be ready with fresh mounts, fodder, and kitchens so the emperor never lacks a roof between one palace and the next.

Think of the sheer logistical ballet behind that single August morning. A European king moving his summer court packs tapestries and silver plate onto baggage carts and leaves his stone castle standing empty behind him. At Chandu, hundreds of carpenters dismantle an entire gilded throne hall—numbering bamboo roof-gutters, coiling two hundred silk guy-cords, and stowing dragon-carved columns in dry storehouses until June returns. Someone used to folding a felt steppe tent has an easy job compared with the steward tallying every timber joint of a royal pavilion before the first autumn frost.

Before the gates open, a sacred herd halts every traveller on the road. Cublay keeps over ten thousand milk-white horses and mares, whose milk only the imperial house—and the Horiad (Oirad), rewarded by Chinghis Kaan (Chinggis Khan) for an ancient battle—may taste [13]. When those white mares cross a highway, traffic freezes. Even a great lord rides half a day out of his way rather than cut across their path [14]. Privilege at court is measured in who has to wait, and in whose horses must stand aside in the dust. A courier with urgent imperial dispatches still reins in at the roadside until the last white foal clears the track. Once the herd has passed, the highway belongs to the court’s business again.

Stay on the turf to watch the parting rite. Priests draw milk from the white mares and scatter it across the grass and air so earth, sky, and spirits take their share. This offering secures blessing for the Kaan’s family, his retinue, his herds, and the harvest [15]. A farmer watching the autumn sky understands why you remind the powers of what rides on good weather. A courtier wonders how the Kaan keeps rain off his summer roofs in the first place. Both stand on the same turf while the milk sinks into the soil and the pack-trains form up at the gate.

---

Weather-conjurers from Tebet (Eastern Tibet) and Keshimur (Kashmir) keep the sky bright over the Kaan’s roofs while rain falls all around. The Book attributes their feats to the Devil, though the practitioners themselves claim holiness [16], and adds the grim charge that they eat executed criminals while sparing natural deaths [17]. That Christian horror shows how quickly a medieval listener fitted unfamiliar ritualists into a familiar mould. Distant ascetics become sorcerers at a stroke, and what a local monk might explain as ritual power turns, in a European prison cell, into dark commerce with demons.

Think of a Venetian merchant standing beneath the eaves of the Cane Palace during an August thunderstorm, watching clouds part around the park wall while thunder rolls across the outer meadows. Whether he credits mountain weather-magic or lucky summer winds, the spectacle leaves every envoy convinced that even the sky obeys the Great Kaan’s household.

Inside the great hall, the *Bacsi* stage a closer wonder. Cublay’s table stands high above the floor while wine and spiced-liquor flagons rest ten paces off upon a grand buffet [18]. Whenever the Kaan calls for drink, golden cups glide through the air to his hand untouched before ten thousand watching guests [19]. Stand at the back of that hall and you see the cup arrive without seeing what set it moving. For a guest seated near the door, half the marvel lies in the sudden hush of ten thousand voices as the golden cup crosses the hall on its own.

Picture the sheer stagecraft of that moment for a European audience. At a feast in Venice or Paris, squires carry silver flagons around the benches while minstrels play. At Chandu, the emperor raises two fingers, ten thousand courtiers hold their breath, and a brimming golden goblet sails across thirty feet of open air straight to his dais—leaving even sceptics wondering whether hidden wires, clever sleight of hand, or court magic drove the show.

When the Bacsi prepare a feast for an idol, they petition the court stewards for black-faced sheep, incense, and fragrant lignaloes, and the Kaan orders every item supplied [20]. Lamps flare, sweet smoke curls, cooked meat is laid before the image, and broth is scattered across the floor on feast-days kept much like European saints’ days [21]. A kitchen porter smells the lignaloes before he ever sees the altar; an outsider grasps what is happening with the broth long before he finds a word for why it is done. Every offering begins with a clerk’s tally in the palace stores.

These Bacsi inhabit monastery towns of two thousand shaven monks, some orders permitting marriage [22]. The larger the abbey, the harder a visitor has to listen to tell one sect’s rule from another’s. You soon have to ask which priests keep vows of celibacy and which raise families beside the cloister. Across the courtyard sit the *Sensin*, eating bran stirred into hot water, wearing hemp, and sleeping on bare mats [23]. Rival monks call them *Patarins*—borrowing an Italian label for European heretics [24]. To catch the real argument between two Asian orders inside the park, a listener first has to peel off the Italian name tag and hear why each sect guarded its own rule before the throne.

Even at the edge of the summer park, the Book’s eye keeps returning to the same double ledger. On one side sits what the court pays out in sheep, silk, lignaloes, and white mares. On the other sits what the watching crowd believes it has just seen, whether a golden cup gliding through the air or a storm-cloud turned aside from the roof. Every marvel in the hall has its price in the storeroom.

With the white mares’ milk scattered on the August grass at Chandu and the silk guy-cords coiled away, the long overland trek from Venice and Ayas (Yumurtalık) is complete. Think back for a second over the road behind us: from the crowded stone mole at Ayas, across the freezing passes of Greater Hermenia (Greater Armenia) and the scorching sand-winds of the Persian Gulf, past the ruby cliffs of Badashan (Badakhshan) and the singing dunes of the Great Desert, all the way to this sixteen-mile summer park on the edge of Cathay (North China). Every city, desert spring, and caravan price has brought us to the gates of Cublay Kaan’s empire.

Step now across the threshold into Volume II: the dust of the caravan trail falls behind us, and the Book marches straight onto Cublay Kaan’s battlefield against his rebel kinsman Nayan and into the most sophisticated state machine on thirteenth-century earth. From this summer park, we move inside the winter capital’s walls, meet the twelve thousand horsemen guarding the imperial feast, and watch a great empire’s paper money, post-horses, and palaces run like clockwork before our eyes.
