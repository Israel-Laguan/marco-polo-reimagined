# Chapter I-13
## Chandu: Bamboo Palace, White Horses, Bacsi and Sensin

> *“Now let us have done with this subject, and let me tell you of the great state and wonderful magnificence of the Great Lord of Lords; I mean that great Prince who is the Sovereign of the Tartars, CUBLAY by name.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 61 (Yule–Cordier)


---

Three days north and east from Chagannor (Chaghan Nor), the summer park rises out of the grass. The Book brings you to Chandu (Shangdu, the Prologue’s Kemenfu), built by Cublay (Kublai). Here, too, the Prologue’s first audience places the Polos [1]. Even today, enough ruined earthworks and foundations survive to trace several rings of enclosure. Start from the outer wall, which Polo measures at sixteen miles around. Inside it lie meadows, clear streams, fountains, and game stocked to keep the imperial falcons fed [2]. A sixteen-mile ring around a sovereign’s park draws a sharp line between his pleasure and the outside world. A rider outside that wall could skirt it for hours without catching a glimpse of court business inside. He would never guess how much country had been set aside for one household’s sport.

See [the map of the desert and Tangut road, I-11](ch-11-lop-tangut.md#map-of-the-desert-and-tangut-road).

Step indoors to the marble and stone palace. Its rooms are *"all gilt and painted"* with beasts, birds, flowering trees, and human figures, executed with rare skill. Nineteenth-century visitors like Bushell still picked up marble fragments across the grass. Stephen Haw argues that the Book’s fit with the excavated three-walled city gives good reason to think Polo saw both park and palace [3]. Inside, you see what court painters were hired to dazzle, and how much gold leaf an imperial treasury could lay onto plaster and timber. To understand what happens outdoors, follow the falconers across the turf.

More than two hundred gerfalcons sit in the mews alone, and Cublay inspects them once a week [4]. Keeping two hundred birds in trim means quiet, steady work while the Kaan is away. The moment his banner appears on the road, the pace quickens so he can watch them fed and flown whenever the mood takes him. A keeper who has spent all spring moulting and feeding his birds knows that one afternoon’s royal inspection decides his standing for the season. Every perch, hood, and meat-block has to be ready when the emperor steps through the door.

Sometimes Cublay rides out through the park with a tame leopard perched behind his saddle. He looses it at deer or wild game while his hawks take the birds [5]. A guest watches an emperor’s afternoon sport. A keeper counts how long the stocked deer will hold out, and how many riders it takes to keep the streams and coverts in order. Even a quiet afternoon’s hawking rests on a small army of grooms and beaters. Time and again at Chandu, the spectacle on the surface rests on unseen labour below.

When you step back from the mews to the outer park wall, you begin to see how every pleasure inside that sixteen-mile ring depends on men whose names never reach the Book.

---

A second palace stands in the park, built to rise and vanish with the seasons. In Ramusio’s text it sits inside a grove, with a gilded dragon coiling up each timber column. The dragon’s head and claws brace against the roof-beam [6]. Ask a carpenter about the roof itself. Thick canes, three palms around and ten to fifteen paces long, are split down the joints into twin half-round tiles and varnished to shed summer rain [7]. Getting such canes hauled intact is feat enough. Split into gutters, they roof a royal hall. In Ramusio and the L witness the tiles are nailed fast so no gale lifts them. More than two hundred silk cords guy the pavilion against the wind [8]. Look from a taut silk cord up to a dragon’s raised claws, and you see engineering and imperial theatre working hand in hand. One holds the roof down in a summer squall. The other reminds every envoy whose roof he is standing under. A timber hall that can be pitched like a tent and gilded like a throne room sums up Cublay’s double world in a single building.

![Diagram C16. Conjectural section of the Chandu cane palace roof, after Polo’s description: split half-cane tiles, silk tie cords, and gilded dragon columns. *(Diagram: this edition, after Polo.)*](../media/diagrams/C16-cane-palace-roof.svg){#fig-c16-cane-palace-roof}

— For an echo of those cords, try the poet Sa Dula, who watched the wind tug brocade ropes on a palm-fibre tower at Chandu [9]. The Chinese “Palm Hall” that Cordier took for confirmation turns out to stand in a Cambaluc (Beijing) garden, attested after Polo’s time [10].

By the Book’s calendar, Cublay spends June, July, and August at Chandu to escape the lowland heat. Then on the twenty-eighth of the August moon, the cane palace comes down [11]. Three cool months are what all that carpentry buys. When the court rides south, the stone palace stays behind while crews unnail tiles, coil two hundred silk ropes, and number every post for next summer. Anyone used to packing up a steppe tent has an easy time compared with the steward charged with dismantling a whole royal hall and stowing its timber until spring. Every beam, tile, and cord has to be tallied so the pavilion can rise again without a missing joint when June returns. Palladius even traced two imperial highways between the capitals, lined with staging palaces along the road [12]. Moving the court meant provisioning every stop between the hills and Cambaluc. Every post-house along the route had to be ready with fresh mounts, fodder, and kitchens for a household on the march, so the emperor’s household never lacked a roof between one palace and the next.

Before the gates open, a sacred herd can halt every traveller on the road. Cublay keeps over ten thousand milk-white horses and mares. Only the imperial house—and the Horiad (Oirad), rewarded by Chinghis Kaan (Chinggis Khan) for an ancient battle—may taste their milk [13]. When those white mares cross a road, traffic freezes. Even a great lord may ride half a day out of his way rather than cut across their path [14]. Privilege at court is measured in who has to wait, and in whose horses must stand aside in the dust. A courier with urgent letters still reins in at the verge until the last white foal clears the track. Once the herd has passed, the road belongs to the court’s business again.

Stay to watch the parting rite. Priests and attendants draw milk from the white mares and scatter it across the grass so earth, air, and spirits take their share. In Polo’s telling, this offering secures blessing for the Kaan’s family, his retinue, his herds, and the harvest [15]. A farmer watching the autumn sky understands why you remind the powers of what rides on good weather. A courtier wonders how the Kaan keeps rain off his summer roofs in the first place. Both stand on the same turf while the milk sinks into the soil and the pack-trains form up at the gate.

---

Polo has an answer for the weather too: enchanters from Tebet (Eastern Tibet) and Keshimur (Kashmir) who drive storm-clouds clear of the palace. They leave the sky bright over the Kaan’s roofs while rain falls all around. Polo credits their feats to the Devil, though the practitioners themselves claim holiness [16]. He adds the grim charge that they eat executed criminals while sparing anyone who dies naturally [17]. The Book’s Christian horror shows how quickly a medieval listener fitted unfamiliar miracle-workers into a familiar mould. Distant ritualists become sorcerers at a stroke. What a local monk might explain as ritual power turns, in a European prison cell, into dark commerce with demons.

Inside the great hall, the *Bacsi* stage a closer wonder. Cublay’s table stands high above the floor while wine and spiced-liquor flagons rest ten paces off. Ramusio sets them upon a great buffet [18]. Whenever the Kaan calls for drink, Polo says, the cups glide through the air to his hand untouched, before ten thousand watching guests [19]. Stand at the back of that hall and you see the cup arrive without seeing what set it moving. The Book even invites sceptical Europeans to check with magicians at home. For a guest seated near the door, half the marvel lies in the sudden hush of ten thousand voices as the golden cup crosses the hall on its own.

When the Bacsi prepare a feast for an idol, they petition the court stewards for black-faced sheep, incense, and fragrant lignaloes. The Kaan orders every item supplied [20]. Lamps flare, sweet smoke curls through the hall, and the priests lay cooked meat before the image while scattering broth across the floor for the god’s portion. They celebrate named feast-days much as Europe keeps saints’ days [21]. A kitchen porter smells the lignaloes before he sees the altar; an outsider grasps what is happening with the broth long before he finds a word for why it is done. Both the steward who signs for the sheep and the priest who pours the broth have their eye on the same festival calendar. Every offering begins with a clerk’s tally in the palace stores.

Polo gives these Bacsi monastic houses as large as towns—two thousand monks in a single abbey. They go with shaven heads and shaven chins, and some orders allow marriage [22]. The bigger the abbey, the harder a visitor has to listen to tell one sect’s rule from another’s. You soon have to ask which priests keep vows of celibacy and which raise families beside the cloister. A passing merchant hears bells and chanting across the wall and still needs a local guide to tell him whose house he has reached.

Across the courtyard sit the *Sensin*, whom Polo puts on a harsher diet of bran stirred into hot water. They wear hemp, sleep on bare mats, and take no wives. Their temples house female-named images, and Polo says they sometimes worship fire [23]. Lean closer to hear what rival monks call them: the quarrel reaches the Book under the Italian label *Patarins*, a word for European heretics [24]. To catch the real argument between two Asian orders, a listener first has to peel off the Italian name tag. Only then can you hear how two local traditions actually spoke about each other inside the park, and why each guarded its own name before the throne.

Even at the edge of the summer park, Polo’s eye keeps returning to the same double ledger. On one side sits what the court pays out in sheep, silk, and white mares. On the other sits what the watching crowd believes it has just seen, whether a cup gliding through the air or a storm-cloud turned aside from the roof. Every wonder in the hall has its price in the storeroom.

The park walls, the silk guy-cords, and the white mares all belong to the sovereign the Book keeps setting at the centre of the world. We have watched what it takes to raise his cane hall in June, keep his falcons and white herds in order, and pack up his summer court when August ends. Now step into the next volume and see what Cublay does when a prince of his own blood takes the field against him.
