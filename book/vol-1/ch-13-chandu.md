# Chapter I-13
## Chandu: Bamboo Palace, White Horses, Bacsi and Sensin

> *“Now let us have done with this subject, and let me tell you of the great state and wonderful magnificence of the Great Lord of Lords; I mean that great Prince who is the Sovereign of the Tartars, CUBLAY by name.”*
> — Book I, close of the chapter Of Chandu (Yule–Cordier)

---

Marco Polo starts by narrating that three days’ riding from Chagannor—Chaghan Nor, the White Pool—between north-east and north, brings you to a city that the book names Chandu (Shangdu)—Upper Court—built by the Kaan now reigning. The road that has led the traveller from Tangut through Tenduc and the lakes of five cranes here ends its Book-I spine; behind lie Lop’s whispering sands and Caracoron’s earthen walls; ahead, beyond the summer park, wait Cambaluc (Khanbaliq / Beijing) and the winter seat that Volume II will open. [1]

Two roads, the Chinese geographers later reckoned, ran between Peking and this cool plateau: an eastern way by Tu-shi-k’ow with its string of temporary *nabo* palaces, and a western return by Ye-hu ling with more staging-houses still. Polo’s three marches from the White Pool sit on that western approach; the book itself, having already told Chagannor, simply names the city and walks you through the gate. Chandu is the same summer orbit the Prologue called Kemenfu—Kaipingfu—when young Marco first stood before Cublay; Book I now arrives by geography where the family drama arrived by embassy. [2]

There stands at Chandu, he says, a very fine marble Palace. Its chambers are gilt and painted with figures of men and beasts and birds, and with trees and flowers of many kinds, executed with such exquisite art that you regard them with delight and astonishment. Round this Palace a wall encloses a compass of some sixteen miles. Inside the Park are fountains and rivers and brooks, and beautiful meadows stocked with all kinds of wild animals—excluding such as are of ferocious nature—which the Emperor has procured to feed his gerfalcons and hawks kept there in mew. Of gerfalcons alone there are more than two hundred, without reckoning the other hawks. The Kaan himself goes every week to see his birds sitting in mew; and sometimes he rides through the park with a leopard behind him on his horse’s croup, and if he sees any animal that takes his fancy he slips the leopard at it, and the game when taken is made over to feed the hawks. This he does for diversion. [3]

Moreover—at a spot in the Park where there is a charming wood—he has another Palace built of cane, of which the book must give a description. It is gilt all over, and most elaborately finished inside. It is stayed on gilt and lacquered columns, on each of which is a dragon all gilt, the tail attached to the column whilst the head supports the architrave, and the claws stretched out right and left to bear the same. The roof, like the rest, is formed of canes, covered with a varnish so strong and excellent that no amount of rain will rot them. These canes are a good three palms in girth, and from ten to fifteen paces in length. They are cut across at each knot, and the pieces split so as to form from each two hollow tiles, and with these the house is roofed—only every such tile of cane must be nailed down to prevent the wind from lifting it. In short, the whole Palace is built of these canes, which serve also a great variety of other useful purposes. The construction is so devised that it can be taken down and put up again with great celerity; it can all be taken to pieces and removed whithersoever the Emperor may command. When erected, it is braced against mishaps from the wind by more than two hundred cords of silk. [4]

— That cane palace is no romance invention. Shangdu archaeology and Yuan notices confirm demountable bamboo or cane ceremonial architecture at the summer seat—Palladius’s Palm Hall in the western garden, Rashid al-Din’s second palace outside the town, the *Altan Tobchi*’s cane tent. Ramusio is fuller on dragons and roofing tiles than the Geographic French; the engineering is confirmed in spirit if not in every gilt claw. Bushell’s 1872 visit found marble fragments and a double *enceinte*; Polo’s sixteen-mile park circuit runs larger than the surveyed outer enclosure, as travellers’ miles often do. [5] Anyway, next he mentions how the year is spent.

The Lord abides at this Park of his, dwelling sometimes in the Marble Palace and sometimes in the Cane Palace, for three months of the year—to wit, June, July, and August—preferring the residence because it is by no means hot; in fact it is a very cool place. When the twenty-eighth day of the August Moon arrives he takes his departure, and the Cane Palace is taken to pieces. But the book must tell what happens when he goes away from this Palace every year on that date. [6]

You must know that the Kaan keeps an immense stud of white horses and mares—more than ten thousand of them, all pure white without a speck. The milk of these mares is drunk by himself and his family, and by none else, except by those of one great tribe that have also the privilege of drinking it. This privilege was granted them by Chinghis Kaan—Chinggis Khan—on account of a certain victory that they helped him to win long ago. The name of the tribe is Horiad—the Oirad. Now when these mares are passing across the country, and any one falls in with them, be he the greatest lord in the land, he must not presume to pass until the mares have gone by; he must either tarry where he is, or go a half-day’s journey round if need so be, so as not to come nigh them—for they are to be treated with the greatest respect. Well, when the Lord sets out from the Park on the twenty-eighth of August, the milk of all those mares is taken and sprinkled on the ground. And this is done on the injunction of the Idolaters and Idol-priests, who say that it is an excellent thing to sprinkle that milk on the ground every twenty-eighth of August, so that the Earth and the Air and the False Gods shall have their share of it, and the Spirits likewise that inhabit the Air and the Earth. And thus those beings will protect and bless the Kaan and his children and his wives and his folk and his gear, and his cattle and his horses, his corn and all that is his. After this is done, the Emperor is off and away—toward Cambaluc and the winter courts that the next volume will describe. [7]

— White horses as imperial prestige, Oirad privilege after submission to Chinggis, and mare’s-milk libations are all well attested: Rubruck saw May consecrations and new kumis; Palladius notes seventh-moon ancestral milk rites; later Qing still received white-horse tributes. Polo’s twenty-eighth of August sits beside May and July ethnographic parallels—calendar compression or a distinct court rite. The headcount of ten thousand is rhetorical. [8] Anyway, the book must now tell a strange thing hitherto forgotten.

During the three months of every year that the Lord resides at that place, if it should happen to be bad weather, there are certain crafty enchanters and astrologers in his train, adepts in necromancy and the arts that Latins call diabolic, who are able—so the book insists—to prevent any cloud or storm from passing over the spot on which the Emperor’s Palace stands. The sorcerers who do this are called Tebet and Kesimur—Tibet and Kashmir—which are the names of two nations of Idolaters. Whatever they do in this way is by the help of the Devil, says the book’s Christian voice; but they make those people believe that it is compassed by dint of their own sanctity and the help of God. They always go, the text adds with Latin distaste, in a state of dirt and uncleanness, unwashed, unkempt, and sordidly attired. [9]

— Weather magic is a cultural fact among Mongols and Inner Asians—jade *yada* stones, shamanic rain-making, European medieval parallels alike. Whether these court workers truly kept storms off the palace, or whether Latin diabolism is simply the frame for a performance the court valued, remains open. Narrate the marvel; weigh the frame. [10] Anyway, next he mentions a darker custom.

These people also have a custom which must be told. If a man is condemned to death and executed by the lawful authority, they take his body and cook and eat it. But if any one die a natural death, then they will not eat the body. So the book ascribes cannibalism to Tibetan and Kashmiri wonder-workers at the summer court; later chapters will repeat similar charges against other highland peoples. The ascription is prejudice speaking with a traveller’s mouth—kept here as the book states it, and weighed in the notes. Oriental writers are often silent or contradictory; Frankish fear of Tartar cannibalism was already a stock topos. [11]

There is another marvel performed by those Bacsi—Bakhshi, the lamas and wonder-workers of whom the book has been speaking as knowing so many enchantments. For when the Great Kaan is at his capital and in his great Palace, seated at his table, which stands on a platform some eight cubits above the ground, his cups are set before him on a great buffet in the middle of the hall pavement, at a distance of some ten paces from his table, and filled with wine or other good spiced liquor such as they use. Now when the Lord desires to drink, these enchanters by the power of their enchantments cause the cups to move from their place without being touched by anybody, and to present themselves to the Emperor. This every one present may witness, and there are ofttimes more than ten thousand persons thus present. ’Tis a truth and no lie!—so the book swears—and so will tell you the sages of our own country who understand necromancy, for they also can perform it. [12]

And when the Idol Festivals come round, these Bacsi go to the Prince and say: “Sire, the Feast of such a god is come” (naming him). “My Lord, you know that this god, when he gets no offerings, always sends bad weather and spoils our seasons. So we pray you to give us such and such a number of black-faced sheep,” naming whatever number they please. “And we beg also that we may have such a quantity of incense, and such a quantity of lignaloes,” and so much of this, so much of that, according to their fancy—“that we may perform a solemn service and a great sacrifice to our Idols, and that so they may be induced to protect us and all that is ours.” The Bacsi say these things to the Barons entrusted with the Stewardship, who stand round the Great Kaan; these repeat them to the Kaan; and he then orders the Barons to give everything that the Bacsi have asked for. And when they have got the articles they go and make a great feast in honour of their god, and hold great ceremonies of worship with grand illuminations and quantities of incense of a variety of odours, which they make up from different aromatic spices. And then they cook the meat, and set it before the idols, and sprinkle the broth hither and thither, saying that in this way the idols get their bellyful. Thus it is that they keep their festivals. You must know that each of the idols has a name of his own, and a feast-day, just as our Saints have their anniversaries. [13]

They have also immense Minsters and Abbeys, some of them as big as a small town, with more than two thousand monks—after their fashion—in a single abbey. These monks dress more decently than the rest of the people, and have the head and beard shaven. There are some among these Bacsi who are allowed by their rule to take wives, and who have plenty of children. Shangdu’s Mongol ruin-name as a “city of 108 temples” and the Yun-Hien abbot stele of 1288 corroborate great Bacsi houses at the summer capital; the cup marvel Odoric later echoes more briefly as jugglers’ work—stagecraft the likelier register than diabolism. [14]

Then there is another kind of devotees called Sensin, who are men of extraordinary abstinence after their fashion, and lead a life of such hardship as the book will describe. All their life long they eat nothing but bran, which they take mixed with hot water. That is their food: bran, and nothing but bran; and water for their drink. ’Tis a lifelong fast!—so that one may well say their life is one of extraordinary asceticism. They have great idols, and plenty of them; but they sometimes also worship fire. The other Idolaters who are not of this sect call these people heretics—*Patarins*, as Latins would say—because they do not worship their idols in their own fashion. Those of whom the book is speaking would not take a wife on any consideration. They wear dresses of hempen stuff, black and blue, and sleep upon mats; in fact their asceticism is something astonishing. Their idols are all feminine, that is to say, they have women’s names. [15]

These Sensin are the Tao-sze—Daoist clergy—of Chinese speech; their abbeys stood even at Shangdu itself, one inside the city and one without; and the book’s “heretics” are Buddhist contempt translated into a Lombard slur. *Patarin* was Polo’s European word for Cathar-type heretics, applied cross-culturally; Buddhist–Daoist rivalry and Yuan-era Daoist persecution colour the label. The bran diet may be a mistranslation or a narrow gloss; the feminine-named idols fit certain Daoist pantheon figures. Ramusian expansions on Sensin run fuller than the Geographic French and should be heard as secondary colour. [16]

Now let us have done with this subject. The young Venetian who rode three days from the White Pool stands at last inside the wonder he will serve. Marble rooms painted with beasts and flowers; a cane palace that can be struck like a tent and braced with silk against the plateau wind; white mares whose milk no common lord may taste; milk flung upon the earth so that spirits and false gods may bless corn and cattle; weather-workers from Tebet and Kesimur; cups that walk, or seem to walk, across a crowded hall; Bacsi abbeys like small towns; Sensin living on bran and water. Chandu is not a rumour heard at Etzina or a digression told at Caracoron. It is the Upper Court, cool in the three summer moons, empty when the August Moon turns and the Court rolls south. Horizon names already whisper: Cachar Modun—wherever that hunting-ground exactly lay—will appear when the book tells the Kaan’s sport; Cambaluc waits as the winter capital and the city of paper money, posts, and granaries. Coleridge’s later “Xanadu,” dreamed from Purchas in 1797, is reception history for another volume—not developed here. [17]

For the great state and wonderful magnificence of the Great Lord of Lords—that great Prince who is the Sovereign of the Tartars, Cublay (Qubilai / Kublai) by name, that most noble and puissant Lord—belongs to the next volume. Volume I has brought three Venetians from a Genoese prison’s memory through Hermenia and Persia, the roof of the world, Lop, Tangut, Caracoron, Tenduc, and the White Pool, to the park where marble and cane share one cool summer. Volume II will open upon the man who built this city, wore its white-horse privilege as heir of Chinghis, and employed a young foreigner long enough for a book to be born.
