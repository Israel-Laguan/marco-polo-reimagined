# Chapter I-13
## Chandu: Bamboo Palace, White Horses, Bacsi and Sensin

> *“Now let us have done with this subject, and let me tell you of the great state and wonderful magnificence of the Great Lord of Lords; I mean that great Prince who is the Sovereign of the Tartars, CUBLAY by name.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 61 (Yule–Cordier)


---

Leave the cranes at Chagannor and ride three days north and east. The Book brings you to Chandu (Shangdu, the Prologue’s Kemenfu). Cublay (Kublai) built it, and the account of the first audience in the Prologue brings the Polos to this same place [1]. The old approach is a ride before you can ask anyone who might show you round. Today the ruins are extensive enough for several lines of enclosure to be made out. How does a man who has spent three days in the saddle go about taking it in? Start from the wall. Inside it are streams and fountains and grass, with game to keep the hunting birds fed. The Book gives the park a circuit of sixteen miles [2]. A line round a ruler’s pleasure ground divides his business from other people’s business. He has put country enough behind the wall to take a horse out, find a stream worth crossing and meet with something to hunt. A visitor on the outside of the wall could pass quite a distance without getting farther into the affairs of the palace.

See [the map of the desert and Tangut road, I-11](ch-11-lop-tangut.md#map-of-the-desert-and-tangut-road).

Go indoors for a moment and look at the stone palace in Polo’s account. There are birds and beasts on the painted walls, trees with flowers on them, figures of people and work picked out in gold. Bushell found fragments of marble when he visited the site in the nineteenth century. Haw argues from the fit between text and the excavated enclosures that Polo had a good chance of seeing both palace and park [3]. From the inside of a room you can see what court painters are asked to do. To understand what is going on outside, follow the man who feeds the hawks.

More than two hundred gerfalcons alone, the Book says. The Kaan comes by to look at the birds in the mews once a week [4]. To keep that many birds is to have a great deal of work to do when the Kaan is absent and more when he proposes to come. The Book allows him to see them as the game is brought in if he pleases.

Sometimes he takes a horse and has a leopard ride behind him. Let it go at one of the animals that lives inside the wall. The hawks get the quarry [5]. The man watching may remember an emperor’s pleasure as a ride through the park, while the man caring for the birds is already thinking how long the game will last. It’s worth noticing how many of the entertainments here have work going on below the line of sight of an ordinary visitor.

---

There is another palace in the account, which the men who put it up can take down again. In Ramusio it stands among trees, with a gilt dragon climbing each supporting column. The head and claws seem to take the weight of the beam [6]. Now ask one of the builders what goes on top. Cane cut across at the joints will split lengthwise to make two tiles. Polo gives the stems a girth of three palms and a length of ten to fifteen paces. Varnish will help them take the rain [7]. Even before you cut one you’ve got something of a job in getting it from one place to another without cracking it. Think what a useful thing it becomes once the builder has his two halves to lay down over the room. In Ramusio and the L witness the tiles are nailed down. When the building is up, more than two hundred silk cords brace it in Polo’s telling [8]. Look from one of those cords to the dragon. You’ve gone from something that has to hold in a hard wind to something that has to make a visitor see the emperor’s house in a pair of raised claws.

— For an echo of those cords, try the poet Sa Dula. He has the wind tug at cords of brocade on a palm-fibre tower in an upper park at Chandu [9]. It is a place to hear the wind rise. The Chinese record that Cordier took for confirmation of the cane palace takes us to a quite different building, in the imperial gardens at Cambaluc (Beijing), and one attested after Polo’s day [10].

The Book gives the court June, July and August here to get out of the heat. On the twenty-eighth of the August moon by its reckoning, the summer stay ends and the cane palace comes down [11]. Stay here in the hot months by all means. The coolness is part of what all the work on the walls and roof is buying the court. Leave at the end of summer, and the air, the wall and the stone palace will stay behind. What goes where when work starts on the roof, and how do you pack the cords so that the men can find the ones they need next time? Anyone used to moving a whole household could have a look and find himself comparatively well off. The man in charge here is moving a building. The larger business of moving the court has left traces as well. Palladius describes two roads to and from the capital, with temporary palaces at intervals for the Kaan [12]. Have people at every stop expecting an arrival and you can begin to picture how far the removal extends beyond the men taking down the roof.

A herd can bring everything to a halt. In the Book the Kaan has over ten thousand white horses and mares. The imperial family drinks the milk of the mares. The Horiad (Oirad) have that honour too, given them by Chinghis Kaan (Chinggis Khan), Polo hears, for helping him win a battle [13]. If the herd comes across your route you have to wait until it passes. Even a lord may have to go half a day out of his way to give the mares room [14]. You can see where the privilege lies even if you’ve been sent on such an errand that all you want is to get past.

Stay to watch the departure. They take milk from the mares and scatter it so that the earth and air and the spirits have their share. In the Book, when they have given this offering the Kaan can leave in the expectation of protection for his family, the people who travel with him, the animals and the crops they need [15]. Someone whose living turns on the rain may see the sense of reminding the powers that can send it how much is riding on the year. Somebody else might look up and ask how the emperor manages to keep bad weather away from his rooms.

---

Polo has men from Tebet (Eastern Tibet) and Keshimur (Kashmir) at court who can keep the clouds from coming over the palace. He readily puts their work down to the Devil, though the people who perform it credit their sanctity [16]. He also attributes to them the eating of executed criminals, sparing those who died naturally. The Book is the source of that charge. The Christian terms of its tale tell us much about what the writer already expects when he hears about people who can command the weather [17].

In the great hall the Bacsi have the Kaan’s drink ready to come to him. Set the table high above the floor. The cups sit some ten paces away, full of wine or spiced liquor. In Ramusio there is a great buffet to put them on [18]. When the emperor wants a drink, the Book says, they move to him by themselves [19]. Ten thousand people may be in the room to see it in Polo’s telling. Stand among the people who could have seen the cup arrive without ever getting near enough to see what started it moving. It’s an unusually good place to have had a close friend a little nearer the table. The Book would let you ask the experts at home. It maintains there are men in Europe who could do the same.

When the Bacsi need a feast for a god, they approach the officials standing near the Kaan. First ask for black-faced sheep. Have incense and fragrant lignaloes on the list as well. The emperor hears from the officials and tells them to give the priests what they ask for [20]. Then come the lights and perfumes and the cooking. They lay the meat before the god and scatter the broth to give him his share. The Book singles out the use of names and particular feast days and thinks of the days saints are given in Europe [21]. A man carrying something into the hall can hear the ceremony and smell the incense before he sees who is there. It takes a moment longer for an observer from abroad to realise what the people holding it are doing with the broth, and a good deal longer to find the words for why they do it. The man asked to provide the sheep and the man who counts on the god for good weather have different reasons to look at the calendar. Both of them need to know when the feast will be.

Polo gives the Bacsi houses large enough to pass for little towns. An abbey may have two thousand monks, in his telling. He notices a shaven head and beard, and says some of the Bacsi are allowed to marry [22]. The more men the Book puts in a single house, the more a visitor will have to ask whose rules he is actually hearing about. Sometimes the people observing the rules of one house will have a good deal to say about the people observing the rules of another.

What can you eat if you are a Sensin? The Book would see you live on bran stirred into hot water. No wife, hemp to wear and a mat to lie on. From the look of the image before you, you might even be able to tell which house you had entered. Polo gives the figures female names and says that the Sensin sometimes worship fire [23]. Lean over to hear what the other religious men at court call their neighbours. The Book has them complain that these people worship in a different fashion. The charge travels into the Book as *Patarins*, a name a man in Italy could use for heretics a very long way from here [24]. A person who wants to know how an argument between neighbours sounded has to get his ear past what it was called in Italy.

The gates, the cords and the herd belong to the man the Book keeps setting in the middle of things. We’ve watched what has to happen when he leaves his park. Come into the next volume and see what Cublay does when a prince of his own house takes the field against him.

[1] ✓ The stage is from the last place in the route, Chagannor. Cordier quotes Palladius on the road, the journey’s stages and the difficulty of fixing the lake. Chandu is the site of the Prologue audience, where Yule compares Kemenfu with Kaipingfu [@yc, 1.61 nn. 1–2; @yc, Prologue 13 n. 1].

[2] ~ The sixteen-mile circuit is the Book’s. Bushell describes the enclosures and UNESCO’s 25,000 hectares refer to the full designated property, including areas outside the city and palace [@yc, 1.61 n. 2; @bushell1874notes; @unesco2012xanadu].

[3] ✓ Bushell records marble remains and describes the site where the account places the palace. Haw finds the fit between the text and the excavated three-walled city consistent with Polo’s having entered the palace and park [@yc, 1.61 n. 2; @bushell1874notes; @haw2006marco, ch. 5, p. 69 in the 2009 paperback; @rossabi1988khubilai, ch. 2].

[4] ~ Polo distinguishes gerfalcons from other hawks and supplies a figure and a weekly visit. The number is the Book’s estimate [@yc, 1.61].

[5] ✓ Yule compares accounts of the use of trained hunting animals, including Rubruck. The ride with a leopard behind the emperor on the horse belongs to Polo’s account [@yc, 1.61 n. 3].

[6] ~ Ramusio-only. The grove and the dragons holding up the beam come from the version Yule follows for those details. V has a description of decorated columns [@yc, 1.61 n. 4; @simion2019devisement, 39.5–6].

[7] ✓ The French has a cane palace gilt inside, varnishing and the cutting at each joint. V also describes split-cane tiles. The witnesses differ in the details they give about the roof [@eusebi2018devisement, ch. 74 §§ 9–13; @simion2019devisement, 39.5–6; @yc, 1.61 n. 4].

[8] ~ Yule draws on Ramusio for the fuller roof description. The Moule–Pelliot edition marks the nails with L as well and the wind clause with P. V gives two hundred silk cords [@moule1938description, ch. 75, p. 186; @simion2019devisement, 39.5–6; @yc, 1.61 n. 4].

[9] ✓ Sa Dula’s poem describes a palm-fibre tower and brocade cords tugged by the wind. The resemblance gives a comparison for some materials and details. The building Polo describes remains unidentified [@sadulashangjing, “上苑棕毛百尺樓”].

[10] ✗ The Chinese Palm Hall passage locates it in the western garden of the Longfu Palace at Cambaluc. The Palace Library records it in 1311 and the dynastic annals record work in 1324–25. Cordier prints Palladius’s placement at Chandu [@tao1959nancun, juan 21; @mishujianzhi, juan 5; @yuanshi1370, juan 29; @yc, 1.61 n. 4].

[11] ~ The Book gives a summer stay and the twenty-eighth of the August moon for departure. Cordier cites Palladius for a usual move to Chandu in the fourth month and return in the ninth. See [Chart C17, the seasonal court](../media/diagrams/C17-seasonal-orbit.pdf) [@yc, 1.61 n. 5].

[12] ✓ The two roads and their temporary palaces come from Palladius, quoted by Cordier. He identifies the western road for the return and gives stages and resting places for both approaches [@yc, 1.61 n. 1].

[13] ~ The Book’s headcount and the story of the origins of the Horiad privilege stand alongside other accounts of honoured white horses. Yule discusses the identification of the group and the terms of the privilege [@yc, 1.61 n. 6].

[14] ~ Polo describes stopping or going round to avoid coming close to the mares. This is his account of the deference shown to the herd [@yc, 1.61].

[15] ✓ Yule compares Rubruck and other accounts of offerings of mare’s milk. Polo’s occasion and day differ from the dates of some of these other observances [@yc, 1.61 n. 7].

[16] ~ Yule compares accounts of weather magic and the use of stones in such practices. The Book identifies the people and gives the Christian explanation of their claimed powers [@yc, 1.61 n. 8].

[17] ? The claim concerns the bodies of people put to death by the authorities. Yule compares accounts with different details and records uncertainty about the charge [@yc, 1.61 n. 9].

[18] ~ From Ramusio in Yule’s edition. The large buffet is his bracketed detail. The table’s height and the distance to the cups also appear in the body of the account [@yc, 1.61].

[19] ~ Yule discusses the form Bacsi and compares Odoric’s account of a related feat. Polo gives the scale of the audience and calls on the hearer’s belief in similar feats at home [@yc, 1.61 nn. 10–11].

[20] ~ The catalogue of what to ask for and the intervention of the stewards come from the Book’s account of securing support for a feast. Yule discusses the terms for the people and ritual [@yc, 1.61 n. 12].

[21] ~ The lights, incense, meat and broth are details of the account. Polo uses the analogy of the Latin calendar of saints to explain the feast days [@yc, 1.61 n. 12].

[22] ~ Yule discusses the scale of religious houses and, with Cordier, the evidence of Chandu’s temples and the abbot’s stele. The Book gives the number of monks and the different rules about marriage [@yc, 1.61 nn. 13–14].

[23] ~ Yule and Cordier identify the Sensin with Daoists and discuss the diet and images. The reading of bran and the breadth of the description of their practices remain open [@yc, 1.61 nn. 15, 17–19].

[24] ✓ The Book uses a Latin Christian word for heretics to explain the difference. Yule identifies *Patarins* and comments on its European context [@yc, 1.61 n. 16].
