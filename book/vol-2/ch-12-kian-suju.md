# Chapter II-12
## Kian, Suju, and the Approach to Heaven’s City

> *“And I assure you this river flows so far and traverses so many countries and cities that in good sooth there pass and repass on its waters a great number of vessels, and more wealth and merchandize than on all the rivers and all the seas of Christendom put together!”*
> — Book II.lxxi (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

Marco Polo, having finished the western digression to Nanghin and Saianfu, returns to Yanju (Yangzhou) and goes on regularly toward the capital of Manzi. He says that when you leave the city of Yanju, after going fifteen miles south-east, you come to a city called Sinju (Yizheng), of no great size, but possessing a very great amount of shipping and trade. The people are Idolaters and subject to the Great Kaan, and use paper-money.

And you must know that this city stands on the greatest river in the world, the name of which is Kian (Yangzi). It is in some places ten miles wide, in others eight, in others six, and it is more than a hundred days’ journey in length from one end to the other. This it is that brings so much trade to the city we are speaking of; for on the waters of that river merchandize is perpetually coming and going, from and to the various parts of the world, enriching the city and bringing a great revenue to the Great Kaan. [1]

So the Book, having set Marco over Yanju for three years and broken Saianfu with Western timber, sets him again upon the water that divides Cathay’s inland provinces from the silk south. Sinju is not a capital. It is a port of salt and masts — I-ching on the Kiang some fifteen miles from Yangzhou, south-west rather than south-east by the compass, yet true enough to Polo’s loose rose of winds. The river itself needs no soft identification. It is the Great Kiang, already named *Kiansui* when the Book spoke of Sindafu’s waters; the Mongols called it a Sea; the Chinese said the Ocean has no shore and the Kiang no bottom. Polo, writing before the New World’s rivers were known to Latins, was entitled to call it the greatest on earth.

And I assure you, he narrates, this river flows so far and traverses so many countries and cities that in good sooth there pass and repass on its waters a great number of vessels, and more wealth and merchandize than on all the rivers and all the seas of Christendom put together! It seems indeed more like a Sea than a River. Messer Marco Polo said that he once beheld at that city fifteen thousand vessels at one time. And you may judge, if this city, of no great size, has such a number, how many must there be altogether, considering that on the banks of this river there are more than sixteen provinces and more than two hundred great cities, besides towns and villages, all possessing vessels?

Messer Marco Polo aforesaid tells us that he heard from the officer employed to collect the Great Kaan’s duties on this river that there passed up-stream two hundred thousand vessels in the year, without counting those that passed down. Indeed, as it has a course of such great length, and receives so many other navigable rivers, it is no wonder that the merchandize which is borne on it is of vast amount and value. And the article in largest quantity of all is salt, which is carried by this river and its branches to all the cities on their banks, and thence to the other cities in the interior. [2]

— Those tallies are arithmetic of wonder: fifteen thousand hulls in one roadstead; two hundred thousand ascending in a year by a customs officer’s word. Later travellers found the swarm of boats no overcharge in kind, if the specific numbers are rounded for the ear. Anyway, next he mentions the craft of the river.

The vessels which ply on this river are decked. They have but one mast, but they are of great burthen, for I can assure you they carry — reckoning by our weight — from four thousand up to twelve thousand cantars each. Now we will quit this matter and tell of another city called Caiju. But first a point the Book had nearly forgotten. You must know that the vessels on this river, in going up-stream, have to be tracked, for the current is so strong that they could not make head in any other manner. Now the tow-line, which is some three hundred paces in length, is made of nothing but cane. ’Tis in this way: they have those great canes of which the Book told before that they are some fifteen paces in length; these they take and split from end to end into many slender strips, and then they twist these strips together so as to make a rope of any length they please. And the ropes so made are stronger than if they were made of hemp.

There are at many places on this river hills and rocky eminences on which the idol-monasteries and other edifices are built; and you find on its shores a constant succession of villages and inhabited places. [3]

So the craft of the Kiang: decked single-masters of four to twelve thousand cantars — five hundred tons and more at the upper end, a burden later salt-junks and rice-boats would still justify; bamboo hawsers twisted from the giant canes already praised in Tebet and Caindu; rock islets crowned with Fo’s houses — the Little Orphan, Silver Island, and the Golden Isle that Caiju will face. Ramusio will add horses to the tracking-teams; the Geographic line keeps men and cane — and that is the better reading.

Caiju (Guazhou), according to the book, is a small city towards the south-east. The people are subject to the Great Kaan and have paper-money. It stands upon the river before mentioned. At this place are collected great quantities of corn and rice to be transported to the great city of Cambaluc (Khanbaliq / Beijing) for the use of the Kaan’s Court; for the grain for the Court all comes from this part of the country. You must understand that the Emperor hath caused a water-communication to be made from this city to Cambaluc, in the shape of a wide and deep channel dug between stream and stream, between lake and lake, forming as it were a great river on which large vessels can ply. And thus there is a communication all the way from this city of Caiju to Cambaluc; so that great vessels with their loads can go the whole way. A land road also exists, for the earth dug from those channels has been thrown up so as to form an embanked road on either side.

Just opposite to the city of Caiju, in the middle of the River, there stands a rocky island on which there is an idol-monastery containing some two hundred idolatrous friars, and a vast number of idols. And this Abbey holds supremacy over a number of other idol-monasteries, just like an archbishop’s see among Christians.

Now we will leave this and cross the river, and tell of a city called Chinghianfu. [4]

So Caiju is the canal’s southern hinge on the Kiang — Kwa-chau, the Island of Gourds, insulated by river and cut; opposite Golden Island’s archbishopric of Fo; the granary mouth from which Kiang-nan rice climbs north by sluice and embankment to feed Cambaluc. Rashīd would describe the same great ditch, stone-revetted, with machinery to hoist vessels past the locks, and a paved high road of forty days lined with shops. Polo’s account of how the dug earth became the road is exact — one of the stronger institutional notices in the Manzi corridor.

Chinghianfu (Zhenjiang), he says, is a city of Manzi. The people are Idolaters and subject to the Great Kaan, and have paper-money, and live by handicrafts and trade. They have plenty of silk, from which they make sundry kinds of stuffs of silk and gold. There are great and wealthy merchants in the place; plenty of game is to be had, and of all kinds of victual.

There are in this city two churches of Nestorian Christians which were established in the year of our Lord 1278; and the Book will tell you how that happened. You see, in the year just named, the Great Kaan sent a Baron of his whose name was Mar Sarghis, a Nestorian Christian, to be governor of this city for three years. And during the three years that he abode there he caused these two Christian churches to be built, and since then there they are. But before his time there was no church, neither were there any Christians. [5]

— Mar Sarghis — *Dominus Sergius* — is a name known on the Si-ngan stele and among Armenian and Syrian clergy; Chinese local memory kept a *Sie-li-ki-sze* who built the temple Ta-hing-kuo-sze in the quarter Kia-t’ao around 1281, commemorated by a Confucian schoolteacher’s inscription that traces the *Ye-li-k’o-wen* faith to Samarcand. The churches are corroborable in type; the exact dual churches and 1278 year rhyme closely with the 1281 monastery notice. His three-year tenure also rhymes with the three years claimed for Marco at Yanju — a parallel worth noticing without forcing identity of office. Anyway, next he mentions Chinginju.

Leaving the city of Chinghianfu and travelling three days south-east through a constant succession of busy and thriving towns and villages, you arrive at the great and noble city of Chinginju (Changzhou). The people are Idolaters, use paper-money, and are subject to the Great Kaan. They live by trade and handicrafts, and they have plenty of silk. They have also abundance of game, and of all manner of victuals, for it is a most productive territory.

Now the Book must tell of an evil deed that was done, once upon a time, by the people of this city, and how dearly they paid for it.

You see, at the time of the conquest of the great province of Manzi, when Bayan was in command, he sent a company of his troops, consisting of a people called Alans, who are Christians, to take this city. They took it accordingly, and when they had made their way in, they lighted upon some good wine. Of this they drank until they were all drunk, and then they lay down and slept like so many swine. So when night fell, the townspeople, seeing that they were all dead-drunk, fell upon them and slew them all; not a man escaped.

And when Bayan heard that the townspeople had thus treacherously slain his men, he sent another Admiral of his with a great force, and stormed the city, and put the whole of the inhabitants to the sword; not a man of them escaped death. And thus the whole population of that city was exterminated.

Now we will go on, and tell of another city called Suju. [6]

— The Alans are the *Aas* / *Asu* of Asian report: Caucasus pastoral peoples long known to Byzantium and to the Khans of Sarai; Christians in Marco’s and Rashīd’s telling, though Ibn Baṭṭūṭa would find Aas who were Muslims; suppliers of officers to Mongol armies and, a generation after Polo, of ministers who wrote to Pope Benedict XII for a new archbishop at Cambaluc. That Bayan should send a Christian Alan corps into Manzi is ethnography the Yuan world makes probable. Chinese annals do not keep the wine-and-slaughter tale in Polo’s shape; they do keep Chang-chau taken, lost, retaken, and put to the sword under Bayan’s order after desperate resistance — provocation enough, Gaubil thought, to explain cruelty in a captain otherwise reputed humane. The drunken-treachery etiology is book tradition; the storm and slaughter have a Chinese kernel. Anyway, next he mentions Suju.

Suju (Suzhou), according to the book, is a very great and noble city. The people are Idolaters, subjects of the Great Kaan, and have paper-money. They possess silk in great quantities, from which they make gold brocade and other stuffs, and they live by their manufactures and trade.

The city is passing great, and has a circuit of some sixty miles; it hath merchants of great wealth and an incalculable number of people. Indeed, if the men of this city and of the rest of Manzi had but the spirit of soldiers they would conquer the world; but they are no soldiers at all, only accomplished traders and most skilful craftsmen. There are also in this city many philosophers and leeches, diligent students of nature.

And you must know that in this city there are six thousand bridges, all of stone, and so lofty that a galley, or even two galleys at once, could pass underneath one of them.

In the mountains belonging to this city, rhubarb and ginger grow in great abundance; insomuch that you may get some forty pounds of excellent fresh ginger for a Venice groat. And the city has sixteen other great trading cities under its rule. The name of the city, Suju, signifies in our tongue, “Earth,” and that of another near it, of which we shall speak presently, called Kinsay, signifies “Heaven”; and these names are given because of the great splendour of the two cities. [7]

— Two soft places need flagging. Rhubarb near Su-chau is a botanical error: *Rheum palmatum* is a Tangut and Upper Yangzi product, exported via Hankau and further west; Bretschneider already denied Kiang-su rhubarb, and the confusion may be with Suh-chau in Gansu, a famous rhubarb mart. Ginger is less clear — Hanbury doubted local mountain production, though later Shanghai returns soften the ginger half. As for Earth and Heaven: Polo was ignorant of Chinese; the folk etymology is a foreign misunderstanding of the proverb *Shang yeu t’ien t’ang, Hia yeu Su Hang* — “Above there is heaven’s hall; below there are Su and Hang.” Odoric and Wassáf keep Heaven for Hangzhou without needing Su-chau’s Earth. Bridge counts are formulaic marvel numbers; cut-stone spans of fifty and sixty feet are real in this water-country; six thousand is the Book’s way of saying they cannot be counted. [8] Anyway, the approach closes with three more towns.

Now let us quit Suju, and go on to another which is called Vuju, one day’s journey distant; it is a great and fine city, rife with trade and manufactures. But as there is nothing more to say of it we shall go on and tell of another great and noble city called Vughin. The people are Idolaters, and so forth, and possess much silk and other merchandize, and they are expert traders and craftsmen. Let us now quit Vughin and tell of another city called Changan, a great and rich place. The people are Idolaters, and so forth, and they live by trade and manufactures. They make great quantities of sendal of different kinds, and they have much game in the neighbourhood. There is however nothing more to say about the place, so we shall now proceed. [9]

Thus Suju — Su-chau of the silk and the tea districts, the Paris of later China — closes the approach: a circuit of sixty miles; philosophers and physicians; six thousand stone bridges; cheap ginger and a claim of mountain rhubarb; sixteen cities under its rule; and the folk pairing of Earth’s city with Heaven’s Kinsay. Vuju, Vughin, and Changan — Hu-chau, Wu-kiang, and Kiahing in the likelier readings — are the last silk towns before the capital. The next chapter opens Kinsay.
