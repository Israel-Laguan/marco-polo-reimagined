# Chapter II-12
## Kian, Suju, and the Approach to Heaven’s City

> *“And I assure you this river flows so far and traverses so many countries and cities that in good sooth there pass and repass on its waters a great number of vessels, and more wealth and merchandize than on all the rivers and all the seas of Christendom put together!”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 71 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

Follow the brine barges fifteen miles out from Yanju (Yangzhou) and you reach the water’s edge at Sinju (Yizheng). Here muddy side creeks open suddenly onto the greatest waterway of the eastern world [1]. Polo sets this river port south-east of Yanju, though on a modern map it sits south-west. That slight compass slip vanishes the instant you smell tung-oil varnish, raw split pine planks, wet hempen cordage, and white brine crusted across the quays [2]. Stacked hemp sacks of salt rise head-high beside cedar tally sheds. Sinju boasts no governor’s yamen or lofty stone ramparts. Yet its timber landing stages shout and creak from grey dawn until oil lanterns sway over dark water. Townsfolk settle tavern bills in black mulberry paper notes, obey the Great Kaan’s harbour masters, and earn their whole bread from the tireless brown current rolling past their piled wharves [3].

See [the map of the canal spine, II-10](ch-10-manzi-sung.md#map-of-the-canal-spine).

For here sweeps the Kian (Yangzi), which Polo crossed far inland as the Kiansui at Sindafu (Chengdu) and now hails proudly as the widest river on earth [4]. He gauges its main stream at six, eight, or even ten miles broad. From wild western snowfields down to the yellow eastern surf, its sweeping channel runs beyond a hundred days’ march. Standing at low ebb on the crowded wharf, you look out across a murky horizon that feels far more like a freshwater ocean than any inland river [5]. Marco vows he once counted fifteen thousand sea vessels and flat cargo lighters swinging together at anchor off Sinju’s creek alone [6]. Letting his eye sweep outward from those wet pine pilings, he maps more than sixteen wealthy provinces and two hundred great walled cities. Endless market towns, temple steps, and willow hamlets sit beside them, all passing winter wheat, white salt, silk, iron, and cut timber down one giant water highway [7].

A harbour customs collector leaning on the tally rails gave Marco an even bolder annual count: two hundred thousand craft working upstream in a single twelve-month. That staggering tally even skipped every hull drifting back down toward open salt water [8]. Feeding this colossal river traffic is a sprawling fan of navigable tributary streams reaching deep into distant hills. Those broad feeders gather silk, hides, ginger, and white porcelain from half a continent onto the trunk river, with bagged white salt leading every bulk cargo weeks inland from the sea [9]. A Venetian merchant’s son could never watch fifteen thousand crescent sterns rock on the ebb without computing port dues inside his head. Even if that dockside clerk inflated his tally over evening rice wine, the crush of working hulls left Venice and Genoa looking quiet as village millponds.

Step down onto the tarred quay and inspect the wooden craft built for that yellow race. Most are broad, flat-bottomed cargo junks framed from tight cypress planks. Skippers caulk their seams tight, smear every hull in pale tung oil, raise high painted stern castles, and step a single towering mast to catch the east wind. Merchants rate each hull at four thousand to twelve thousand *cantars* burden, deep enough to stow hills of dried fruit, wheat, and white salt [10]. Pulling such heavy hulls upstream against a four-knot current takes far more than square canvas. Boatmen split fifteen-pace green bamboo canes into narrow supple strips and plait them into three-hundred-pace tracking cables tougher than spun hemp [11]. On the flooded clay towpath, teams of ten or twelve strong horses lean into the collar beside bare-shouldered human trackers. They haul past steep granite river islands crowned with red-eaved idol monasteries and tidy fishing hamlets [12].

Watch a deeply laden salt junk groan foot by foot upriver past those cliff-top temple roofs. Along the muddy embankment, trackers throw their weight forward into linen slings beside the harnessed horses. Wet straw sandals stamp in step to a steersman’s drum while yellow water boils furiously round the blunt square stem. Stand beside that towpath for a breath and feel the pull on those plaited cane cables: if a single strand snaps mid-current, five hundred tons of hull and brine spin sideways down the channel. No skipper measures a river’s power on dry parchment. He feels every hard league through the hum and shiver of that taut bamboo cable.

Downstream and slightly south-east lies Caiju (Guazhou), a compact river depot where mounds of pale wheat and winter rice out of Manzi (southern China) rest beneath dry matting roofs awaiting shipment north to Cambaluc (Beijing) [13]. Here Polo lays bare the hydraulic artery keeping northern courtiers fed. Cublay (Kublai) ordered wide, deep cuts dug between scattered lakes and streams. Heavy grain barges could now glide cleanly out of Caiju to the northern capital without braving winter typhoons along rocky outer coasts. Dark earth dug from those canal beds was heaped high along both flanks to form dry willow-planted causeways. Pack-mules, couriers, and iron-rimmed carts rolled smoothly dry-shod beside the water [14]. Independent Persian histories praise that same forty-day Grand Canal trunk, noting stone sluice-gates hauling hulls past level drops and paved courier roads hugging the bank [15].

Out in midstream opposite Caiju rises a steep rocky islet crowned by an ancient Buddhist abbey housing some two hundred monks and bright gilded shrines [16]. At dusk a ferryman drifting below wet stone stairs hears heavy bronze bells booming over quiet water between tiered eaves and willow trees. Polo compares the house’s authority over sister cloisters across the province to an archbishop’s see in Christendom. Golden Island stands out cleanly as the bright stone seamark guarding the canal mouth [17]. Ferry across that tight channel to the southern bank and you climb into Chinghianfu (Zhenjiang), a wealthy silk-and-gold port stocked with hill game, river carp, winter wheat, and bustling dyeworks [18].

Here Polo pauses beside two Nestorian Christian churches erected in 1278, naming the commissioner who founded both [19]. In that year, the Book asserts, the Great Kaan sent a Christian noble named Mar Sarghis to govern Chinghianfu for three full years. During his office he raised both sanctuaries in a port where Christians had never built an altar before [20]. Yet a local Chinese gazetteer muddies that neat foundation tale. Carved stone inscriptions credit Mar Sarghis with seven monasteries across the region, dating two on Chin-shan land to 1279 and the Ta-hsing-kuo house to 1281 [21]. Between twin churches recalled along the wharf and seven foundations chiselled into memorial steles, the Christian commissioner unmistakably left his mark across the delta—just never on one tidy calendar.

Three days’ march south-east through an unbroken ribbon of mulberry gardens, stone culverts, and bustling villages carries the traveller into Chinginju (Changzhou), another prosperous silk city—before the narrative turns stark [22]. During Bayan’s sweep across Manzi, Polo relates, the Mongol commander detailed a troop of Christian Alan horsemen to hold the city gates [23]. Once safely behind high brick walls, the thirsty Alans stove in merchants’ oak wine casks in dark tavern cellars and drank themselves helpless. They snored away like butchered swine until midnight curfew. Furious townsfolk crept out with sharp kitchen knives and cut the throat of every sleeping soldier [24]. Outraged at the ambush, Bayan launched another commander forward with overwhelming force. Siege crews battered down the ramparts and put every living soul inside to the sword [25].

Compare that cellar tragedy with Chinese histories, and the story compresses a far bloodier sequence. Mongol divisions first occupied Chinginju early in 1275, lost it two months later to a sudden Song counterattack, and finally saw Bayan storm the breached walls in fury after ferocious house-to-house resistance [26]. In camp retellings, two gruelling sieges and a stubborn defence collapsed into one dark moral lesson on drunken discipline and merciless reprisal.

Push on past Chinginju to Suju (Suzhou), and every civic scale leaps outward. Polo hands this lagoon metropolis a sixty-mile circuit, crowded streets past counting, wealthy silk guilds, diligent physicians and natural philosophers, and sixteen subject trading centres [27]. Then he drops a blunt soldier’s verdict. Had workmen of such brilliant handcraft only carried a warrior’s heart inside their quilted coats, they might have mastered half the earth. Instead they live quietly for silver balances, fine brocade looms, and polished guild craft [28].

Most of all, Marco delights in Suju’s six thousand cut-stone bridges, vaulted with white granite arches lofty enough for a sea galley—even two slim galleys oars-shipped abreast—to shoot safely beneath the keystone [29]. Six thousand plainly stretches the mason’s count for a water-laced city whose stepped granite humps still stunned European visitors centuries later [30]. Climb to the crown of one bright arch at sunrise. Feel grey sandstone steps hollowed smooth by generations of straw sandals, and watch slender silk boats slip cleanly below your boots while blue-clad water-porters trot steadily past your shoulder. Down below, canal barges glide nose to stern without touching an arch. Up on the roadway, sedan-chairs, bamboo baskets, and pack-ponies thread past one another without dropping a step. You see at a glance why Marco never forgot the stonework of Suju, even if six thousand spans stretched the mason’s tally.

Wander through Suju’s spice and herb squares before passing the southern watergate. Fresh ginger heaps fill wide cedar baskets beside dark medicinal roots. The Book announces that true medicinal rhubarb and fresh green ginger flourish across neighbouring hills—so richly that one Venetian groat buys forty crisp pounds of fresh ginger [31]. True medicinal rhubarb in fact rode south along mountain rivers from Tangut and western heights rather than crowding damp canal banks. Even fresh green ginger largely arrived by water in packed bamboo panniers [32]. Happier still is Polo’s pair of civic translations: he explains that *Suju* signifies “Earth” and the royal metropolis waiting ahead, Kinsay (Hangzhou), means “Heaven” [33]. Foreign traders had simply half-caught the ancient Chinese proverb praising Paradise above and Su-Hang below—turning an affectionate local compliment into a literal dictionary gloss [34].

Come down from Suju’s high granite steps for a breath and feel the road quicken toward the Qiantang basin. One crisp stage south sits Vuju (Huzhou)—carefully distinct from the southern hill city of that name waiting past Kinsay—followed by Vughin (Wujiang), loud with clacking shuttles and rising steam from dye vats. Beyond Vughin waits Changan (Jiaxing), a trim market town packed with glossy sendal and cheap game birds [35].

Marco refuses to wear out our boots listing every temple bell, guildhall, or watchtower down those final canal miles [36]. Watch instead as Changan’s weavers trim loose silk threads from crisp bolts of sendal before barring wooden shop shutters at dusk. Boatmen coil damp tow-ropes on stone steps while pack mules wait beside stable lanterns. Silk folders lay shimmering cloth inside oiled paper against damp autumn fog and strap fresh bales onto narrow wooden pack-frames for the high road. Listen to the iron hoops clink in the lantern light as the last mule strings form up beside the canal gate. Only three brisk marches ahead waits Kinsay itself, the City of Heaven, where a trader bringing northern silk to market will soon find pale granite arches spanning every canal and cargo lighters sliding smoothly past his door [37].

## Notes

[1] ✓ Sinju is Yizheng (Chên-chau / I-ching hsien) on the northern bank of the Yangzi, linked to Yangzhou by branch canals and serving as its primary salt-shipping port [@yc, 2.71 n. 1]. Admiral Collinson and Sir John Davis later remarked on the vast fleets of crescent-sterned salt junks anchored off the creek.

[2] ✗ Polo places Sinju fifteen miles south-east of Yanju, whereas Yizheng actually lies south-west of Yangzhou [@yc, 2.71 n. 1]. Yule notes that such compass slips recur when Polo resumes his main road after a western digression such as Nanghin and Saianfu.

[3] ~ Paper money and imperial customs obedience recur as Polo’s shorthand for towns fully integrated into Yuan fiscal control [@yc, 2.71]. The harbour activity fits Yizheng’s documented role in Lower Yangzi shipping.

[4] ✓ Polo’s Kian is the Great Kiang or Yangzi River, introduced upstream as Kiansui at Chengdu in Chapter II-6 [@yc, 2.44 n. 2; @yc, 2.71 n. 2]. Calling it the greatest river in the world held true for Eurasian geography before European contact with the Americas.

[5] ~ Widths of six, eight, or ten miles overstate the main channel except near the widening tidal estuary or during peak summer floods [@yc, 2.71 n. 2]. By contrast, over a hundred days’ length from headwaters to sea is a fair medieval travel estimate.

[6] ~ Counting fifteen thousand vessels at Sinju at one time is a vivid impression of crowded anchorage rather than a harbour register [@yc, 2.71 n. 3]. Later observers such as Barrow confirmed immense river traffic along the Yangzi, even without matching Polo’s exact totals.

[7] ✓ Sixteen provinces and two hundred major cities express the vast commercial catchment drained by the Yangzi and its tributaries [@yc, 2.71]. See [Map A12, the canal spine](ch-10-manzi-sung.md#map-of-the-canal-spine).

[8] ✗ An annual customs count of two hundred thousand vessels sailing upstream alone cannot serve as an audited tax ledger [@yc, 2.71]. Yet attributing the figure to a Kaan’s revenue collector shows how Polo gathered commercial claims on the docks.

[9] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Ramusio’s 1559 text expands the Sinju passage with navigable tributaries and identifies salt as the river’s chief cargo carried into the interior [@yc, 2.71]. That salt role agrees with Yizheng’s historical function, even though the wording belongs to Ramusio’s layer.

[10] ~ Single-masted decked river junks of 4,000 to 12,000 *cantars* correspond to roughly 200 to over 500 tons burden [@yc, 2.71 n. 4]. Admiral Collinson recorded tall 90-foot masts and 30-foot sterns on Yizheng salt junks, supporting heavy capacity while leaving Polo’s upper ceiling rounded.

[11] ✓ Splitting long green bamboo canes into narrow strips and twisting them into tracking hawsers about three hundred paces long was standard Yangzi boatmen’s practice [@yc, 2.71 n. 5]. The short clause noting the canes were split into slender strips comes from Ramusio’s phrasing in Yule.

[12] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Tracking junks with teams of ten or twelve horses appears in Ramusio alone and finds no confirmation among the later China travellers Yule consulted [@yc, 2.71 n. 5]. By contrast, the Ramusio passage describing rocky river islands topped with monasteries matches Little Orphan, Silver, and Golden Islands [@yc, 2.71 n. 6].

[13] ✓ Caiju is firmly identified as Guazhou (Kwa-chau), standing right at the Grand Canal’s southern junction with the Yangzi directly opposite Golden Island [@yc, 2.72 n. 1]. Van Braam later counted over fifty huge rice barges passing there in a single day.

[14] ✓ Digging channels to link lakes and throwing up excavated earth into embanked towpaths accurately describes Cublay’s Grand Canal extension toward Cambaluc [@yc, 2.72 n. 2]. Chinese records note grain shipments north from 1283 onward, with the main northern section completed in 1292.

[15] ✓ Rashīd al-Dīn’s Cathay survey describes the same forty-day canal corridor, adding sluices where vessels were hauled over locks and paved roads along the bank [@yc, 2.72 n. 2]. His independent Persian notice corroborates the grain-transport system without proving every detail of Polo’s phrasing.

[16] ✓ Golden Island (Jinshan, near Zhenjiang) held a famous Buddhist abbey founded in the third or fourth century, though the name *Jinshan* was popularized later under the Kangxi emperor [@yc, 2.72 n. 3]. By the 1860s river silt had joined the former island to the southern shore.

[17] ~ Comparing the abbey’s authority over sister monasteries to a Christian archbishop’s see is Polo’s cultural translation for European listeners [@yc, 2.72]. Two hundred resident monks is a plausible round count for a major imperial monastery.

[18] ✓ Chinghianfu is Zhenjiang (Chin-kiang fu), standing where the Grand Canal meets the Yangzi’s tidal estuary [@yc, 2.73 n. 1]. Its silk-and-gold textiles and rich provisions match its prominence as a river-guard prefecture.

[19] ~ Polo places two Nestorian churches in Chinghianfu built in 1278 and credits Mar Sarghis (*Dominus Sergius*) with a three-year governorship [@yc, 2.73 n. 1]. Nestorian presence under the Yuan is securely attested across Jiangsu and Zhejiang, though Polo’s chronology and numbers stand apart from local records.

[20] ? No independent source establishes Polo’s claim that Christians were wholly absent from Zhenjiang before Mar Sarghis arrived in 1278 [@yc, 2.73]. Local records document the governor's monastery foundations rather than the earlier history of the Christian community there [@moule1915christians].

[21] ? The translated Zhenjiang local gazetteer attributes seven monasteries in China to Mar Sarghis, dating two on Chin-shan land to 1279 and Ta-hsing-kuo to 1281 [@moule1915christians, pp. 630–645, 651; @yc, 2.73 n. 1]. Those records show inconsistencies and cannot be merged with Polo’s two-church notice or collapsed into 1279 alone.

[22] ✓ Chinginju is Changzhou (Chang-chau), three days south-east along the canal road from Zhenjiang [@yc, 2.74 n. 1]. Its silk industry and dense agricultural hinterland align with Yuan-era Jiangnan geography.

[23] ✓ Christian Alans (*Asu* / *Aas*) from the northern Caucasus served prominently in Yuan armies, as attested in Chinese annals, Rashīd al-Dīn, Carpini, and Rubruck [@yc, 2.74 n. 2]. Placing an Alan detachment under Bayan’s command fits documented Mongol practice.

[24] ? The story that Chinginju’s townspeople massacred sleeping, wine-soaked Alan soldiers has no corroboration in Chinese chronicles [@yc, 2.74 n. 3]. It reads like a camp rumour explaining why an otherwise restrained commander ordered total slaughter.

[25] ✓ Chinese sources confirm that Bayan stormed Changzhou and put its inhabitants to the sword after meeting desperate resistance [@yc, 2.74 n. 3]. Pauthier’s Chinese extracts recount grim siege brutalities far beyond Polo’s brief notice.

[26] ✓ According to Chinese annals cited by Gaubil, Changzhou first fell in April 1275, was retaken by Song loyalists two months later, and was finally stormed by Bayan himself later that year [@yc, 2.74 n. 3]. Polo compresses two captures and a stubborn defence into one treacherous night.

[27] ✓ Suju is Suzhou (Su-chau), the great silk and craft metropolis of Jiangnan [@yc, 2.75 n. 1]. A sixty-mile wall circuit and sixteen subject towns reflect its urban stature on a rhetorical scale.

[28] ~ Calling Suzhou’s citizens pure merchants and naturalists rather than soldiers echoes the Book’s wider stereotype of Manzi civilians [@yc, 2.75]. The city’s silk guilds and scholars were indeed celebrated across China.

[29] ✗ Six thousand stone bridges lofty enough for two galleys abreast are poetic exaggerations [@yc, 2.75 n. 2]. Yet a 1247 marble city plan of Suzhou and later surveys show a dense canal grid crossed by hundreds of lofty cut-stone arched bridges.

[30] ✓ European and missionary observers from Van Braam to Du Bose confirmed Suzhou’s distinctive arched stone canal bridges spanning ten to fifteen feet of water [@yc, 2.75 n. 2]. The architectural impression is real even if six thousand is rhetorical.

[31] ✗ Rhubarb (*Rheum palmatum*) did not grow in Suzhou’s hills [@yc, 2.75 n. 3]. Bretschneider and Yule note it came from Tangut and the upper Yangzi via Hankow, suggesting either a confusion between Suzhou in Jiangsu and Sukchur (Suzhou in Gansu) or a mistake between a transshipment market and a growing district [@yc, 2.75 n. 3].

[32] ? Forty pounds of fresh ginger for a Venetian groat reflects busy delta spice markets rather than confirmed local cultivation around Suzhou [@yc, 2.75 n. 3]. Hanbury doubted ginger grew there in quantity, though later Shanghai customs returns recorded exports passing through the port.

[33] ✗ *Suju* and *Kinsay* do not literally translate as “Earth” and “Heaven” in Chinese [@yc, 2.75 n. 4]. Kinsay renders *xingzai* (“temporary imperial residence”), while Suzhou derives from ancient regional names.

[34] ✓ Yule traces Polo’s false etymology to a foreign misunderstanding of the famous Chinese saying, *Shang you tiantang, xia you Su-Hang* (“Above is paradise; below are Su[zhou] and Hang[zhou]”) [@yc, 2.75 n. 4; @yc, 2.76]. Odoric likewise calls *Cansay* the “City of Heaven” [@odoric1913travels, ch. 32].

[35] ? The Geographic Text alone names all three towns between Suju and Kinsay: Vuju (usually Huzhou / Hu-chau, distinct from Jinhua in Chapter II-14), Vughin (Wujiang), and Changan (Jiaxing) [@yc, 2.75 n. 5]. Pelliot [-@pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, pp. 872–874] tentatively shifts the names along the canal route, though the editors note his emendations lack manuscript support.

[36] ✓ Polo’s brisk compression keeps the narrative moving along the final canal stages rather than stalling over repeated inventories [@yc, 2.75]. The recurring formula—trade, crafts, paper money, and subject towns—marks the dense urban ribbon leading into Kinsay.

[37] ✓ Changan’s reputation for sendal silk and plentiful game closes the canal approach three days short of Kinsay [@yc, 2.75–2.76]. That textile thread carries directly into the opening markets of Chapter II-13.
