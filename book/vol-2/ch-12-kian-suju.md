# Chapter II-12
## Kian, Suju, and the Approach to Heaven’s City

> *“And I assure you this river flows so far and traverses so many countries and cities that in good sooth there pass and repass on its waters a great number of vessels, and more wealth and merchandize than on all the rivers and all the seas of Christendom put together!”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 71 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

The river carries Polo south from Yanju (Yangzhou) toward the capital of Manzi. After his account of Nanghin (Kaifeng) and Saianfu (Xiangyang), he returns to Yanju and follows the route to Sinju (Yizheng), a smaller place whose wealth comes from ships and trade. Its people use paper money and answer to the Great Kaan. Polo puts Sinju about fifteen miles south-east of Yanju, though the modern sites lie to its south-west. The mismatch does not erase the port's place in the story: he is moving from a major administrative centre to a smaller river town whose importance comes from the cargo moving past it.

See [the map of the canal spine, II-10](ch-10-manzi-sung.md#map-of-the-canal-spine).

Sinju stands on the Kian (Yangzi), which Polo measures in widths of six, eight, or ten miles and in a length of more than a hundred days’ travel. The river drains a vast stretch of the country, and traffic brings money into the port and revenue to the Kaan. Polo had already called the upper river Kiansui in his account of Sindafu (Chengdu); here it becomes the great commercial road between the inland provinces and the coast. Its scale is part of the point: the river links places too numerous to list, and a modest town can prosper because it sits on that moving route. Polo treats the waterway not as scenery around the journey but as its own system of exchange.

The port also serves the salt trade of Yanju, whose supplies can travel by water to other cities. The river's changing width and long course are given as broad measures rather than a survey, but Polo's picture of its economic reach is precise in one respect: the river brings trade within the Kaan's fiscal system. Sinju matters because it can collect, transfer, and send onward goods from a far larger region than the town itself. [1]

At Sinju, he recalls seeing fifteen thousand vessels together. The number is part of the chapter’s scale, not a harbour register: he imagines the fleets serving more than sixteen provinces and two hundred major cities, besides smaller towns along the river. A customs officer, Polo reports, counted two hundred thousand vessels moving upstream in one year, not including those going down. It is an account of traffic seen from two angles: the crowded port and the annual count claimed by a man who collected duties. The traffic is believable; the exact totals are not. The distinction matters, since the chapter's abundance is credible even where its arithmetic is not. [2]

Ramusio's account adds details about the river's reach and its salt trade. The river, it says, takes in navigable tributaries and carries goods of great value; salt is the most abundant cargo, moving from the river towns into the interior. In the older account, the salt detail is absent, while the officer's striking annual total remains a report heard at Sinju. This later layer turns the river from a measure of busy shipping into a description of a supply network: one commodity travels outward from the port and branches inland with the waterways.

The boats are decked, single-masted craft, and Polo gives their capacity as four to twelve thousand cantars. The upper figure is enormous, but later descriptions of river junks also give them great carrying power. Upstream, crews haul the vessels against the current on long towlines made by splitting tall canes into narrow strands and twisting them together. Ramusio's version adds horse teams to the trackers. These boats are built for the river's force and for the weight of the trade it bears; the towline is a practical answer to a current sail alone cannot master. Ramusio's account also places monasteries and other buildings on the island heights and describes villages lining the river. The named Little Orphan, Silver, and Golden Islands lie along this stretch of the Kiang. [3]

Polo gives the towline a length of about three hundred paces and says the cane strips make a rope stronger than hemp. The detail is small beside the thousands of vessels and the vast cargoes, but it brings the river's work into view: boats do not simply drift with the current, and moving them upriver takes both material and labour. The island monasteries stand above the same working waterway, in sight of the vessels they overlook.

Caiju (Guazhou) is a small port to the south-east, where people use paper money and grain and rice gather before their northbound journey to Cambaluc (Khanbaliq, now Beijing). Polo describes a broad waterway linking streams and lakes, wide enough for cargo vessels to travel between Caiju and the capital. The excavated earth forms raised banks beside it, doubling as a road in wet weather. The canal's purpose is plain in his account: food from the south can reach the northern court by water, and the same route supports travel overland beside the channel. The river and canal work together, carrying provisions onward from the port rather than leaving them at the water's edge. A later account by Rashid al-Din describes the same canal system, including locks and a paved route; that comparison supports the broad picture, not every detail of Polo’s account.

Rashid al-Din's account gives the canal a still longer reach, connecting the capital with the southern cities and ports. It describes vessels being lifted past sluices and a road running beside the bank, with settlements along the way. These details show how the waterway belonged to a larger transport corridor, not simply a ditch between two places. Polo's image of spoil piled into roads catches the practical union of water and land travel, even if his brief description leaves out the engineering needed to keep large boats moving through the locks.

Across the river from Caiju, Golden Island holds a Buddhist monastery with about two hundred monks and many images. Polo compares its authority over other monasteries to that of a Christian archbishop. The image is a local landmark as well as an account of religious rank: an island monastery visible from the river becomes a way to orient the traveller at the canal's meeting with the Kiang. From there the route crosses to Chinghianfu (Zhenjiang), a city under the Kaan whose people use paper money and make their living through trade and crafts. It has silk woven with gold, wealthy merchants, and abundant food and game. The city's crafts and provisions make it more than a waypoint; it is another centre drawing value from both land traffic and the river. [4]

Polo links two Nestorian churches in Chinghianfu to Mar Sarghis (Mar Sargis), whom he describes as the Kaan’s governor there for three years from 1278. He says the churches were built during that term and that no Christian community had preceded them. His account places a Christian official inside the administration of a city otherwise described through its merchants and crafts. A local inscription, translated by Moule [-@moule1915christians] and Giles, instead attributes seven monasteries in China to Mar Sarghis: two on Chin-shan land are dated 1279, while Ta-hsing-kuo is dated 1281. The source preserves a distinct local memory and its dates, not a simple confirmation of Polo's account. Those are separate records, not evidence that all seven were founded in 1279 or that Polo’s two churches and the later list are the same institution. [5]

Three days’ travel through busy settlements brings Polo to Chinginju (Changzhou), a prosperous silk town whose people use paper money and are subject to the Kaan. Trade, crafts, game, and plentiful food mark the city's ordinary life before the narrative turns to violence. During the conquest of Manzi, Polo says, Alan soldiers serving under Bayan entered the city, found wine, and drank until they could no longer defend themselves. The townspeople killed them during the night. Bayan responded with another attack, and the city was taken with orders to kill its inhabitants. In Polo's telling, one slaughter follows another, each made the cause of the next. The city then disappears back into the chain of places that lead south.

— Alan service in Mongol armies is well attested, but the wine episode belongs to Polo's account. Chinese histories describe Chinginju being taken, recovered, and finally stormed by Bayan; they also report the killing of its inhabitants. The records place the final assault after an earlier capture and a counterattack, leaving a historical sequence behind Polo's shorter tale. [6]

Suju (Suzhou) is another great city under the Kaan, where people use paper money and silk is made into gold brocade and other fine cloth. Polo gives it a circuit of sixty miles, a vast population, and wealthy merchants; the measures belong to the book’s grand scale. He also calls the people accomplished traders and makers, and describes scholars and physicians studying nature. The city has six thousand stone bridges, another great number that should not be read literally. Stone bridges were plentiful in the region, and the description shifts from cargo and government to an urban landscape: workshops make luxury cloth, wealthy merchants serve a broad market, and waterways are crossed again and again on stone. He also credits the city with sixteen dependent trading towns, extending its influence beyond the walls into a wider commercial district.

The six thousand bridges are an extravagant count, but not an invented landscape: later observers found many cut-stone bridges in the region, some with arches high enough for large boats. The number turns a common feature into a marvel, just as the city's circuit enlarges its fame. Polo's description of the people as traders rather than soldiers is a broad judgement, not a census of character. Suju is prosperous, skilled, and devoted to making and selling goods. Its scale makes it a fitting threshold to Kinsay (Hangzhou), which the following chapter will present at still greater length.

The Book pairs the names of Suju and Kinsay as “Earth” and “Heaven.” Yule treats this as a foreign misunderstanding of a Chinese saying that praises the two cities as earthly paradises, not a literal translation. Polo also says the nearby mountains grow rhubarb and ginger. The rhubarb claim is mistaken: the plant came from farther west. He says a Venetian groat could buy forty pounds of fresh ginger, but whether it grew nearby or arrived through trade is uncertain. A commodity may pass through a market without growing in its neighbourhood; this chapter's account of circulation is stronger than every explanation of origin. [7] [8]

Beyond Suju, Polo gives brief notices of Vuju (Huzhou), Vughin (Wujiang), and Changan (Jiaxing). Each is a busy manufacturing town; silk and other fabrics recur, while Changan is also noted for game. Their descriptions repeat the pattern established on the river: productive land, skilled work, and trade. Sendal, a fine silk fabric, marks Changan's place in the textile economy. Polo does not pause to inventory each town's gates or markets. Instead, he keeps the reader moving through a settled landscape where one centre follows another, until the route reaches Kinsay. The identifications of these stops are not equally certain, and the sequence need not map a straight line on a modern road. [9]
