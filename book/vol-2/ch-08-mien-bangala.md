# Chapter II-8
## Mien and Bangala: Elephants, Battle, Towers of Gold and Silver

> *“But I was forgetting to tell you of a famous battle that was fought in the kingdom of Vochan in the Province of Zardandan, and that ought not to be omitted from our Book.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 51 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

Vochan (Baoshan, in western Yunnan) is the setting for a battle Marco Polo calls too important to leave out. It lies in Zardandan (western Yunnan, the Gold-Teeth country), on the frontier between the Great Kaan’s holdings and the kingdom of Mien (Bagan, in modern Myanmar). Polo places the event in 1272, when the Kaan sent troops into Carajan (Yunnan, around Dali and Kunming) and Vochan to protect the frontier from raiders. His force arrived before the Kaan appointed his grandson Sentemur (Esen-Temür), son of a deceased son, to rule there. [1]

See [the map of the southwest circuit, II-7](ch-07-tebet-carajan.md#map-of-the-southwest-circuit).

Polo dates the clash to 1272; the Chinese chronicle places an elephant battle in 1277, during a campaign in the Vochan region. Those accounts may describe the same clash, but neither the site nor the correspondence is settled. A second Yuan expedition against Mien followed later that year, with a different commander. The distinctions matter: the numbers in Polo’s story cannot simply be merged with every figure in the Chinese account. [2]

The king Polo calls ruler of Mien and Bangala (Bengal) had not yet submitted to the Kaan. On hearing that Mongol troops had reached Vochan, he resolved to wipe them out and make the Kaan think twice before sending another army west. His preparations were formidable in Polo’s telling: two thousand elephants, each carrying a timber fighting tower for twelve to sixteen armed men, plus sixty thousand horsemen and foot soldiers. The king marched to within three days of the Yuan camp and paused to rest his army. Those figures are much larger than the numbers in the Chinese chronicle. [3]

In this force, the elephants are not an ornament to the king’s army. Each bears a fighting platform, built to hold a dozen or more armed men above the field. The king’s horse and foot soldiers accompany the elephant force. The account makes the elephants the king’s answer to the Mongol cavalry: their size and towers should drive the horses away before the foot soldiers come within reach. The numbers are Polo’s, and their scale makes the scene more imposing than the Chinese account does.

The figures on each side must stay with their own engagement. Polo’s sixty thousand and the chronicle’s forty to fifty thousand both refer to the Mien force; the chronicle’s roughly seven hundred are Yuan troops in the earlier elephant fight. The more than 3,840 commanded by Nescradin belong to the separate October expedition, not the smaller force at the elephant engagement.

The Yuan chronicle assigns the third-month elephant engagement to Hudu and companions, not to the commander Polo names. It records Nasr al-Din on a separate expedition in the tenth month. The resemblance between the first engagement and Polo’s story is worth noting, but it does not establish that the reports concern the same battle or the same place.

Polo calls the Mongol captain Nescradin (Nasr al-Din) and gives him twelve thousand mounted men. Nescradin’s troops took position on the Plain of Vochan, with a thick wood nearby. He had reason to be uneasy: his cavalry faced an army many times its size, led by a king who had prepared for this frontier campaign. But the nearby trees gave the Mongols a place to tether their mounts if the elephants frightened them. In Polo’s account, the captain’s careful use of that terrain turns the disadvantage into an opening. [4]

The two armies arranged themselves across the plain. After resting, the Mien king resumed his march and brought the full force into the open ground. As his troops closed to about a mile, he stationed armed fighters in the elephant towers and brought his horse and foot soldiers forward in order. The Mongols advanced too, but their horses balked at the sight of the elephants, swerving aside instead of meeting them. The Mien kept moving. If the Mongol line could not ride forward, it risked being overrun. [5]

For a moment, the same cavalry that had brought the Mongols to the plain could not move forward. The king’s army continued its advance while the Mongols tried to turn their horses back toward the elephants. Polo gives Nescradin a quick answer rather than a new formation: dismount, tie the horses among the trees, and fight with bows. The Mongols were skilled archers; on foot they shot at the advancing elephants and the men in their towers. Mien archers answered, but the Book gives the Mongols the advantage in weapons and skill. The animals took many arrows, and most were wounded or killed along with many of the men they carried. They turned and ran. In the wood they crashed their towers against tree trunks, splintering the timber and throwing their crews into confusion. The stampede was loud enough, Polo says, to drown out any other sound. The charge that had frightened the Mongol horses now broke the Mien advance.

The Mongols remounted and charged the disordered army. The fight then became hand-to-hand combat, with swords and maces. Polo gives the larger Mien force no advantage in numbers once the lines had met: it could not stand against the Mongol cavalry, which he presents as more seasoned for war. The fighting continued until midday. The Mien troops broke and fled, and the Mongols pursued them, cutting down many. Their chase did not last long; they turned back toward the wood to deal with the elephants, now running without control. They felled trees to block the animals’ escape. Even then, Polo says, Mongol soldiers needed captured Mien handlers, who knew how to manage the beasts. They took more than two hundred elephants, a capture the story makes the beginning of the Kaan’s elephant herds. Chinese annals record a 1277 elephant engagement and a rout after arrows sent the animals fleeing; Burmese annals omit that first clash. The match with Polo’s scene, its location and the commanders remain unsettled, as does the claim that this was the first time the Kaan kept elephants. [6]

Beyond the fighting, the road drops for two and a half days. A market serves the scattered communities along the descent three times each week. There, traders bring silver to exchange for gold at a rate of five weights of silver for one of gold. The gold is plentiful, but its owners live in remote country and refuse to show outsiders where they come from. They will not let a visitor follow them home, so the route ends at the market. Merchants who carry silver in can profit from the exchange without learning the source of the metal; the trade depends on that boundary between the accessible market and hidden settlements. [7]

At the end of the descent, Polo enters Mien by way of fifteen days of empty forest. Travelers must carry their own provisions, since there are no villages along the way and nothing to buy. He names elephants, wild beasts and unicorns—rhinoceroses, as he later realizes in Java—among the animals in the woods. The road’s isolation gives way, after those fifteen days, to a large city called Amien, the capital of Mien. Its people speak a distinct language, are described as idolaters, and are subjects of the Great Kaan. The southern province is described as close to India, but Polo’s route through it is not settled: the proposed destinations include Pagán, Tagaung and a descent from Nan-tien toward the Salween. The competing routes leave the capital’s identification uncertain, even as the Book presents the journey as a continuous passage from Vochan. [8]

The city’s towers are the feature Polo lingers over. A former king, he relates, ordered two stone towers built by his tomb before he died, one plated with gold and the other with silver. Each stood about ten paces high. Their rounded tops carried rows of gilded or silvered bells that chimed when the wind moved through them. Polo presents the metal as thick enough to make each tower look solid, and says the pair shone in the sun, visible from far away. The story gives the king’s purpose as preserving his memory and securing the good of his soul. In the Book, the towers are not merely treasure: their height, bells and gleam make them a public memorial, visible to travelers approaching the city. Ramusio’s version adds that the tomb itself was partly plated in gold and partly in silver. The gilded stupas at Mien’s capital, with their bell-topped *hti* canopies, offer a plausible visual parallel, but they do not confirm Polo’s account of twin funerary towers or finger-thick plating. [9]

After the memorial, the tale shifts to an unlikely conquest. Polo says the Kaan sent court musicians and jugglers to Mien, with a captain and armed support, and that they conquered the country. The performers did not replace the soldiers: a body of men-at-arms accompanied them, and a captain led the force. When they found the towers, they asked whether they should strip away the wealth. The Kaan forbade it, because the dead king had dedicated the structures to his memory and soul; the story adds that Mongols avoided disturbing anything belonging to the dead. It reads as hearsay or court jest, not a reliable account of the campaign. The later invasion belongs to another episode: a prince named Singtur led the 1282–83 expedition, and King Narathihapade fled the capital in 1283–84 after its temples were dismantled for fortifications. Mien’s animals, Polo adds, include wild oxen and many kinds of deer and other game. [10]

South lies Bangala. Polo places it near India and says that, as late as 1290, while Marco was still at the Kaan’s court, the Kaan’s armies had gone there to conquer it but had not done so. The province has its own language, and its people are described as idolaters. Polo singles out eunuchs: he claims that the Kaan’s barons obtained them from Bangala.

He also reports oxen as tall as elephants, though smaller, and describes a diet of meat, milk and rice. Cotton and spices—spikenard, galingale, ginger and sugar among them—support trade. Indian merchants come for eunuchs and for enslaved men and women captured in wars with neighboring peoples; traders carry them away for sale. The geographical placement and conquest claim remain uncertain. There is no confirmed Yuan conquest of the region itself, and the double title “king of Mien and Bangala” may reflect a Burmese royal claim or a confusion with a frontier kingdom. [11]

The portrait is brief, but it ties together the goods and people moving through the province. Cotton and spices travel as merchandise; eunuchs and enslaved people are also bought and carried away. Polo describes the people as suppliers to distant markets, while his placement of Bangala beside Mien leaves open which political territory he has in mind. The book’s claim of an unfinished conquest is therefore distinct from the campaigns against Mien.

Polo’s account now turns to Caugigu (Kiang Hung, now Jinghong).
