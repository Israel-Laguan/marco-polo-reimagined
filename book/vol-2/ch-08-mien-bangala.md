# Chapter II-8
## Mien and Bangala: Elephants, Battle, Towers of Gold and Silver

> *“But I was forgetting to tell you of a famous battle that was fought in the kingdom of Vochan in the Province of Zardandan, and that ought not to be omitted from our Book.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 51 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

Five weights of silver will buy one of gold in Zardandan (western Yunnan). If you have brought a mule-load of silver, that is reason enough to risk the road to Vochan (Baoshan). The court has its own reasons for wanting to hold that road. Cublay (Kublai) Kaan sends soldiers into Vochan and Carajan (Yunnan), in Polo’s account, to protect the frontier from raids [1]. The next army to appear on the plain has towers strapped to its elephants.

Polo puts the clash in 1272 [2]. He puts the posting of the garrison before the appointment of Sentemur (Esen-Temür), the son of one of Cublay’s dead sons [3]. The Yuan chronicle gives us a later date for an encounter with elephants. Keep that difference in mind when the armies meet. We can follow this battle right down to the tree where a bowman ties his pony, and still be unsure which battle we’ve fought.

From Mien (the kingdom of Bagan, in present-day Myanmar) comes a king who owes the Kaan no allegiance. The Book gives him Bangala (usually read as Bengal, though its position is disputed) as well [4]. Word of the garrison reaches him and he marches to drive it off. Three days short of Vochan he calls a halt to rest his army. A cavalryman can get off and loosen a girth there. Consider what it takes just to look after the elephants. A tower has to ride securely when the animal changes pace, and the men in it have to be able to use their weapons without getting in one another’s way. Read the muster as a merchant for a moment. The timber and the harness for one of these towers would be an order worth having. Multiply it by the number Polo is about to give us.

Two thousand of them, by Polo’s count, have wooden fighting towers. He reckons twelve to sixteen armed men to a tower [5]. Add sixty thousand horse and foot to the picture [6]. Even at a mile’s distance a line of these animals with men standing over their heads would give a rider something to think about. In the Book, it’s the horse that makes up its mind first.

Across the plain, Polo puts twelve thousand Yuan horsemen under Nescradin (Nasr al-Din) [7]. There’s a thick wood on their side. The captain has them take the field alongside it. At a distance of about a mile the Mien king posts his fighters in their towers and brings up the rest of his army. Both lines advance until the Mongol horses get a good look at what’s coming. They shy and turn away. The elephants keep coming [8].

Down from the saddle. As Polo tells it, Nescradin has the men lead their horses into the wood and tie them to the trees. They come back with bows. You can see the advantage of those towers while the horses are balking. Now the men at ground level have a remarkable expanse of animal to shoot at. Shafts come back from the Mien side, but the Book gives the Mongols the better of that exchange. Men fall from the towers and wounded elephants turn and run. Some bolt into the very wood where the Mongols have left their horses. The towers catch on the trees. Harness gives way, timber comes down, and the king’s great advantage goes to pieces in the branches [9]. The men who came to the fight on horseback have won their chance to fight on horseback again.

— Stand for a moment at the edge of that wood. From the plain you could see the elephants turn. From here you’d hear the towers break before you knew where the animals were going.

Back in the saddle, the Mongols close with the men who followed the elephants onto the field. This is the part of the tale that could fill a hall. Swords and maces come out, riders fall among the foot soldiers, and the noise in the Book could drown a clap of thunder. Polo gives the king the larger army and the Mongols the steadier hand. By midday the king’s line breaks. The cavalry gives chase, then turns back to the wood [10].

Catching an elephant is a different business from shooting one. The men fell trees to block the animals’ way out. They still need the king’s captured handlers to get near them and bring them under control [11]. Polo puts the haul at more than two hundred and makes it the beginning of the Kaan’s great elephant herds [12]. Before the fighting the towers had made the animals look like fortifications. Look at them now as something a commander can keep and put to work, if he can feed them and find people who know what they are doing. There’s a conqueror’s inventory for you. The most useful captives on that field may have been the people who knew how to get the elephants home.

Set Polo’s account alongside the Yuan chronicle and the arrows are still there. In the third month of 1277, the chronicle puts about seven hundred Yuan soldiers under Hudu and his fellow commanders against some forty to fifty thousand opponents with about eight hundred elephants [13]. In the tenth month it sends Nescradin on a separate expedition, with rather more than 3,840 men [14]. In Yule’s rendering of the Chinese account, the riders use sharp bamboo stakes to prick the animals in the confusion [15]. The chronicle gives us good reason to take the elephant fighting seriously. Where we ought to put the wood, and whether the two tellings describe the same clash, are questions the arrows can’t settle.

---

After all that timber comes down, I’m glad to get back to a set of scales. Polo gives us two and a half days’ riding, all downhill, and a market held three times a week [16]. Polo gives no name for the market. It is a big enough gathering to draw the people of the district, and regular enough that a trader can set out knowing when he is likely to find a buyer. People bring gold down from places they don’t disclose. A man with silver can buy a weight of fine gold for five of his own metal [17]. That price pays for a good deal of rough road. You might weigh out a second purchase if you’d packed enough silver. It won’t buy an invitation to follow the sellers home. For a trader who can make his profit where the scales stand, it hardly needs to.

Beyond the descent, the Book sends us through forest for fifteen days. Put whatever you’ll need on a pack animal before you go. There’s no village on Polo’s road where you can buy so much as a handful of rice. There are elephants and what the Book calls unicorns [18]. After those days on provisions, the traveller comes out at Amien, which Polo calls the capital of Mien [19]. The story gives it a language of its own, a religion Polo calls idolatry and subjects of the Great Kaan. Where that particular road comes out is another matter. The forest has fewer landmarks than a map-maker would like. If the city is indeed the old capital, a great many miles of country have gone missing from the route. Yule looked for a place farther up the river where the days would fit. The name and the length of the journey pull him in different directions.

Listen before you look. In Polo’s account a former king has ordered two stone towers to be built beside his tomb. Each rises a good ten paces, with a rounded top and bells all the way round [20]. On one the bells are gilded, on the other silvered. I like the care the teller takes over that last touch. The tops could have rung just as well with plain bells, but nobody would have walked away with a story of two colours the way they do here. In a wind the tops ring, and in the sun the towers can be seen a long way off. The Book gives each tower metal a finger thick, enough to make one look solid gold and the other solid silver [21]. Ramusio’s text carries the gleam across to the tomb itself, with a share of each metal [22]. The dead king has made himself a memorial that can catch the eye of somebody who never knew him, and catch the ear of somebody who has yet to see it.

— A finger’s breadth, dear reader. Put yours against the side of an imaginary tower. A goldsmith would have done quite enough to make it shine before using anything like that much metal.

Who gets the gold when the city falls? Here the Book sends in the Kaan’s court entertainers, with a captain and a company of men-at-arms [23]. It gives the musicians and jugglers the credit for taking the country, which would make a splendid boast at the next feast. The campaign Yule reconstructs from the chronicles has a prince in command and the king of Mien fleeing his capital [24].

In Polo’s tale, the conquerors send back to ask what to do about the towers. Cublay orders them to leave the metal where it is, on the memorial its builder intended for the good of his soul. The Book puts this down to a Mongol reluctance to lay hands on anything that belongs to the dead [25]. So the bells can go on ringing. In the country beyond the city, Polo puts the elephants back among wild oxen and several sorts of deer [26].

---

South again, in the Book’s telling, lies Bangala. Polo puts it near India, gives it a separate language and says the people practise what he calls idolatry. He has Yuan armies on their way to take it, with the job still unfinished in 1290, when he places himself at court [27]. We have to leave the army somewhere off the page. The country Polo describes draws its own traffic.

Look at what comes to market. Cotton makes a trade of its own. There is ginger, galingale, spikenard and sugar. Rice, milk and meat feed the people who produce them [28]. The oxen are as tall as elephants, Polo would have us believe, though he concedes a difference in bulk [29]. After Vochan an elephant is quite a yardstick to apply to a cow.

Some of the people leaving these markets have had no choice in their journey. Polo has Indian merchants buying men and women taken in wars with neighbouring peoples. He also reports a trade in eunuchs and makes Bangala the source from which the Kaan’s barons supply their households [30]. A load of cotton can tell a merchant what a province grows. The people carried away with the goods tell us something about where the profits came from.

Gold travels on from one report to the next. East of Bangala, Polo puts a king in Caugigu (perhaps Kiang Hung, now Jinghong) who sends tribute to the Kaan. Take a look at the people of that country before you count his treasure. Their dragons are drawn on skin.

## Notes

[1] ~ Polo describes the troops as a frontier garrison [@yc, 2.51]. Vochan is conventionally associated with Baoshan, while the battlefield itself remains disputed. See [Map A11, the southwest circuit](ch-07-tebet-carajan.md#map-of-the-southwest-circuit).

[2] ? The Book dates its engagement to 1272. Yule suspects a corrupt numeral and compares the 1277 elephant fight, whose resemblance cannot establish that the two accounts describe the same event [@yc, 2.51 n. 1; @yc, 2.52 n. 3; @yuanshi1370, juan 210].

[3] ~ Sentemur is Cublay’s grandson in this passage. The timing of the appointment relative to the fighting depends on the campaign chronology and cannot be recovered from Polo’s date alone [@yc, 2.51; @yc, 2.48 n. 1].

[4] ? Yule suspects some confusion between Bengal and Pegu. Phayre, quoted by Yule, argues that the Burmese kings might have claimed the title on dynastic grounds [@yc, 2.51 n. 2].

[5] ~ War elephants are attested in the Yuan account, while the timber towers and their crews of twelve to sixteen belong to Polo’s telling. Yule compares widely divergent crew sizes in accounts of war elephants and doubts whether so many fighters would have room to use their weapons [@yuanshi1370, juan 210; @yc, 2.51 n. 3].

[6] ? Sixty thousand is Polo’s tally for the Mien horse and foot. The Yuan figures for a possibly related engagement are forty to fifty thousand on that side [@yc, 2.51; @yuanshi1370, juan 210].

[7] ? Nescradin was the son of Sayyid Ajall and a Yuan commander in Carajan. The chronicle places him on the later expedition of 1277, under a different date and with a smaller force than Polo’s account gives him [@yc, 2.52 n. 1; @yuanshi1370, juan 210].

[8] ~ The refusal of the horses makes the dismount necessary in Polo’s account. Cordier’s comparison with Herodotus concerns animals balking in battle, while the mechanism here is the horses’ fright at elephants [@yc, 2.52 n. 2].

[9] ~ The Yuan account likewise sends the elephants flying under arrows. The tethered horses and splintered towers belong to Polo’s staging, and Baber’s attempt to locate the battlefield from its terrain is one proposal, reproduced in Yule [@yuanshi1370, juan 210; @yc, 2.52 n. 3].

[10] ~ The Yuan chronicle records a rout, while the sword-and-mace battle, its din and the midday pursuit are Polo’s. Yule notes that the Burmese annals as reported to him omit the earlier engagement, and that Phayre doubted it [@yuanshi1370, juan 210; @yc, 2.52 n. 3].

[11] ~ The trees felled across the animals’ path and the reliance on captured handlers are details in the Book’s account. By this stage it gives the prisoners’ expertise more weight than the cavalry’s skill [@yc, 2.52].

[12] ~ The total of more than two hundred is Polo’s figure. His account makes this the beginning of the Kaan keeping elephants in numbers, a neat origin story that the campaign evidence cannot date [@yc, 2.52].

[13] ✓ The *Yuan shi* records about seven hundred soldiers under Hudu, Xinjuri and Tuoluotuohai in the third-month fight. It gives their opponents about eight hundred elephants and forty to fifty thousand men, all on the Mien side [@yuanshi1370, juan 210].

[14] ✓ The chronicle assigns more than 3,840 men to Nasr al-Din’s expedition in the tenth month. It describes submissions at fortified settlements and a withdrawal owing to the heat [@yuanshi1370, juan 210].

[15] ~ In Yule’s relay of the Chinese account, the elephants flee into a place set with sharp bamboo stakes, which their riders seize and use to prod them. In Polo’s story the flight ends among trees, with the towers smashed [@yc, 2.52 n. 3; @yuanshi1370, juan 210].

[16] ~ The length of the descent and the three market days are Polo’s. Yule cites Sladen on a five-day market cycle in the Shan country, a parallel for regular markets with a different interval [@yc, 2.53 n. 1].

[17] ~ The five-to-one rate agrees with Polo’s figure for Zardandan. Yule’s note reports gold from districts north of Bhamo, while the account of the merchants’ bargain and the guarded route to the sellers’ homes is Polo’s [@yc, 2.50; @yc, 2.53 n. 1].

[18] ~ When the Book meets the animals it calls unicorns on Java Minor, it gives them the hide, horn and mud-loving habits of rhinoceroses. That description offers a reading of the name in the forest report [@yc, 2.53; @yc, 3.9].

[19] ? Yule’s notes compare routes via Nan-tien and towards the Salween, debate a starting point near Bhamo and discuss Pagán and Tagaung as possible termini. The fifteen-day itinerary on its own cannot decide between them [@yc, 2.52 n. 3; @yc, 2.53 n. 2; @yc, 2.54 n. 1].

[20] ~ The bell-hung *hti* canopies and gilding of Burmese stupas offer a visual parallel. Their likeness cannot identify these two towers or establish their funerary purpose [@yc, 2.54 n. 2].

[21] ✗ Finger-thick plating on a pair of towers is probably an exaggeration. Yule discusses the Mangala Chaitya finished in 1274 and the gold images associated with it as possible material for the story [@yc, 2.54 n. 2].

[22] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Yule brackets the plating on the tomb itself and the placement of a tower at either end, while the two towers and their metal coverings are already in the base account [@yc, 2.54].

[23] ✗ The entertainers’ conquest reads like court hearsay. Polo himself supplies the troupe with a captain, armed men and an army’s equipment [@yc, 2.54].

[24] ~ Yule identifies the prince of the 1282–83 campaign as probably Singtur and compares Chinese accounts of the advance with Burmese reports of Narathihapate’s flight in 1283–84. The latter reports describe temples dismantled to fortify the capital [@yc, 2.54 n. 2].

[25] ~ The order to spare the towers belongs to the conquest tale. Polo explains it by a reluctance to disturb the property of the dead, and the order itself has no independent confirmation here [@yc, 2.54].

[26] ~ Among the game in the Book’s account are deer and wild cattle. Yule reads the latter as gaur [@yc, 2.54 n. 3].

[27] ✗ Yule could find no dependable record of a Yuan attempt to take Bengal and doubted that Polo reached it from Carajan. Its position in the Book also sits awkwardly with Bengal’s place in India [@yc, 2.55 n. 1; @yc, 2.59 n. 6].

[28] ~ Polo names cotton as a mainstay of trade and lists the spices and food separately. The combination gives the report a recognisable commercial profile without fixing the position of Bangala [@yc, 2.55].

[29] ✗ Yule takes the comparison as a figure of speech. His note distinguishes accounts of the unusually tall wild buffalo from the domestic cattle Polo seems to mean [@yc, 2.55 n. 2].

[30] ~ Yule cites the *Āʾīn-i Akbarī* and Barbosa for the trade in Bangala and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa for the price of enslaved people. These comparisons support a traffic in people and leave the claim about supplying the Kaan’s barons with Polo [@yc, 2.55 n. 1].
