# Back Matter II-1
## Trivia and Disputes — Volume II

> *“And there are in it twelve thousand bridges of stone, for the most part so lofty that a great fleet could pass beneath them.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 76 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Volume II is where wonder and paperwork shake hands. This companion gathers short trivia the chapters do not tell and a shortlist of disputed claims flagged inline.

### Trivia worth keeping

**Cathay and Manzi are foreigner’s halves.** Polo’s north–south split mirrors how outsiders talked about conquered China, not a Yuan ministry label. Cathay points north to the old Jin and Mongol sphere; Manzi names the rich Song south, where Kinsay (Hangzhou) stands as the conquered prize.

**Achmath the bailo is not only Polo’s villain.** Chinese historiography remembers Achmath (Ahmad Fanakati) as a powerful, hated finance minister assassinated in 1282, while the `Po-lo` who spoke to Cublay (Kublai) afterward was the Mongol grandee Bolad, not Marco [1].

**Surviving tablets and money plates.** Late thirteenth-century iron-and-silver *paiza* passes inscribed in ’Phags-pa script and bronze Zhiyuan note-printing blocks survive in museum collections today—contemporary Yuan administrative objects, though none is Marco’s personal tablet [2].

**Pulisanghin became a modern nickname.** The stone span Polo praises at Pulisanghin (Lugou Bridge) later attracted the popular English nickname “Marco Polo Bridge,” a reception afterlife rather than anything Polo called it.

**Zayton is the door to Volume III.** Zayton (Quanzhou) closes Volume II’s China circuits and opens the maritime book toward Chipangu (Japan), with twenty-two Song–Yuan port, kiln, and religious sites still preserved across the city [3].

**Robes of one colour.** The feast-uniform passages sound like pageantry catalogue, yet they also reflect sumptuary and ceremonial discipline in a conquest court that needed visible hierarchy.

### Disputed claims — the Volume II shortlist

**Yanju (Yangzhou) governorship.** City identification firm; whether Marco held a three-year governorship, a temporary fiscal commission, or merely sojourned (*seingneurie* vs *séjourna*) has no Chinese record and divides the manuscripts; see II-11, Notes 10–16 [4].

**Saianfu (Xiangyang) and the mangonels.** The siege ended in March 1273, two years before the Polos reached Cublay’s court in 1275, and the *Yuan shi* credits the counterweight trebuchets to Muslim engineers sent from Persia; see II-11, Notes 21–37 [5].

**Kinsay’s numbers.** The former Song capital’s vast scale is real, whereas tallies like twelve thousand stone bridges and 160 *tomans* of hearths reflect rounding, unit confusion, or manuscript inflation; see II-13, Notes 2–3 and 37–42 [6].

**Paper money simplicity.** Yuan bark-paper currency (*chao*) is thoroughly attested, though Polo’s frictionless marvel picture passes over the inflation behind the five-for-one Zhiyuan devaluation of 1287; see II-5, Notes 13–18 [7].

**Coal “discovery.”** Polo did not discover coal; he accurately reported ordinary North China fuel to a Mediterranean audience that burned very little of it; see II-5, Notes 30–31 [8].

For the full ledger of hits, misses, and silences across all three volumes, see [Chapter III-12](../vol-3/ch-12-reliability-essay.md).

## Notes

[1] ✓ Achmath (Ahmad Fanakati) appears in the *Yuan shi* under “villainous ministers,” confirming his fiscal power and assassination in 1282 [@yc, 2.23 n. 5; @yuanshi1370, juan 205]. Parker’s correction, printed by Cordier, identifies the official `Po-lo` in the aftermath as Bolad rather than Marco [@ycadd, on 2.23; @ma2025chinese]; see [Chapter II-5, Notes 8–12](ch-05-ahmad-paper-coal.md#notes).

[2] ✓ The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a late thirteenth-century iron safe-conduct *paiza* with silver inlay in ’Phags-pa script [@metpaiza1993256], and the Chinese University of Hong Kong Art Museum holds bronze Zhiyuan printing blocks and seals for 200 and 500 *wen* [@cuhkzhiyuanblocks]. Neither object is Marco Polo’s personal possession.

[3] ✓ Zayton (Quanzhou) is firmly anchored by Yuan *shibo* maritime-trade ordinances and harbour archaeology [@yc, 2.82 n. 2]. In 2021 UNESCO inscribed twenty-two Song–Yuan administrative, religious, production, and transport sites across Quanzhou as a serial World Heritage property [@unesco2021quanzhoudecision]; see [Chapter II-14, Notes 26–28](ch-14-fuju-zayton.md#notes).

[4] ? The Yangzhou governorship claim divides both the manuscripts (*seingneurie* in F against *séjourna* in French B/C and Ramusio’s “in place of one of the said Barons”) and modern historians [@yc, 2.68 n. 3; @moule1938description, vol. 1, p. 316]. Larner [-@larner1999marco, ch. 3], de Rachewiltz [-@derachewiltz1997marco; -@derachewiltz2002wood], and Vogel [-@vogel2013marco, chs. 1, 5] reject a formal civil governorship while weighing a temporary court commission or residence; see [Chapter II-11, Notes 10–16](ch-11-yanju-saianfu.md#notes).

[5] ✗ The siege of Saianfu (Xiangyang and Fancheng) ended in spring 1273, two years before the Polos reached Cublay’s court about May 1275, and the *Yuan shi* names the Muslim engineers Alawating and Ysemain sent from Persia [@yc, 2.70 n. 5; @yuanshi1370, juan 203]. How the family’s name became attached to the counterweight trebuchets is examined in [Chapter II-11, Notes 21–37](ch-11-yanju-saianfu.md#notes).

[6] ~ Kinsay’s urban scale is corroborated by Song and Yuan records, whereas figures such as twelve thousand stone bridges and 1,600,000 houses reflect rounding or mismatched counting units [@yc, 2.76 nn. 2, 4; @mascherpa2024quinsai, p. 138; @vogel2013marco, ch. 6]. See [Chapter II-13, Notes 2–3 and 37–42](ch-13-kinsay.md#notes) for the comparison with Chinese gazetteers and revenue accounts.

[7] ✓ Yuan paper currency is thoroughly confirmed by surviving notes and administrative texts, while Polo’s frictionless picture passes over the inflation that led to the five-for-one Zhiyuan conversion of 1287 [@yc, 2.24 n. 1; @vogel2013marco, ch. 1; @guan2024yuan]. See [Chapter II-5, Notes 13–18](ch-05-ahmad-paper-coal.md#notes).

[8] ✓ Polo’s account of burning black stones dug from the mountains is a well-attested description of North China coal use [@yc, 2.30 n. 1; @golas1999mining]. Even Frances Wood [-@wood1995did, ch. 8] credits the accuracy of the coal passage; see [Chapter II-5, Notes 30–31](ch-05-ahmad-paper-coal.md#notes).
