# Back Matter II-1
## Trivia and Disputes — Volume II

> *“And there are in it twelve thousand bridges of stone, for the most part so lofty that a great fleet could pass beneath them.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 76 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Volume II is where wonder and paperwork shake hands. Readers remember paper money, burning stones, Kinsay’s bridges — and then the arguments begin. This companion collects trivia worth pocketing and the main disputes the chapters already mark with short asides. Use it after the narrative, not instead of it. [1]

### Trivia worth keeping

**Cathay and Manzi are foreigner’s halves.** Polo’s north/south split mirrors how outsiders talked about conquered China, not a Yuan ministry label. Cathay points north to the old Jin/Mongol sphere; Manzi names the rich Song south. Once you hear the pair, Book II’s geography clicks — and Kinsay’s elegiac splendour makes political sense as praise of a prize.

**Two capitals, one gravity.** Shangdu for summer; Dadu/Cambaluc for winter. Feast calendars and hunt chapters behave better if you let the court migrate. A static “palace chapter” reading misses the seasonal machine.

**Paper money shocked Europe more than China.** Yuan notes were ordinary imperial tools (and later ordinary imperial problems). For Latin readers they were nearly science fiction — hence Polo’s insistence that refusal was punished and that the Khan “bought” the world’s goods with mulberry bark.

**Coal as quiet proof.** The black-stones passage is small and stubborn. Mocked by some early readers, it now reads as one of the easier ethnographic hits: North China really did warm itself that way. It will not settle the whole travel debate by itself; it still earns its keep.

**Ahmad the bailo is not only Polo’s villain.** Chinese historiography remembers Ahmad Fanakati as a powerful, hated finance chief whose assassination in 1282 was a political earthquake. Polo’s chapter is vivid and partial; the man’s fall is not invented out of Venetian spite alone.

**Pulisanghin became a modern nickname.** The stone bridge Polo praises over the Sanggan/Yongding later attracted the popular name “Marco Polo Bridge” — a reception fact, not something Marco called it. Nicknames are afterlives.

**Zayton is the door to Volume III.** Quanzhou’s haven closes Volume II’s China circuits and opens the maritime book. Spices, ships of Manzi, and the Chipangu hearsay all lean on that coast.

**Book IV is weather, not a second journey.** The Great Turkey / Ponent wars chapter at the end of this volume is mostly reported imperial horizon — kept so the realm feels larger than Marco’s saddle.

**Robes of one colour.** The feast-uniform passages sound like pageantry catalogue, and they are — but they also hint at sumptuary and ceremonial discipline in a conquest court that needed visible hierarchy.

### Disputed claims — the Volume II shortlist

**Yanju (Yangzhou) governorship.** The city identification is firm. The claim that Marco governed Yangzhou for three years is one of the most contested autobiographical statements in the book. No tidy Chinese appointment record has convinced the whole field; defenders argue for some local fiscal or commission role lost in translation; sceptics call it inflation of a merchant-guest’s errands. This retelling keeps the claim as book tradition and flags it inline. [2]

**Saianfu (Xiangyang) and the mangonels.** Xiangyang’s siege is real and decisive in the Song war. That Niccolò and Maffeo (or their engineers) introduced the counterweight trebuchets that broke the stalemate is a famous Polo-family boast. Military historians note Muslim/Mongol engineers (often named in Chinese sources) as the better-attested technical agents. Visit versus report, and family credit versus court credit, remain open. [3]

**Kinsay’s numbers.** Houses, bridges, market crowds, bathhouses, pleasure boats — Hangzhou was genuinely one of the world’s great cities, and Song–Yuan urban density supports wonder. Exact figures in the book often look rounded, stacked, or manuscript-inflated. Believe the scale; audit the digits. Yule already hedged; modern urban historians hedge harder. [4]

**Paper money simplicity.** That notes circulated widely under Yuan authority is sound. That the system was as frictionless and universally beloved as Polo’s marvel tone suggests is softer. Currency crises, regional distrust, and later collapse stages appear more clearly in Chinese fiscal history than in *Il Milione*. [5]

**Coal “discovery.”** Polo did not discover coal. He reported it to an audience that used it little. The dispute, when it appears, is usually about European reception, not Chinese fact.

**Personal travel versus circuit report.** Sindafu, Kinsay, Zayton, and the bridge chapters feel closer to autopsy. Mien’s elephant battle, some highland ethnonyms (Caugigu, Anin, Coloman), and the Ponent wars read as intelligence and story. Mixed tags in the chapter heads are there on purpose. [6]

**Silence seeds that grow later.** Volume II still does not serve a Great Wall set-piece; it also does not write Chinese script samples or give a modern ethnographer’s account of language or tea. Those silences become ammunition in Volume III’s Wood debate and reliability essay — noted here only so Volume II readers are not blindsided. [7]

### How to use this back matter

When a feast description overwhelms you, enjoy it — then check whether the Note says ✓ institution or ~ number. When Yanju or Saianfu comes up at dinner, you now have the short form: city firm, personal credit contested. When Kinsay’s bridges multiply past belief, remember Jiangnan’s water-city geography and the medieval love of tall counts.

Volume III will trade canal marvels for monsoon logic, Chipangu gold, and Cocachin’s return fleet. Volume II’s arguments are mostly about service claims, fiscal wonder, and urban arithmetic inside a real Yuan world — which is already a better quarrel than “did any of this exist?”
