# Chapter II-3
## Feasts, Robes, and the Twelve Thousand Keshican

> *“A guard of twelve thousand serves the Kaan, taking its watch in turns.”* *(adapted)*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 12 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

At the palace, twelve thousand horsemen serve the Great Kaan as Keshican, a name Polo glosses as *“Knights devoted to their Lord.”* He presents the guard as a display of rank, not a force needed to frighten an enemy. Four captains divide the men into companies of three thousand. Each company spends three days and nights on duty at the palace, where its members also eat; then another company takes its place. One group is always on watch, and the rotation continues through all four companies before beginning again. The account makes the guard feel less like a permanent wall of men than a great household moving through a fixed timetable. Their watch is one part of the Kaan’s state, alongside the servants, the barons and the ceremonies that make his rank visible. [1]

— Palladius gives the active watch as about four hundred men, reduced to one hundred in 1330. The three-day change of guard is credible; Polo’s twelve thousand and four equal brigades look more like a formal, inherited count than a daily headcount. [2]

---

The guard’s discipline becomes part of the feast. At a great court gathering, the Kaan sits at the north end of the hall, raised above everyone else and facing south. His chief wife sits beside him on the left. Sons, nephews and other imperial relatives take places to his right, but at a lower level, so their heads are no higher than his feet. The barons sit lower still. Their wives and the other noblewomen have their own ordered ranks, with the imperial women nearest the throne. The wives of the Kaan’s sons, nephews and other kinsmen occupy one lower table to his right; the barons’ and knights’ ladies sit below them. Every place has been assigned by the Kaan’s order, and the rows of tables let him see the company from end to end. In this room, position is visible to everyone. [3]

Ramusio’s account adds that most soldiers and their officers do not sit at those tables. They eat on carpets in the hall, while more than forty thousand visitors and tribute-bearers gather outside, many bringing gifts or curiosities from distant lands. The crowd turns a court dinner into a public demonstration of the empire’s reach. The Kaan’s own table is only the centre of a much larger gathering, with rank visible inside and the empire’s visitors collected beyond the doors.

Ramusio’s version also places a carved, gilded buffet near the Kaan’s table. It is a square chest, about three paces on each side, decorated with animals. At its centre stands a large gold vessel for wine or a spiced drink; four smaller vessels sit at its corners.

Ramusio further specifies the size of those smaller containers: each holds about a firkin. The central vessel could hold roughly as much as a large wine cask, and the smaller ones served as a convenient measure for the spiced drink.

Ramusio’s text calls the gold pitchers on the buffet verniques. A pitcher stands between each pair of guests, with two handled gold cups beside it, and the ladies are served in the same way. Each pitcher is large enough to serve eight or ten people. Polo’s account makes their value and the court’s stores of gold and silver seem almost beyond belief. [4]

Ramusio’s version assigns barons to guide foreign guests to seats suited to their rank and make sure they receive food and drink. They move about the hall, checking what guests need and directing servants to bring wine, milk or meat. At the doors stand two exceptionally tall guards with staves. No one entering may step on the threshold: a violator might lose his clothes until he paid a fine, or receive blows instead. The rule is treated as protection against bad luck, and ushers explain it to foreigners unfamiliar with the custom. Leaving is treated less strictly, since a guest may have drunk too much to mind his footing. [5]

The Kaan’s attendants are themselves high-ranking barons. They cover their mouths and noses with silk-and-gold cloths so that neither breath nor odour reaches his food or drink. Music begins whenever he is about to drink. As he lifts his cup, the company kneels and bows; the ceremony is repeated at every drink. Polo passes quickly over the dishes rather than naming them, but says that the barons’ wives eat with the other ladies.

After dinner, the tables are cleared and performers enter. Jugglers and players show feats of skill before the Kaan and his guests, who laugh and enjoy the spectacle. The banquet is not over until the formal meal has given way to this lighter show. When the performance ends, the company returns to its quarters. [6]

---

The birthday feast gives the court another occasion to display its order. Polo dates the Kaan’s birthday to the twenty-eighth day of the September moon, and calls its celebration the greatest of the year apart from New Year. The Kaan wears a robe worked with beaten gold. Twelve thousand barons and knights appear in silk-and-gold robes of the same colour, though less costly, and each receives a gold belt from the ruler. Their clothing makes the assembly echo the Sovereign’s own appearance, while the finer work on his robe keeps the distinction between ruler and court. The costly suits are not reserved for a handful of favourites: in Polo’s account, the whole body of barons and knights is dressed for the occasion. Some suits, he claims, carry enough pearls and precious stones to be worth ten thousand bezants. [7]

The gifts are not limited to clothes. Across the Kaan’s dominions, people send presents according to their means and the required quotas, while others arrive hoping that a gift will help them obtain a position at court. Twelve barons are assigned to consider their requests and give each petitioner an answer. The birthday is at once a celebration and a day for seeking favour. People of different religions also pray for the Kaan’s health and long life, with singing, lamps and incense according to their own rites. The same wish is offered through distinct customs, each community keeping its own form of devotion. [8]

Polo links the birthday robes to a cycle of thirteen distributions each year, each in a different colour, with the Kaan and his barons wearing matching colours at each celebration. The birthday is therefore one occasion in a wider sequence of shared dress, not an isolated display. The number is uncertain: some accounts suggest three distributions, though Polo repeats thirteen in another chapter. [9]

---

The year’s other great gathering begins in February, at the New Year Polo calls the White Feast. On that day, everyone—men and women, young and old—is dressed in white, a colour believed to bring good fortune for the year ahead. The provinces and subject kingdoms present the Kaan with gold, silver, pearls, gems and fine textiles. People also exchange white gifts, embrace, kiss and wish one another happiness. Polo gives the number of white horses offered to the Kaan as more than one hundred thousand. These offerings are meant to secure abundance for the Kaan throughout the coming year.

Ramusio’s version adds a rule for the tribute: where a province can afford it, gifts come in groups of nine times nine. That means eighty-one horses, for example, or the same count of gold pieces, lengths of cloth or other goods. The scale of the recorded gifts is extravagant, but this count gives the tribute a clear pattern: not a single token from each district, but carefully measured sets of goods. [10]

Polo places a procession before the Kaan as part of the White Feast. He counts five thousand elephants, covered in rich cloth patterned with animals and birds. Each carries two chests filled, he says, with the court’s vessels and other equipment for the celebration. Camels follow, likewise dressed and loaded with supplies. The procession passes before the ruler as a display of abundance. The parade makes the feast’s preparations visible: the animals carry what the court needs, as well as the splendour meant for the occasion. The reported elephant count is enormous; the imperial court may have kept elephants for tribute and spectacle, but the scale is disputed. [11]

Before the feast tables are set, officials and dignitaries assemble in the great hall. Polo’s list ranges from princes, kings and nobles to astrologers, philosophers, physicians, falconers and other officers from the surrounding lands. Those unable to enter stand where the Kaan can see them. Rank determines the order: first the Kaan’s sons, nephews and other imperial relatives, then kings, dukes, marquesses, counts, barons, knights and the remaining officials. Everyone is placed before the ceremony starts.

A senior religious figure calls on the gathering to bow. The company prostrates itself four times toward the Kaan, as if he were a god, then approaches an altar bearing a vermilion tablet inscribed with his name and a gold incense burner. Each person offers incense and returns to his place. Gifts are presented next; after the Kaan has seen them, the tables are laid and the feast begins. The same performers return after dinner, and the guests then go back to their quarters. [12]

The Keshican appear in yet another set of robes at these festivals. Polo describes thirteen outfits for each of the twelve thousand men, one colour for each set, decorated with gems and pearls. All twelve thousand share the colour assigned to that feast, and there is an established order telling them which of the thirteen sets to wear. Each issue also includes a rich gold belt and a pair of boots described as Camut or Borgal, Polo’s terms for Rosia (Russia) leather, a kind of horsehide shagreen, embroidered with silver thread. The Kaan has matching colours for his own thirteen suits, but in finer materials. He dresses in the same colour as the guard, which Polo presents as a sign of fellowship between ruler and men.

In Polo’s arithmetic, the guard’s wardrobe alone amounts to 156,000 suits, not counting the belts or boots. The expense, he suggests, is part of the point: these gifts add grandeur to the festivals and bind the Keshican to the Kaan. At a glance, the court can see that the guard belongs to the same ceremony as the ruler, though his finer dress sets him apart. The figures and the claim that all twelve thousand were “barons” may be inflated, but the shared festival colours fit the Mongol custom described by other observers. A gift repeated across every turn of the calendar makes the Kaan’s generosity part of the guard’s daily identity. [13]

At the feast, Polo also places an unchained “lion” before the Kaan. The animal lies down as if it recognizes him as its master, and stays there without restraint. The story is extraordinary, though travellers sometimes used “lion” for a tiger; a trained big cat may lie behind the display. [14]

The court’s feasts turn guard duty, rank, tribute and dress into public ceremony; the next scene follows the Kaan from the hall into the hunt. [15]
