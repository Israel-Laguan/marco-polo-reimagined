# Chapter II-13
## Kinsay: Capital of Manzi

> *“And since we have got thither I will enter into particulars about its magnificence; and these are well worth the telling, for the city is beyond dispute the finest and the noblest in the world.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 76 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

Marco Polo narrates that when you have left the city of Changan (Jiaxing) and have travelled for three days through a splendid country, passing a number of towns and villages, you arrive at the most noble city of Kinsay (Hangzhou), a name which is as much as to say in our tongue “The City of Heaven,” as the Book told you before when it paired Earth’s Suju with this capital of splendour.

And since we have got thither, he says, the narrative will enter into particulars about its magnificence; and these are well worth the telling, for the city is beyond dispute the finest and the noblest in the world. In this the Book will speak according to the written statement which the Queen of this Realm sent to Bayan the conqueror of the country for transmission to the Great Kaan, in order that he might be aware of the surpassing grandeur of the city and might be moved to save it from destruction or injury. All the truth as it was set down in that document — so the Book avers — Messer Marco Polo at a later date was able to witness with his own eyes. [1]

First and foremost, then, the document stated the city of Kinsay to be so great that it hath an hundred miles of compass. And there are in it twelve thousand bridges of stone, for the most part so lofty that a great fleet could pass beneath them. And let no man marvel that there are so many bridges, for you see the whole city stands as it were in the water and surrounded by water, so that a great many bridges are required to give free passage about it. And though the bridges be so high the approaches are so well contrived that carts and horses do cross them.

The document aforesaid also went on to state that there were in this city twelve guilds of the different crafts, and that each guild had twelve thousand houses in the occupation of its workmen. Each of these houses contains at least twelve men, whilst some contain twenty and some forty — not that these are all masters, but inclusive of the journeymen who work under the masters. And yet all these craftsmen had full occupation, for many other cities of the kingdom are supplied from this city with what they require. The number and wealth of the merchants was so enormous that no man could form a just estimate thereof. Those masters of the crafts neither they nor their wives ever touch a piece of work with their own hands, but live as nicely and delicately as if they were kings and queens. The wives indeed are most dainty and angelical creatures. Moreover it was an ordinance laid down by the King that every man should follow his father’s business and no other, no matter if he possessed a hundred thousand bezants. [2]

— Circuit and bridge counts are the Book’s loudest arithmetic of wonder here. A hundred English miles will not stand; a hundred *li*, or the popular Western figure that Odoric and Wassáf also share in scale, fits Hangzhou’s Sung and early-Yuan greatness better. Twelve thousand bridges is formulaic — Odoric repeats it; Chinese *Fu* lists run in the hundreds — yet lofty cut-stone canal arches are real. Guilds of twelve thousand workshops apiece are marvel numbers; craft concentration and export role are sound. Anyway, next he mentions the water that makes the city famous still.

Inside the city there is a Lake which has a compass of some thirty miles: and all round it are erected beautiful palaces and mansions belonging to the nobles of the city, and many abbeys and churches of the Idolaters. In the middle of the Lake are two Islands, on each of which stands a rich, beautiful and spacious edifice, furnished as if fit for an Emperor. And when any citizen desired to hold a marriage feast or other entertainment, it used to be done at one of these palaces, where everything would be found ready to order — silver plate, trenchers, dishes, napkins and table-cloths. The King made this provision for the gratification of his people. Sometimes there would be at these palaces a hundred different parties; some holding a banquet, others celebrating a wedding; and yet all would find good accommodation, one party never in the way of another.

Thus Si-hu — the Western Lake — enters the Book: a ring of palaces and Fo’s houses; island halls stocked for weddings by Sung civic hospitality. Later travellers would still praise the gilt barges and flowering shores. Thirty miles shrinks toward thirty *li*; Chinese maps keep a third island Polo’s two omit. The houses of the city, meanwhile, are provided with lofty towers of stone in which articles of value are stored for fear of fire; for most of the houses themselves are of timber, and fires are very frequent. [3]

The people are Idolaters; and since they were conquered by the Great Kaan they use paper-money. Both men and women are fair and comely, and for the most part clothe themselves in silk, so vast is the supply of that material from the district and from imports. And you must know they eat every kind of flesh, even that of dogs and other unclean beasts, which nothing would induce a Christian to eat.

Since the Great Kaan occupied the city he has ordained that each of the twelve thousand bridges should be provided with a guard of ten men, in case of disturbance or treason. Each guard has a hollow instrument of wood and a metal basin, and a time-keeper; when one hour of the night is past the sentry strikes one on wood and basin, so that the whole quarter knows; at the second hour two strokes, and so on. From sunrise they count anew. Part of the watch patrols for unlawful lights or fires after hours — marking the door for morning punishment — and arrests night-walkers. If in the daytime they find any poor cripple unable to work, they take him to one of the hospitals, of which there are many, founded by the ancient kings and endowed with great revenues; or if he be capable of work they oblige him to take up some trade. If any house has caught fire they beat the wooden instrument, and watchmen flock from the other bridges — one or two thousand at the least — to extinguish it and save goods to the stone towers or by boat to the lake islands. No citizen dares leave his house at night, or come near the fire, save the owners and those watchmen.

Moreover, within the city there is an eminence on which stands a Tower, and at the top hangs a slab of wood. Whenever fire or alarm breaks out, a man with a mallet beats upon the slab, making a noise heard to a great distance.

The Kaan watches this city with especial diligence because it forms the head of all Manzi, and because he has an immense revenue from the duties on trade therein — such that no one would credit it on mere hearsay. [4]

All the streets of the city are paved with stone or brick, as indeed are all the highways throughout Manzi. Were it not for this pavement you could not travel after rain, for the country is very low and flat. But as the Great Kaan’s couriers could not gallop over pavement, the side of the road is left unpaved for their convenience. The main street is laid in two parallel ways of ten paces on either side, leaving a middle space of fine gravel under which vaulted drains convey rain into the canals; and thus the road is kept ever dry.

You must know also that the city of Kinsay has some three thousand baths, supplied by springs. They are hot baths, and the people take great delight in them, frequenting them several times a month, for they are very cleanly in their persons — the finest and largest baths in the world, large enough for a hundred persons to bathe together.

And the Ocean Sea comes within twenty-five miles of the city at a place called Ganfu (Kanp’u), where there is a town and an excellent haven, with a vast amount of shipping engaged in the traffic to and from India and other foreign parts. A great river flows from Kinsay to that sea-haven, by which vessels can come up to the city itself, and extends also further inland.

Know also that the Great Kaan hath distributed Manzi into nine parts, constituted into nine kingdoms. To each a king is appointed subordinate to the Great Kaan, who every year renders the accounts of his kingdom to the fiscal office at the capital. Kinsay is the seat of one of these kings, who rules over a hundred and forty great and wealthy cities. In the whole of Manzi there are more than twelve hundred great and wealthy cities, without counting towns and villages. And in each of those twelve hundred the Great Kaan has a garrison, the smallest mustering a thousand men, some ten, twenty, or thirty thousand — a total scarcely calculable. The troops are not all Tartars; many are from Cathay, and good soldiers too. Nor are they all cavalry; a very large proportion are foot-soldiers, according to each city’s need. All belong to the army of the Great Kaan. [5]

Thus the Geographic spine: silk and fire-towers; bridge-watches striking the hours; hospitals of the ancient kings; paved roads with gravel mid-lanes for the couriers’ gallop; three thousand spring-fed baths; Ganfu twenty-five miles seaward on the Ts’ien-t’ang estuary; nine subordinate “kingdoms” with Kinsay ruling a hundred and forty cities; dense garrisons, Cathayan as well as Tartar. Ramusio will claim colder baths for natives; the Geographic *estuves* fit Chinese hot-bath practice better.

The people write down the day and hour and the planet and sign of each child’s birth, so that every one knows his nativity. When any one intends a journey he goes to the astrologers; sometimes they say no, and the journey is put off. These astrologers are very skilful, and the people have great faith in them.

They burn the bodies of the dead. Friends and relations mourn in hempen garments, follow the corpse with instruments and idol hymns, and at the burning place put on the fire, with the corpse, parchment representations of horses, slaves, camels, armour, cloth of gold, and money, that the dead may have them alive in the next world, and that the music and the idols may welcome him there.

Furthermore there exists in this city the palace of the king who fled, him who was Emperor of Manzi — the greatest palace in the world. Its demesne hath a compass of ten miles, enclosed with lofty battlemented walls; inside are the finest gardens upon earth, fountains, and lakes full of fish. In the middle stands the palace itself: twenty great halls, one more spacious than the rest, all painted in gold with histories of beasts and birds, knights and dames; and besides these, a thousand large chambers painted in gold and divers colours.

Moreover there are a hundred and sixty *tomans* of fires — that is, of houses. The *toman* is ten thousand, so altogether one million six hundred thousand houses, among them many rich palaces. There is one church only, belonging to the Nestorian Christians.

Every burgess — indeed every description of person — writes over his door his own name, his wife’s, his children’s, his slaves’, and all the inmates’, and the number of animals he keeps. If any one dies the name is erased; if a child is born it is added. So the sovereign knows exactly the population of the city — and this throughout Manzi and Cathay. Every hosteler must register travellers’ names and the day and month of arrival and departure. And thus the sovereign knows who come and go. Certes this is a wise order and a provident. [6]

So far the older texts. What follows — markets and pepper, pleasure-barges and covered carriages, and the merchant’s guided tour of Facfur’s ruined pleasure-grounds — is Ramusio’s continuation, collected by Yule into a separate chapter so that the Geographic line and the Venetian enrichment may be told apart. The narrative flags it plainly and then tells it.

[Ramusio.] The city has on one side the fresh lake already spoken of, and on the other a very large river whose canals run through the quarters, carry away impurities into the Lake, and issue again toward the Ocean — a most excellent atmosphere. Streets and canals are so wide that carts and boats pass readily with supplies. On the far side a channel perhaps forty miles long, dug by the ancient kings for flood relief, also defends the city; the dug earth thrown inward forms a mound enclosing it.

Here are the ten principal markets — squares of half a mile to the side — fronted by the main street, forty paces wide, running straight from end to end of the city. At every four miles comes one of those great squares. Parallel behind the markets runs a large canal, and on its bank toward the squares stand great stone houses where merchants from India and foreign parts store their wares. In each square a market is held three days in the week, frequented by forty or fifty thousand persons, with every necessary of life: game and fowl in infinite quantity — for so many ducks and geese are bred on the Lake that for a Venice groat you may have a couple of geese and two couple of ducks — and shambles for calves, beeves, kids, and lambs, eaten by the rich and great. Vegetables and fruits abound; certain pears weigh as much as ten pounds, white and fragrant as a confection; peaches yellow and white in season. Neither grapes nor wine are produced there, but raisins and wine come from abroad; the natives prefer their rice-and-spice wine. Fish from the Ocean Sea comes daily up the river, and from the lake also, so fat and plentiful that what seems unsellable is cleared in a few hours. The markets are ringed with lofty houses and shops of crafts, spices, jewels, pearls, and cheap fresh rice-wine.

— One silence is honest to flag: Ramusio’s shambles list calves, beeves, kids, and lambs, but not pork — China’s staple meat. Odoric specifies swine at Cansay; Yule wondered whether court manners had been “Saracenized.” The omission is a dispute hook, not proof of invention. [7]

Certain streets are occupied by the women of the town — in such number that the Book dares not say what it is — splendidly attired and perfumed, so accomplished that strangers bewitched by them go home saying they have been to Kinsay, the City of Heaven, and desire only to return. Other streets hold Physicians and Astrologers. In each square two great palaces face one another, where officers decide merchants’ differences and see that the bridge-guards are at their posts.

To give an example of vast consumption: Messer Marco heard from one of the Great Kaan’s customs officers that the pepper introduced daily into Kinsay amounted to forty-three loads, each load equal to two hundred and twenty-three pounds.

The natives are peaceful, knowing nothing of arms; honest in trade and manufacture; neighbourly as one family; cordial to foreign merchants — yet they hate to see soldiers, and not least the Kaan’s garrisons, regarding them as the cause of having lost their native kings.

On the Lake are numbers of pleasure barges holding ten, fifteen, twenty or more, fifteen to twenty paces long, flat-bottomed, always furnished for a feast. The crew pole from the roof-deck — the Lake is not more than two paces deep — while the painted interior, with windows that open and shut, lets the party enjoy the prospect on both sides: the city’s whole length of palaces, temples, monasteries, and gardens sloping to the shore. After the day’s business, citizens take their pleasure here with the ladies of their families — or others less reputable — or drive about in long covered carriages fitted with curtains and cushions for six, to garden pavilions where they divert themselves until evening.

[Ramusio — palace of Facfur.] The Palace enclosure was divided into three parts: gilded and azure pavilions where King Facfur feasted his lords — ten thousand at table for ten or twelve days, all in silk and gold; cloister apartments and a corridor to the lake with a thousand young ladies in his service; and groves and preserves where he took his pleasure with those damsels alone. Thus he passed his life without knowing what arms meant — and so lost his dominion to the Great Kaan.

All this was told to Messer Marco by a very rich old merchant of Kinsay, once intimate with Facfur, who guided him over the ruin: outer pavilions still kept for the Kaan’s king; ladies’ courts only just traceable; garden wall fallen. [8]

Thus Ramusio’s Kinsay: markets, pepper, lake parties, and a merchant’s memory of Facfur’s gold halls. The pepper figure is wonder arithmetic — huge beside later Chinese spice habits; Moule softens it with street-cook evidence; it belongs with Zayton’s hundred-to-Alexandria claim in the next chapter. The guide’s romance is second-hand colour; Chinese sources keep a soft Sung court without needing every gilt detail.

Now the Book will tell of the great revenue which the Great Kaan draweth every year from Kinsay and its territory, forming a ninth part of all Manzi.

First there is the salt, which brings in a great revenue: every year, in round numbers, fourscore *tomans* of gold; and the *toman* is worth seventy thousand *saggi* of gold, so that the fourscore tomans amount to five millions and six hundred thousand *saggi*, each worth more than a gold florin or ducat — in sooth, a vast sum. This province adjoins the ocean, whose lagoons and salt marshes yield, when the summer sun dries the sea-water, salt enough for five of the other kingdoms of Manzi besides this one.

Next, the duties on merchandize and other matters. In this city and its dependencies they make great quantities of sugar — as in the other eight divisions — so that the Book believes the rest of the world together does not produce such a quantity, if what many have told be true; and sugar alone yields an enormous revenue. All spicery pays three and a third per cent. on the value; all merchandize likewise three and a third; but sea-borne goods from India and distant countries pay ten per cent. Rice-wine makes a great return, and coals; so do the twelve guilds with their twelve thousand stations apiece, for every article they make pays duty. Silk, produced in such abundance, pays ten per cent., and many other articles likewise.

And you must know that Messer Marco Polo was several times sent by the Great Kaan to inspect the customs and revenue from this ninth part of Manzi — so Pauthier’s line; the Geographic text says rather that he had several times heard the calculations made — and he found it to be, exclusive of salt, two hundred and ten *tomans* of gold, equivalent to fourteen million seven hundred thousand *saggi*; one of the most enormous revenues ever heard of. If the sovereign has such a revenue from one-ninth, judge what he must have from the whole. Yet this part is the greatest and most productive; and because of that revenue it is his favourite province, and he takes all the more care to watch it well and keep the people contented.

Now we will quit this city and speak of others. [9]

— The revenue figures are a metrological nightmare — *toman*, *saggio*, tael, *ting*, paper discounted as gold — and Vogel is useful here. Absolute gold equivalents will not stand; the fiscal enormity of Kiang-Ché salt and silk is the sound kernel. Whether Marco was sent to inspect (Pauthier) or only heard the count (Geographic) remains open. Enough that the arithmetic helped make a Venetian nickname. Anyway, the Book of Heaven’s city closes.
