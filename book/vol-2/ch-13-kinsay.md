# Chapter II-13
## Kinsay: Capital of Manzi

> *“And since we have got thither I will enter into particulars about its magnificence; and these are well worth the telling, for the city is beyond dispute the finest and the noblest in the world.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 76 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

At Changan (Jiaxing) they make sendal. Three days farther on, a man bringing a bolt of it to Kinsay (Hangzhou) has to find his buyer in a city threaded with water. There’s a boat moving between the houses. Look up from it and there’s a road over your head. The Book calls this the City of Heaven [1]. When you see what it takes to get a cart and a boat past each other all day, you may find the people on the ground deserve some of the credit.

The first account of the city comes, in Polo’s telling, from a written appeal. The queen of Manzi (southern China) sends it to Bayan for Cublay (Kublai) Kaan, hoping the conqueror will spare so fine a place. Polo says he later saw for himself what she described [2]. Imagine making the case to a ruler who can order the city destroyed, and whose officers could also be collecting its revenues. It’s worth giving him a good idea of what he stands to lose.

A hundred miles round and twelve thousand stone bridges, the Book’s account begins. Ships can pass below the arches [3]. Up on the road there are carts going over them, in Ramusio’s version, because somebody has taken the trouble to grade the approaches [4]. That’s the detail I’d look for before looking for a man who has counted every bridge. A carter who can get a loaded vehicle over the hump without backing into the street will know what the builders have done for him.

Down in the workshops, the Book has twelve great trades, each with twelve thousand premises [5]. A man can find a dozen workers in one shop, and up to forty in another. The masters and their wives, by this account, can live like royalty while the hired hands make the goods that supply other cities. Even a man with a hundred thousand bezants, Polo maintains, is bound to his father’s trade [6]. The privilege of sitting idle is considerable if your son still has to know what the workshop does.

At the West Lake the palaces and temples stretch along the shore. Polo makes the lake thirty miles round and puts a hall on each of two islands [7]. For a wedding feast you can cross to either one and find the silver plate and dishes waiting for you. Ramusio throws in table linen and sets a hundred parties going at once, with room enough for each wedding to carry on undisturbed [8]. A shopkeeper who’s been stepping round other people’s bundles all morning would find it a fine thing to have room to lay out the bowls. In the tale, at least, somebody else has already thought of the plates.

From one of those islands, turn round and look back at the timber houses. Among them stand stone towers where people can put things they’re afraid of losing to fire [9]. After the conquest, the Book puts ten of the Kaan’s guards at every bridge [10]. In Ramusio, the men keep the hours by striking a wooden instrument and a metal basin. On patrol they mark the door of any house with a light burning after hours, and a person caught out at night can expect to face the magistrate in the morning [11].

Fire has its own signal. From a tower on a rise, someone beats a wooden slab with a mallet [12]. Ramusio sends watchers running in from the other bridges. A chest that can’t get to a stone tower has another way out, by boat to a lake island [13]. In this account the owner can come out to help. Everybody else stays indoors. Next morning there will be things to fetch back across the lake and things nobody came in time to save. The same hands, in the daytime, take people who cannot work to hospitals founded by the old kings, or set to work those judged capable [14].

— Think of what it means to know the noises of your own street. The length of time since sundown and the difference between a fire and an ordinary hour can reach you through a closed door.

When it rains, the low ground would be a great deal of mud without the brick and stone streets. Ramusio has a strip left soft for the Kaan’s horses and a gravel middle lane with drains underneath, carrying the water off into the canals [15]. Off the road there are baths. The Book puts three thousand in the city and fits a hundred people at a time into a large one [16]. In this part of the account the water is hot.

Ask a man who ships goods to India where the sea begins. Polo puts the harbour at Ganfu (Ganpu, in the Haiyan area), twenty-five miles down the river, and lets vessels come all the way back up to Kinsay [17]. Ask a tax officer how far the city’s accounts reach. The Book makes Kinsay the seat of one of nine governments in Manzi, with a hundred and forty cities under it [18]. It scatters garrisons across the whole south, with infantry as well as cavalry and men from Cathay (northern China) as well as Mongols [19]. Some of those soldiers, in Ramusio, are a sight the people of Kinsay still resent [20].

A name board at the door of a house is another way of keeping count. Polo has the names of the family, servants and enslaved people on it, with the household’s animals counted as well. Rub out a name at a death and add one at a birth. An innkeeper also has to record a traveller’s arrivals and departures [21]. It makes the Book’s million and six hundred thousand houses sound like something you could add up, one door at a time [22]. Polo gives the city a single church of the Christians of the East [23]. Ramusio has men and women dressed in silk from the district and from elsewhere [24]. Run your eye from the names over the door down to the cloth a man standing in his doorway is wearing. The clerk who needs a tally of the house and the dealer who wants to sell its people cloth can walk down the same street with something quite different in mind.

Before a journey, the traveller can take the hour and sign of his birth to an astrologer and change his plans according to the answer [25]. When someone dies, people in hemp clothes follow the body with music. They send cut-out horses, armour, cloth and money into the fire, where the Book says it will furnish the dead person in the life to come [26]. It’s another inventory, made by people who know exactly which things the fire is meant to take.

---

Yule gives Ramusio’s fuller account of the city a chapter of its own [27]. Before we go into the market, look at the water on the far side of the city. There’s a channel broad enough to look like a piece of river, in Ramusio’s account, dug to give floodwater somewhere to go. The earth from the digging has been thrown up on the inner side. Water and bank do service as part of the city’s defences [28].

Come round the back of the market now. Carts use the broad street out front and boats come alongside the stone warehouses on a parallel canal. On market days Ramusio fits forty or fifty thousand people into each square [29]. You can get two geese and four ducks for a Venetian groat [30]. Fish come up the river from the sea and in from the lake. Whatever you may think of the likelihood of anybody selling the lot, Ramusio’s account has it gone in a few hours.

A rich customer can buy calf, beef, kid or lamb. It’s an oddly well-stocked butcher’s list to leave out pork [31]. A little farther on, fragrant pears the size of a good parcel of meat sit beside peaches in season. Rice wine is made fresh. Shops sell spices and jewels and pearl, and courts on the squares settle merchants’ quarrels and make sure the bridge guards are at their posts [32].

Pepper has a reckoning of its own. In Ramusio’s account a customs officer tells Polo that the city takes in forty-three loads a day, each of two hundred and twenty-three pounds [33]. You might credit a man at the customs with knowing how to count a sack. Whether you can turn his number into what a whole city ate is a different question. I’d want to know what was going out of the warehouses again.

After business, the lake barges in Ramusio can carry a table and chairs and twenty guests. The crew pole them from the roof. Below, a person at dinner can open a window and watch the temple gardens slide past [34]. On land a covered carriage fitted for six will take a party to the gardens and bring them back by evening [35]. A person with money to spend can see the city from almost any height except that of a man poling the boat.

Ramusio puts an old merchant beside Polo at the former palace of Facfur, the ruler of Manzi. The earlier account has gardens, lakes and a thousand splendid rooms. The merchant points out which pavilions survive, where the women’s courts were and where the garden wall has fallen [36]. In his recollection the royal feasts could seat ten thousand, all in silk and gold. Looking at walls you can barely trace is a good way to hear a man’s idea of the years when they were full.

---

At the Kaan’s accounts there are two columns worth keeping apart. The Book puts eighty *tomans* of gold against salt and reckons that as 5.6 million *saggi* [37]. In Ramusio the summer sun on the coastal pans makes enough salt for five other divisions as well as this one [38]. You can believe a ruler would watch the kettles and evaporation pans without accepting every zero that comes out of the conversion.

With salt out of the reckoning, the Book has sugar and rice wine, coal and the wares of the shops. It charges three and a third per cent on spices and ten on silk [39]. Ramusio also has ten per cent on merchandise arriving by sea from India and farther off [40]. The total for the column comes out at two hundred and ten *tomans*, or 14.7 million *saggi* of gold in Yule’s text. The amount looks different if the accountants started with units of silver or paper [41]. In one version the Kaan sends Polo to inspect the receipts. In an earlier telling, Polo has heard the calculations several times [42]. You can hear the pride he takes in knowing what the numbers are supposed to mean. An appointment is harder to hear.

At the edge of the city, there’s a road still within reach of those accounts. Follow it south for a day through houses and gardens and you can spend the Kaan’s paper at the next market.

## Notes

[1] ~ Kinsay is the former Sung capital at Hangzhou. The name represents a term for the capital, while “City of Heaven” belongs to the Book’s reading of it. See [Map A12, the canal spine](ch-10-manzi-sung.md#map-of-the-canal-spine) [@yc, 2.76 n. 1].

[2] ? The Book calls the queen’s written account the source for the description and adds that Polo later saw the city’s wonders for himself. Mascherpa identifies the claimed document with a dispatch of 1276. The attribution in the narrative cannot establish the document’s independent survival [@yc, 2.76; @mascherpa2024quinsai, p. 138].

[3] ✗ The 100 miles and 12,000 bridges come from the Yule-based Book, while Odoric gives similar figures (with variants across his texts) and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa gives a length of three days and six walled divisions in Lee’s translation (ch. XXIII) [@yc, 2.76 n. 2; @odoric1913travels, ch. 32, p. 195]. Yule himself cites the *Hang-chau Fu-Chi* (*Hangzhou fuzhi*) for a modern 35-*li* circuit (after 36 *li* under the Sui, 70 under the Tang, and about 100 under the Southern Song), Waṣṣāf’s 360 bridges, and Bishop G. E. Moule’s counts of 848 bridges across the department (*fu*) and 111 on a modern city map [@yc, 2.76 n. 2]. Mascherpa pairs 35 *li* with 347 bridges (A. C. Moule’s figure, reported second-hand as 117 in the city and 230 in the environs), though A. C. Moule’s pages and the underlying Chinese gazetteer counting boundaries remain uninspected here [@mascherpa2024quinsai, p. 138; @moule1957quinsai].

[4] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule brackets the design of the approaches that lets loaded traffic use the high bridges. The bridges and the claim about boats passing below are already in the base account [@yc, 2.76 n. 2].

[5] ✗ Polo assigns twelve thousand premises to each of twelve trades, and puts twelve or more men in each. The repeated scale puts an extraordinary number on the real concentration of crafts [@yc, 2.76].

[6] ~ The Book calls the inheritance of a father’s trade an ordinance binding even the wealthy. Yule considers a much less rigid custom more likely [@yc, 2.76 n. 3].

[7] ~ The West Lake and its waterside buildings are identifiable. Mascherpa gives the lake a circuit of about six miles, against the Book’s thirty, without establishing a set of identical limits for the two figures [@yc, 2.76 n. 4; @mascherpa2024quinsai, p. 138].

[8] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The tablecloths, napkins and hundred simultaneous parties are bracketed in Yule. The island halls and the plate for banquets belong to the earlier description [@yc, 2.76 n. 4].

[9] ~ The Book describes fireproof stores among wooden houses. It is a practical observation about protecting property in a city prone to fires [@yc, 2.76].

[10] ✗ The Book assigns the same guard strength to all twelve thousand bridges. The size of the force depends on accepting an implausible bridge count [@yc, 2.76 n. 2].

[11] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The bridge guards are in the base text. The hourly signals, marking of doors and night arrests are in the account Yule brackets [@yc, 2.76].

[12] ~ The alarm tower with its mallet and suspended board follows the bracketed bridge-watch account. Yule prints it in the base narrative [@yc, 2.76].

[13] ~ Ramusio-only. Boats to carry property to the islands are one of the responses to a fire in Ramusio’s description of the watch. The stone stores are already present in the earlier account [@yc, 2.76].

[14] ~ Ramusio-only. The description of the bridge guards’ duties includes taking people unable to work to hospitals and finding work for others. Yule’s notes compare the account of charitable provision with other descriptions [@yc, 2.76 n. 6].

[15] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The pavement is in the base account, and the provision for couriers and the street’s vaulted drains are additions bracketed by Yule [@yc, 2.76 n. 7].

[16] ~ Yule compares descriptions of public baths and records Ramusio’s different account, in which the inhabitants favour cold water. The number and capacity here are the Book’s estimates [@yc, 2.76 n. 8].

[17] ~ Ganfu is generally identified with Ganpu. Yule’s notes describe changes to the estuary and shipping arrangements that complicate a direct comparison with Polo’s account [@yc, 2.76 n. 9; @yc, 2.77 n. 2].

[18] ~ Polo counts nine divisions in the south and gives one hundred and forty cities to Kinsay. Yule’s note considers the administrative divisions behind these numbers [@yc, 2.76 n. 10].

[19] ~ The mixed composition of the garrisons matters more than the enormous headcount that follows from Polo’s figures. Those figures give a thousand as the minimum and sometimes ten or twenty times as many [@yc, 2.76 n. 11].

[20] ~ Ramusio-only. The continuation depicts a generally friendly city that welcomes foreign merchants and resents the occupying troops. That contrast is the account’s judgement of its inhabitants [@yc, 2.77 n. 8].

[21] ~ The physical detail of names above a door and the separate register for visitors belong to the Book’s account of how the government tracks a population. Yule cites comparisons with household registration [@yc, 2.76 n. 14].

[22] ✗ The Book counts 160 *tomans* of hearths, at ten thousand a *toman*. A population register and a number of this scale are separate claims [@yc, 2.76].

[23] ~ Polo reports a single church belonging to the Christians of the East. The number belongs to his description of the former capital [@yc, 2.76].

[24] ~ Ramusio-only. The bracketed description gives the people silk clothes and the district both local silk and imports. It leaves the quantities unspecified [@yc, 2.76 n. 5].

[25] ~ Polo describes recording a birth’s hour, planet and sign for use in astrological advice. Journey dates are one of the occasions the account gives for consulting an astrologer [@yc, 2.76].

[26] ✓ Yule’s notes discuss hemp mourning and the burning of representations of things for the dead. The Book describes the practice along with the return of the funeral music in the next world [@yc, 2.76 nn. 12–13].

[27] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule prints the long description separately. Mascherpa proposes a fuller, Z-related source for Ramusio’s account and discusses a family-notes hypothesis alongside possible interpolation [@yc, 2.77 n. 1; @mascherpa2024quinsai; @mascherpa2018fonte, pp. 62–63].

[28] ~ Ramusio-only. The relief channel and its embankment are part of Ramusio’s longer account. Yule compares older city plans showing a canal along the east and north sides, with the reported length open to question [@yc, 2.77 n. 3].

[29] ~ Ramusio-only. The text sets ten main squares between a broad road and a canal with warehouses. Yule compares descriptions of the canal and the squares and doubts the enormous dimensions and turnouts can be taken at face value [@yc, 2.77 nn. 3–4].

[30] ~ Ramusio-only. The price compares a Venetian silver coin to the buying power it would have for fowl in Kinsay. The cleared fish stalls belong to the account of how quickly the markets absorb supplies [@yc, 2.77 nn. 4–5].

[31] ? Ramusio-only. Ramusio’s list of the bigger animals sold at the shambles omits pigs. Yule points out that Odoric specifically mentions them in his account of this city. The reason for the omission is open [@yc, 2.77 n. 4; @odoric1913travels, ch. 32].

[32] ~ Ramusio-only. The text places shops and trades round the markets and gives the officers overseeing them jurisdiction over the bridge guards. The varieties of food and goods in the square are part of the same description [@yc, 2.77 nn. 4–6].

[33] ? Ramusio-only. The quantity comes from an unnamed customs officer in the text. Yule’s note includes observations of pepper in street cookery, which help place the commodity in the city without verifying the tally [@yc, 2.77 n. 7].

[34] ~ Ramusio-only. The flat-bottomed barges, their roof decks and the furnished cabins are features of the fuller account. The comparison to Chinese accounts of city life and lake outings gives context for the scene [@yc, 2.77 n. 9; @wu2015mengliang; @gernet1962daily].

[35] ~ Ramusio-only. The carriages furnish a second way of going out for recreation in the Venetian account. Their cushions, curtains and six places are the particulars it gives [@yc, 2.77 n. 10].

[36] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The palace’s scale appears in the earlier description. The old merchant’s tour and the recent alterations to the grounds belong to Ramusio. The Sung palace itself is known from Chinese sources [@yc, 2.76; @yc, 2.77; @moule1957quinsai].

[37] ✗ Yule’s text converts the salt income into eighty *tomans* of gold. Vogel’s examination of its bullion equivalent shows the problem with taking the printed total as an amount literally received in gold [@yc, 2.78 n. 2; @vogel2013marco, ch. 6].

[38] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule brackets the description of the coast’s lagoons, salt marshes and summer production. The large salt income is already present in the base account [@yc, 2.78].

[39] ~ The Book distinguishes ordinary duties at three and a third per cent from higher rates on goods such as silk. Its catalogue names several sources of revenue besides salt [@yc, 2.78 n. 2].

[40] ~ Ramusio-only. The ten per cent rate for seaborne imports is in brackets in Yule. The ten per cent on silk is in the base account [@yc, 2.78].

[41] ✗ The conversion of 210 *tomans* into gold poses the same problem as the salt figure. Yule considers the *ting*, an accounting unit with a silver basis, and Vogel compares what the figures imply when expressed in bullion [@yc, 2.78 n. 2; @vogel2013marco, ch. 6].

[42] ? Pauthier’s text has Polo sent to inspect the revenue, while the Geographic text has him hearing the calculations. Vogel weighs the stronger claim as part of his evaluation of the revenue account [@yc, 2.78 n. 1; @vogel2013marco, ch. 6].
