# Chapter II-11
## Yanju and Saianfu: Governorship Claim and the Mangonel Story

> *“And Messer Marco Polo himself, of whom this book speaks, did govern this city for three full years, by the order of the Great Kaan.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 68 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

Marco Polo, having told how the Great Kaan conquered Manzi, returns — as the Book promised — to Coiganju (Huai’an) and goes on regularly with the southern kingdom. He says that Coiganju is a very large city standing at the entrance to Manzi. The people are Idolaters and burn their dead, and are subject to the Great Kaan. They have a vast amount of shipping, as he already said when he spoke of the Caramoran (Huang He / Yellow River) ferry; and an immense quantity of merchandize comes hither, for the city is the seat of government for this part of the country. Owing to its being on the river, many cities send their produce thither to be again distributed in every direction. A great amount of salt also is made here, furnishing some forty other cities with that article, and bringing in a large revenue to the Great Kaan. [1]

So the threshold town is not only a ferry and a gate. It is a salt-mart and a redistribution hub, where canal and river traffic meet under Mongol paper-money and the old cremation formula.

When you leave Coiganju, according to the book, you ride south-east for a day along a causeway laid with fine stone, which you find at this entrance to Manzi. On either hand there is a great expanse of water, so that you cannot enter the province except along this causeway. At the end of the day’s journey you reach the fine city of Paukin (Baoying). The people are Idolaters, burn their dead, are subject to the Great Kaan, and use paper-money. They live by trade and manufactures and have great abundance of silk, whereof they weave a great variety of fine stuffs of silk and gold. Of all the necessaries of life there is great store.

When you leave Paukin you ride another day to the south-east, and then you arrive at the city of Cayu (Gaoyou). The people are Idolaters (and so forth). They live by trade and manufactures and have great store of all necessaries, including fish in great abundance. There is also much game, both beast and bird, insomuch that for a Venice groat you can have three good pheasants. [2]

Thus the stone road threads the flooded country: Paukin’s silk and gold cloth; Cayu’s cheap pheasants and lake water west of the canal bank. Later travellers measured the low country east of the waterway many feet below the canal’s brim — so that walls may hide from a boatman’s eye — and the western sheet is the Kao-yu Lake of later maps. Polo’s causeway is the only dry hinge into Manzi from this ferry.

When you leave Cayu, he says, you ride another day to the south-east through a constant succession of villages and fields and fine farms until you come to Tiju (Taizhou), which is a city of no great size but abounding in everything. The people are Idolaters (and so forth). There is a great amount of trade, and they have many vessels. And you must know that on your left hand, that is towards the east, and three days’ journey distant, is the Ocean Sea. At every place between the sea and the city salt is made in great quantities. And there is a rich and noble city called Tinju, at which there is produced salt enough to supply the whole province, and the Book can tell you it brings the Great Kaan an incredible revenue. The people are Idolaters and subject to the Kaan. Let us quit this, however, and go back to Tiju. [3]

So the coastal salt belt opens on the traveller’s left: Tiju of moderate size and many vessels; Tinju — or Chinju in some texts — whose pans feed a province and a treasury. The identification of Tinju is soft — Tung-chau near the Yangzi estuary, or I-chin as the Yangzhou salt port — but the Book’s aside is clear enough, and then it returns to the southeast road.

Again, leaving Tiju, you ride another day towards the south-east, and at the end of your journey you arrive at the very great and noble city of Yanju (Yangzhou), which has seven-and-twenty other wealthy cities under its administration; so that this Yanju is, you see, a city of great importance. It is the seat of one of the Great Kaan’s Twelve Barons, for it has been chosen to be one of the Twelve *Sings*. The people are Idolaters and use paper-money, and are subject to the Great Kaan. And Messer Marco Polo himself, of whom this book speaks, did govern this city for three full years, by the order of the Great Kaan. The people live by trade and manufactures, for a great amount of harness for knights and men-at-arms is made there. And in this city and its neighbourhood a large number of troops are stationed by the Kaan’s orders. [4]

— Here the asides have to be especially clear and honest. The city itself is firm: Yanju is Yangzhou, salt headquarters, silk and sweetmeat fame, and later Odoric found a Franciscan house and three Nestorian churches there. The claim that Marco *governed* it for three full years is one of the Book’s two loudest self-insertions in Manzi — and one of its hardest disputes. The Geographic Old French says *seingneurie* (lordship) for three years; some manuscripts read *séjourna* (sojourned), which may be a corruption or a quieter truth; Ramusio elevates the post until Marco governs *in place of one of the Barons* — almost certainly a translator’s gloss. Chinese administrative records do not name him. Yangzhou was briefly a *Sing* after the conquest, then a *Lu* circuit; a foreigner might have held a fiscal watch on the salt that made the Kaan rich, a temporary residency, or a *Lu*-type headship parallel to Mar Sarghis’s three years at Chinghianfu — without ever appearing in a Chinese biography. Frances Wood made the silence a pillar of her scepticism; de Rachewiltz, Haw, and Vogel reply that foreign service under the Yuan need not leave a Chinese notice. This retelling keeps the claim as the Book tells it, and does not pretend the office is proven. [5] Anyway, next he mentions two great provinces of Manzi which lie towards the west. And first of that called Nanghin.

Nanghin, according to the book, is a very noble Province towards the west. The people are Idolaters (and so forth) and live by trade and manufactures. They have silk in great abundance, and they weave many fine tissues of silk and gold. They have all sorts of corn and victuals very cheap, for the province is a most productive one. Game also is abundant, and lions too are found there — tigers, in Polo’s usual loose fauna. The merchants are great and opulent, and the Emperor draws a large revenue from them, in the shape of duties on the goods which they buy and sell. [6]

So Nanghin stands as a western silk province of duties and cheap grain. Yule preferred Ngan-king (Anqing) on the Kiang over the later name Nan-king, which the city of Jiankang did not yet bear in Polo’s day; the project gazetteer lists Nanghin as Nanjing — a soft ID. Either way, the Book uses it as a hinge toward a siege that, in the telling, outlasted a kingdom.

And now, Polo narrates, the Book will tell of the very noble city of Saianfu (Xiangyang), which well deserves a place in our book, for there is a matter of great moment to tell about it.

Saianfu is a very great and noble city, and it rules over twelve other large and rich cities, and is itself a seat of great trade and manufacture. The people are Idolaters (and so forth). They have much silk, from which they weave fine silken stuffs; they have also a quantity of game, and in short the city abounds in all that it behoves a noble city to possess.

Now you must know that this city held out against the Great Kaan for three years after the rest of Manzi had surrendered. The Great Kaan’s troops made incessant attempts to take it, but they could not succeed because of the great and deep waters that were round about it, so that they could approach from one side only, which was the north. Saianfu and her twin across the Han commanded the junction of approaches from Shen-si and from the north; while the waters held, the southern kingdom’s bulwark held. And the Book tells you they never would have taken it, but for a circumstance that it is going to relate. [7]

— Pause for chronology, because honesty requires it. Chinese and Persian memory keep the siege of Siang-yang and Fan-ch’eng from late 1268 to March 1273 — a prologue to the Sung conquest, not an epilogue after Manzi fell. Wassáf shares Polo’s misplacement. The Polo family’s standard arrival at court falls at the end of 1274, roughly a year and a half after the twin cities surrendered. So the Book’s “three years after the rest of Manzi surrendered” reverses the campaign order. The waters, the northern approach, and the three-year length are sound; the epilogue frame is not. [8] Anyway, the Book’s scene continues.

You must know that when the Great Kaan’s host had lain three years before the city without being able to take it, they were greatly chafed thereat. Then Messer Nicolo Polo and Messer Maffeo and Messer Marco said: “We could find you a way of forcing the city to surrender speedily;” whereupon those of the army replied, that they would be right glad to know how that should be. All this talk took place in the presence of the Great Kaan. For messengers had been despatched from the camp to tell him that there was no taking the city by blockade, for it continually received supplies of victual from those sides which they were unable to invest; and the Great Kaan had sent back word that take it they must, and find a way how. Then spoke up the two brothers and Messer Marco the son, and said: “Great Prince, we have with us among our followers men who are able to construct mangonels which shall cast such great stones that the garrison will never be able to stand them, but will surrender incontinently, as soon as the mangonels or trebuchets shall have shot into the town.”

The Kaan bade them with all his heart have such mangonels made as speedily as possible. Now Messer Nicolo and his brother and his son immediately caused timber to be brought, as much as they desired, and fit for the work in hand. And they had two men among their followers, a German and a Nestorian Christian, who were masters of that business, and these they directed to construct two or three mangonels capable of casting stones of three hundred pounds’ weight. Accordingly they made three fine mangonels, each of which cast stones of three hundred pounds’ weight and more. And when they were complete and ready for use, the Emperor and the others were greatly pleased to see them, and caused several stones to be shot in their presence; whereat they marvelled greatly and greatly praised the work. And the Kaan ordered that the engines should be carried to his army which was at the leaguer of Saianfu. [9]

— Here again the aside must be honest. The engines in question are not the torsion *ballistae* of the ancients. They are great slings of timber: a long tapering beam pivoted near the butt, a sling at the tip, and either the pull of many men on ropes or — more terrible — the fall of a heavy counterpoise, suddenly released, to fling the rounded stone in its parabola. Such machines the Latins called mangonels and trebuchets; the Persians *manjanīq*; the Chinese *pao* when the radical still meant stone. Mongol and Kin armies already cast millstones and fire long before Saianfu; Chinese pictures show man-rope slings. What the West may have brought was the counterpoised trebuchet — the improvement Rashīd thought missing when he said Cathay lacked *Kumghá* engines. Chinese annals name the Western engineers plainly: Alawating (Alauddin) of Mufali and his pupil Ysemain (Ismail) of Huli (Herat), sent from Abaqa’s Persia after Alihaiya urged stone-casters; they demonstrated at Dadu, reached the camp late in 1272, assaulted Fan-ch’eng first, and saw Siang-yang surrender weeks later. Shot of about a hundred and twenty-five catties — roughly a hundred and sixty-six pounds — is recorded, not three hundred. The Polo story rhymes the crash and the surrender, but relocates the credit to a German, a Nestorian, and the three Venetians. Some manuscripts even omit Marco from the agents. Family appropriation of a Western-engineer kernel is the likeliest reading; presence at the 1272–73 climax does not fit the standard chronology. [10] Anyway, the Book’s climax runs thus.

And when the engines were got to the camp they were forthwith set up, to the great admiration of the Tartars. And what shall one tell you? When the engines were set up and put in gear, a stone was shot from each of them into the town. These took effect among the buildings, crashing and smashing through everything with huge din and commotion. And when the townspeople witnessed this new and strange visitation they were so astonished and dismayed that they wist not what to do or say. They took counsel together, but no counsel could be suggested how to escape from these engines, for the thing seemed to them to be done by sorcery. They declared that they were all dead men if they yielded not, so they determined to surrender on such conditions as they could get. Wherefore they straightway sent word to the commander of the army that they were ready to surrender on the same terms as the other cities of the province had done, and to become the subjects of the Great Kaan; and to this the captain of the host consented.

So the men of the city surrendered, and were received to terms; and this all came about through the exertions of Messer Nicolo, and Messer Maffeo, and Messer Marco; and it was no small matter. For this city and province is one of the best that the Great Kaan possesses, and brings him in great revenues. [11]

Thus the twin cities at the Han’s hinge fall in the Book’s telling as an epilogue to Manzi, broken by Venetian counsel and Western timber. Chinese and Persian memory keep another cast of names and another order of years. The narrative has told what Rustichello set down.
