# Chapter II-11
## Yanju and Saianfu: Governorship Claim and the Mangonel Story

> *“And Messer Marco Polo himself, of whom this book speaks, did govern this city for three full years, by the order of the Great Kaan.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 68 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

Marco Polo enters Coiganju (Huai’an) at the northern edge of Manzi. It stands at a river junction, receives ships and goods, and pays the Kaan through trade and salt. He says about forty neighbouring cities depend on the place for redistribution and salt revenue. The detail matters because Coiganju is not merely a checkpoint: it gathers produce and moves it outward through the waterway. [1]

See [the map of the canal spine, II-10](ch-10-manzi-sung.md#map-of-the-canal-spine).

South-east of Coiganju, Polo follows a stone causeway raised between flooded ground. It leads to Paukin (Baoying), then to Cayu (Gaoyou), both making and trading silk. Fish and game abound at Cayu, and Polo gives one Venice groat for three pheasants. The causeway makes the western plain readable: water surrounds the road, and the road is the province’s entrance.

Paukin and Cayu belong to the road rather than to a coastline. Their textiles, fish and low surrounding ground explain why a stone ridge could serve as an edge to the province. Later drawings of the canal country show the embankment rising above fields and flood basins. Polo notices the road and the useful plain, but he does not need to enumerate the engineering that maintains them. [2]

The route continues south-east through villages and farms to Tiju (Taizhou), a busy but modest city. It has vessels and a market in constant movement. Salt is made along the coast to the east, and a larger place, Tinju (Nantong, Yule's Tung-chau), sends enough from its pans to supply the province and enrich the court. Other readings place the name beside a canal-side town, so the exact site remains uncertain. [3]

One more day brings the traveller to Yanju (Yangzhou), a great commercial city with twenty-seven subordinate places. The Kaan has made it one of his twelve *Sings*, and troops guard its rich workshops and harness trade. Marco Polo says he governed Yanju for three full years under the Kaan’s order. [4]

The city claim is not established in Chinese records. The Old French line gives *seingneurie*, a lordship or government; other manuscripts read *séjourna*, that he stayed or sojourned, and Ramusio expands the post to governorship in a baron’s place. Yanju was a salt and fiscal centre, so a temporary imperial commission or residence remains imaginable; a provincial baronship is not. Vogel’s work makes an audit or tax position plausible, but not proven, and later Italian tombstones in Yanju show other Latin residents, not Marco. The chapter keeps the Book’s claim as a claim.

The evidence can support a stay or a mission without supporting a three-year appointment. Yanju’s salt office drew Chinese and foreign specialists into the same fiscal system. A merchant who knew paper money, transport and customs might be useful to an inspector or a visiting agent. But he would not need a hereditary baronship, and the Book’s later readers must not turn that useful possibility into a proven title. The manuscript variants remain the best guide to what the text itself said. [5]

Polo also names Nanghin (Kaifeng) as a western province rich in silk, grain, game and mercantile duties. Pelliot identifies it with the Yuan Southern Capital, while Yule follows Pauthier’s reading for a lower-river city. Nanghin should not be silently moved to a later southern capital name. The Book’s lion is probably a tiger or another loose term for local beasts. [6]

Polo goes on to say that Nanghin is a province of cheap grain and costly commerce. He puts its merchants, silk workshops and taxes close together, as he does in other commercial regions. This is less a portrait of a single market than of a layered fiscal district: goods arrive from the province, workshops make them costly, and the court takes duties from the trade. The identification determines only which place he means; it does not make the revenue totals exact.

The route now turns to Saianfu (Xiangyang), with its twin settlement Fan-ch’eng across the Han River. Together they commanded northwestern approaches to southern China. The city was also a major silk and trade centre, ruling a dozen dependent places. The siege lasted about three years, but it did not begin after the rest of Manzi fell: it ran from late 1268 to March 1273 and was a preliminary to conquest of the south. [7]

The Han held water on every approach where Saianfu could be supplied, while Fan-ch’eng guarded the crossing opposite it. Together they controlled traffic from the west and the north. The siege was therefore less a wall brought down from a plain road than a blockade around a junction. Cublay (Kublai)’s forces had to stop boats, isolate garrison and civilian traffic, and keep a line along the northern bank.

The city could still receive supplies while the investment remained incomplete. That made time its own weapon: the court could afford a long siege, but the defenders could hold goods and reinforcements as long as a side remained open. The fall came only when the twin positions were separated from the river routes that had sustained them.

Blockade and bombardment served different purposes. The blockade closed the river and prevented fresh men or goods from entering; the engine made the inhabited side of the walls costly to defend. A stone could break a roof or frighten a company, but it could not feed a besieging army or hold a captured gate. Victory depended on the combination of soldiers, ships, roads and mechanics.

The defenders had to decide whether the remaining river routes and walls were worth the injury. When Fan-ch’eng surrendered first, the opposite city lost one of its two anchors. News of terms, an approaching bombardment and the absence of relief could persuade the remaining garrison to yield. The Book compresses that sequence into a moment of terror and a direct surrender; the surviving record shows a longer collapse.

The chronology is decisive for the Polo episode. Wassaf repeats the Book’s incorrect post-conquest frame, while Chinese and Persian sources place the twin cities before the end of the Song. The Polos reached Cublay (Kublai) at court about May 1275, roughly two years after Saianfu surrendered. Marco could not have advised the siege in 1272–73 under the standard family dates. [8]

The standard date is not absolute proof that Marco was absent from every earlier journey or that the family chronology contains no error. It is enough to separate a claimed source of the story from its remembered setting. The Book’s mangonel episode could preserve reports gathered later, heard in court or heard from a person who watched the siege. It could also enlarge the role of the family in a report already moving around the imperial circle.

Polo says the Great Kaan’s army had invested Saianfu without taking it because deep waters left only a northern approach. Messengers asked for help, and Niccolò, Maffeo and Marco offered to bring in experienced engine-makers. The Kaan agreed. Two followers, a German and a Nestorian Christian, then prepared timber and built three mangonels said to throw stones of three hundred pounds.

The scene is built to give the traveller’s family a useful, visible skill. First comes a distance: the walls resist ordinary blockade. Next comes expertise: the family says it knows men who can build a more terrible machine. Finally comes demonstration: the Kaan watches the engine throw at Cambaluc (Beijing), and the device is carried south. The narrative joins technical novelty to court authority. [9]

The engines were not ancient torsion *ballistae*. A trebuchet is a timber beam pivoted near one end, with a sling at the long arm and, in the counterweight form, a heavy box released to throw a stone. Mongol and Chinese armies already knew rope-pulled and other stone-casting engines, so the Polo account should not be read as the first appearance of artillery in China.

The practical difference is the counterweight. A rope crew has to pull together and reset by hand; a falling box supplies a more regular force to the beam. It does not make every building collapse at once, and it does not explain the surrender by itself. Yet a heavy stone landing among roofs, towers and stores can turn a siege from a contest of supplies into a fear of repeated damage. Polo’s long distance and 300-pound weight are dramatic figures, not engineering specifications.

The *Yuan shi* and Rashid al-Din credit different specialists. Abaga (Abaqa), the Ilkhan, sent Alawating and Ysemain, who demonstrated their engines at Cambaluc before attacking Fan-ch’eng and Saianfu. Yule gives the engineers as men of Miafarakain and Herat; Cordier’s Parker addendum reads Mausul (Mosul) and Serazy (Shiraz). Chinese records confirm the engine and surrender, not the Venetian presence. The recorded shot of about 125 catties, roughly 166 pounds, is smaller than Polo’s 300-pound stones. [10]

Polo places the three engines beside the army and makes the first stone strike the town. The walls, roofs and people receive the strange machine; the garrison concludes that it cannot endure the bombardment and surrenders on the terms offered to other cities. In the Book, Niccolò, Maffeo and Marco are the men who caused the breakthrough. [11]

The siege therefore preserves two accounts: a tactical one of engineers and counterweights, and a family one in which the Polos enter the court before the conquest. The first is supported by independent sources. The second carries the uncertainty of manuscript, chronology and later retelling.

Fan-ch’eng fell before Saianfu, and the engine’s effect was likely to undermine the lesser crossing and then dominate the larger city across the river. The surrender did not spring from a single stone or a single instant. The surrender terms, the control of the river and the pressure of the continuing siege mattered together.

That is the limit of the evidence and of the storyteller’s task. It is possible to describe the machine, to trace the Ismail and Ala al-Din names, and to say that the twin cities fell to Yuan arms. It is not possible to turn Polo’s late family claim into a witnessed role. The Book keeps an engineer’s spectacle and a traveller’s memory; the record keeps a separate cast of specialists.

The route has therefore moved from Coiganju and Yanju to a field where the same imperial court operated through many kinds of work. Salt collectors, local officials, soldiers and Persian specialists belonged to the same expanding state, even when later narratives assigned their achievements to a single merchant family. The traveller’s report can preserve that complexity, provided we keep the claim, the witness and the record distinct. It can show the state gathering wealth, force and knowledge in one system without pretending that every impressive episode has the same eyewitness or source behind it. The result is a more exact map of what the Book remembers and what independent evidence still establishes in the surviving Yuan and Persian records and accounts.
