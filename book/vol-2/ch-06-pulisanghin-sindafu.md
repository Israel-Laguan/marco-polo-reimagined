# Chapter II-6
## West and Southwest: Pulisanghin to Sindafu

> *“Now you must know that the Emperor sent the aforesaid Messer Marco Polo… on business of his into the Western Provinces.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 35 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

Marco Polo starts by narrating that the Emperor sent him — the author of this whole story — on business of his into the Western Provinces. According to the book, he travelled from Cambaluc (Khanbaliq / Beijing) a good four months’ journey toward the west; and so now the Book will tell what he saw as he went and returned. The administration chapters have already shown the capital as winter court and machine of rule — Ahmad cut down, mulberry-bark money, the twelve Barons, the *yam* posts, coal that burns through the night. Now Polo turns from those instruments to the roads they serve. [1]

He says that when you leave Cambaluc and have ridden some ten miles, you come to a very large river called Pulisanghin — Persian for “Stone Bridge,” though the name attaches first to the crossing and then, in foreign speech at Court, to the stream itself. Merchants with their merchandise ascend it from the sea, the Book claims. Over this river stands a stone bridge so fine that it has very few equals in the world: three hundred paces in length and a good eight in width, so that ten mounted men may ride abreast; four-and-twenty arches and as many water-mills; all of fine marble, well built and firmly founded. Along either side runs a parapet of marble slabs and columns: at the beginning a marble column stands upon a marble lion’s loins, and atop the column a second lion; a pace farther another pair, the space closed with grey marble so that none may fall into the water; and so the columns run from space to space. Travellers who have seen the Lugou Bridge in later centuries still recognise the lions and the long stone balustrade — even when the count of arches no longer matches the Book’s four-and-twenty; memory, repair, and neighbouring crossings have tangled the numbers. [2]

Beyond the bridge, Polo narrates, you find excellent hostelries, fine vineyards, fields and gardens, and springs. After thirty miles you come to a fine large city called Juju (Zhuozhou), where there are many abbeys of Idolaters, and the people live by trade and manufactures. They weave cloths of silk and gold, and very fine taffetas — *sendals* — and hostelries for travellers abound. A mile beyond this city the roads fork: one goes west, the other southeast. The westerly road is that through Cathay; the southeasterly goes toward the province of Manzi. The Book takes the westerly first, and will return, whimsically enough, not to the capital but to this same bifurcation when the time comes to lead south into Manzi. For the present, ten days on the Cathay road bring a constant succession of cities and boroughs, thriving villages, trade and manufactures, fine fields and vineyards — nothing, says the Book, worthy of special mention — until you reach a kingdom called Taianfu (Taiyuan). [3]

— A Ramusian aside, preserved only in that tradition: at the end of five days out of those ten there is a city unusually large and handsome called Acbaluc, where terminate in this direction the hunting preserves of the Emperor; within them none dares to sport except the Emperor and his family and those on the books of the Grand Falconer. Beyond that limit any gentleman may sport. The Great Kaan scarcely ever hunted that way, and the game — particularly the hares — so multiplied that the crops were destroyed. Informed of this, he proceeded thither with all his Court, and the game taken was past counting. So much for Ramusio’s colour; flag it as such. [4] Anyway, next he mentions that the capital of the kingdom is likewise called Taianfu, a very great and fine city of trade and industry, where they manufacture a large quantity of the most necessary equipments for the Emperor’s army. There grow excellent vines supplying great plenty of wine; and in all Cathay this is the only place, the Book avers, where wine is produced — carried thence over the country. There is also a great deal of silk, for the people have mulberry-trees and silkworms in quantity.

From Taianfu you ride westward again seven days through fine districts of towns and boroughs, much trade and various industry. Out of these districts go forth not a few great merchants who travel to India and other foreign regions, buying and selling and getting gain. After those seven days you arrive at Pianfu (Pingyang / Linfen), a large and important place of traders and silk. So the Book would leave it and speak of Cachanfu — but stay: first of the noble castle called Caichu. [5]

On leaving Pianfu, he says, you ride two days westward and come to the noble castle of Caichu, built in time past by a king of that country whom they used to call the Golden King, who had there a great and beautiful palace. In a great hall of that palace are portrayed all the ancient kings of the country, done in gold and other beautiful colours — a very fine sight — and each king in succession as he reigned added to those pictures. This Golden King was a great and potent Prince; during his stay at this place there used to be in his service none but beautiful girls, of whom he had a great number at Court. When he went to take the air about the fortress, these girls used to draw him about in a little carriage which they could easily move — so Ramusio again — and they were in attendance for everything pertaining to his convenience or pleasure.

Now the Book will tell a pretty passage that befell between the Golden King and Prester John, as related by the people of the Castle. It came to pass that this Golden King was at war with Prester John. The King held a position so strong that Prester John could not get at him, and was in great wrath. Seventeen gallants of Prester John’s Court came to him in a body and said that, an he would, they were ready to bring him the Golden King alive. He desired nothing better. So they took leave, presented themselves before the Golden King as men come from foreign parts to enter his service, and were welcomed — never imagining he that they had ill intent. They served him so well that he grew to love them dearly. When they had abode nearly two years, conducting themselves like persons who thought of anything but treason, they one day accompanied the King on a pleasure party with very few else along. After they had crossed a certain river about a mile from the castle and saw that they were alone with him, they said one to another that now was the time. They all incontinently drew, and told the King he must go with them and make no resistance, or they would slay him. The King was in alarm and great astonishment. They answered: “You shall come with us, will ye, nill ye, to Prester John our Lord.” [6]

And on this the Golden King was so sorely grieved that he was like to die. He pleaded the honourable entertainment they had had in his house; but they answered only that so it must be, and away they had him to Prester John. When Prester John beheld the King he was right glad, and greeted him with something like a malison. The King answered not a word. Prester John ordered him taken forth straightway and put to look after cattle — yet to be well looked after himself — of the grudge he bore him. When the King had thus kept cattle for two years, Prester John sent for him, treated him with honour, clothed him in rich robes, and said: “Now Sir King, art thou satisfied that thou wast in no way a man to stand against me?” “Truly, my good Lord, I know well and always did know that I was in no way a man to stand against thee.” And Prester John replied: “I ask no more.” He caused horses and harness of war to be given him, with a goodly train, and sent him back to his own country. Thereafter he remained ever friendly to Prester John and held fast by him. So much of this adventure — told as romance, with a possible memory of the Jin “Golden Dynasty” behind the name; no Chinese Jin-history matches the exact tale. [7] Anyway, the Book proceeds with its subject.

When you leave the castle and travel about twenty miles westward, Polo narrates, you come to a river called Caramoran — the Mongols’ *Qara Mören*, Black River, the Chinese Hwang Ho (Yellow River) — so big that no bridge can be thrown across it; for it is of immense width and depth, and reaches to the Great Ocean that encircles the earth. On this river are many cities and walled towns, and many merchants, for much traffic takes place upon it, there being a great deal of ginger and a great deal of silk. Game birds are in wonderful abundance: you may buy at least three pheasants for a Venice groat of silver — or rather for an *asper*, which is worth a little more. On the lands adjoining grow vast quantities of great canes, some a foot or a foot and a half in girth, which the natives employ for many useful purposes. [8]

After passing the river and travelling two days westward you come to the noble city of Cachanfu. The inhabitants are all Idolaters — and the Book reminds you again that all the people of Cathay are Idolaters. It is a city of great trade and of work in gold-tissues of many sorts. There is nothing else worth mentioning, and so the Book proceeds to a noble city which is the capital of a kingdom, called Kenjanfu (Xi’an / Chang’an).

When you leave Cachanfu and travel eight days westward, he says, you meet cities and boroughs abounding in trade and industry, beautiful trees and gardens, and fine plains planted with mulberries. The people are all Idolaters. There is plenty of game of all sorts. And when you have travelled those eight days, you come to that great city Kenjanfu — capital of the kingdom of Kenjanfu, which in old times was a noble, rich, and powerful realm, and had many great and wealthy and puissant kings. But now the king thereof is a prince called Mangalai — Mangala — the son of the Great Kaan, who hath given him this realm and crowned him king thereof. It is a city of great trade and industry. They have great abundance of silk, from which they weave cloths of silk and gold of divers kinds, and manufacture all sorts of equipments for an army. They have every necessary of man’s life very cheap. Outside the city is the palace of the Prince Mangalai: a fine palace and a great, standing in a great plain abounding in lakes and streams and springs. Round about it is a massive and lofty wall, five miles in compass, well built and all garnished with battlements. Within are many great and splendid halls, and many chambers, all painted and embellished with work in beaten gold. This Mangalai rules his realm right well with justice and equity, and is much beloved by his people. The troops are quartered round about the palace, and enjoy the sport that the royal demesne affords. [9]

— Here an honest aside: Kenjanfu is Xi’an, the old Chang’an; Mangala, Kublai’s third son, Wang of Anxi, died in 1280, which puts a useful ceiling on Polo’s western journey. What the Book does *not* mention is the Nestorian Christian monument at Xi’an — the stele of 781, rediscovered in 1625 — a famous silence that later readers have weighed. The stone may have been buried or obscured; Nestorians may have been thin on the ground; or Polo simply passed without notice. Silence is not proof of absence from China. [10] Anyway, next he mentions a very mountainous province called Cuncun.

On leaving the Palace of Mangalai you travel westward three days, finding a succession of cities and boroughs and beautiful plains, inhabited by people who live by trade and industry and have great plenty of silk. At the end of those three days you reach the great mountains and valleys which belong to the province of Cuncun. There are towns and villages in the land, and the people live by tilling the earth and by hunting in the great woods; for the region abounds in forests wherein are many wild beasts — lions, bears, lynxes, bucks and roes, and sundry other kinds — so that many are taken by the people of the country, who make a great profit thereof. So this way you travel over mountains and valleys, finding a succession of towns and villages and many great hostelries for the entertainment of travellers, interspersed among extensive forests. Twenty days, in most manuscripts, are assigned to those mountains; one tradition shortens the count — enough, in either case, to mark a road that later travellers still call a work of tremendous engineering: timber corbels in the rock, cliffs rounded on poles above a foaming river, rest-houses at convenient distances. [11]

After you have travelled those days through the mountains of Cuncun, Polo narrates, you come to a province called Acbalec Manzi, which is all level country, with plenty of towns and villages, and belongs to the Great Kaan. The people are Idolaters, and live by trade and industry. In this province grows such a great quantity of ginger that it is carried all over the region of Cathay, and affords a maintenance to all the people of the province. They have also wheat and rice and other kinds of corn in great plenty and cheapness. The capital city is called Acbalec Manzi, which signifies “the White City of the Manzi Frontier.” This plain extends for two days’ journey. After those two days you again come to great mountains and valleys and extensive forests, and continue to travel westward through this kind of country for twenty days, finding however numerous towns and villages. The people are Idolaters, and live by agriculture, by cattle-keeping, and by the chase, for there is much game. And among other kinds there are the animals that produce the musk, in great numbers. The exact town behind Acbalec remains unsettled among commentators — Hanzhong’s plain is almost certain; the particular settlement is not. [12]

When you have travelled those twenty days westward through the mountains, according to the book, you arrive at a plain belonging to a province called Sindafu (Chengdu), which still is on the confines of Manzi, and the capital city of which is also called Sindafu. This city was in former days a rich and noble one, and the Kings who reigned there were very great and wealthy. It is a good twenty miles in compass, but divided in the way that shall be told. The King of this Province, in the days of old, when he found himself drawing near to death, leaving three sons behind him, commanded that the city should be divided into three parts, and that each of his three sons should have one. So each of these three parts is separately walled about, though all three are surrounded by the common wall of the city. Each of the three sons was King, having his own part of the city and his own share of the kingdom. But the Great Kaan conquered the kingdom of these three Kings and stripped them of their inheritance. [13]

Through the midst of this great city runs a large river, in which they catch a great quantity of fish. It is a good half mile wide, and very deep withal, and so long that it reaches all the way to the Ocean Sea — a very long way, equal to eighty or a hundred days’ journey. And the name of the River is Kian-suy. The multitude of vessels that navigate this river is so vast that no one who should read or hear the tale would believe it. The quantities of merchandise which merchants carry up and down are past all belief. In fact it is so big that it seems to be a Sea rather than a River.

Let us now speak of a great Bridge which crosses this River within the city. This bridge is of stone; it is seven paces in width and half a mile in length; and all along its length on either side there are columns of marble to bear the roof, for the bridge is roofed over from end to end with timber, and that all richly painted. And on this bridge there are houses in which a great deal of trade and industry is carried on. But these houses are all of wood merely, and they are put up in the morning and taken down in the evening. Also there stands upon the bridge the Great Kaan’s *Comercque* — that is to say, his custom-house — where his toll and tax are levied. And the dues taken on this bridge bring to the Lord a thousand pieces of fine gold every day and more. The people of Sindafu live by manufactures, for they make fine sendals and other stuffs. [14]

When you leave this city you travel for five days across a country of plains and valleys, finding plenty of villages and hamlets whose people live by husbandry. There are numbers of wild beasts — lions and bears and such like. After those five days’ march you reach a province of which the Book will speak in its place — Tebet and the southwestern marches beyond.

Here the chapter stops short of those marches. For the present it has done what it promised after administration: the bridge of Pulisanghin and the fork at Juju; Taianfu’s wine and army-stores; Caichu’s Golden King and the Prester John romance; Caramoran and Cachanfu; Kenjanfu under Mangalai; the wearisome mountains of Cuncun; Acbalec Manzi’s ginger plain; and Sindafu with its tripartite walls, its sea-like river, its roofed bridge and morning markets, and the Kaan’s daily gold from the *Comercque*. Next the Book will turn from the Chengdu basin toward Tebet, Caindu, and Carajan.
