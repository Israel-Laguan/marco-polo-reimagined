# Chapter II-6
## West and Southwest: Pulisanghin to Sindafu

> *“Over this River there is a very fine stone bridge, so fine indeed, that it has very few equals.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 35 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

The lion above the water has another lion under its feet. Ride ten miles out of Cambaluc (Beijing), to the place where the emperor's business takes Marco across the river, and there are pairs of them all along the bridge. This is the beginning of a western journey that Polo reckons at about four months [1].

Pulisanghin (the Lugou Bridge) is Persian for stone bridge, and the name in the Book has gone from the bridge to the river. Marco can tell you where the merchandise under it comes from. The Book brings boats up from the sea [2]. Polo allows ten mounted men to ride abreast along the stone span. The marble slabs between the carved uprights protect a man from falling, and the lions make a sight worth slowing down for [3]. Polo counts twenty-four arches and as many mills. The surviving crossing has eleven arches. Either way you can see why a traveller might measure the length by remembering what he saw repeated all along it [4].

On the far side, a man looking for a place to spend the night sees vineyards, gardens, springs and hostelries along the road. Juju (Zhuozhou) lies south-west of the capital [5]. The road it stands on has a trade in fine silk and cloth of gold. A man walking through the workshops could hear a fine fabric called by a different name depending on who hoped to sell it [6]. A mile farther, the roads part. The one you can take south-east goes to Manzi. Follow Marco out the other way now. We shall have a reason to come back to the fork [7].

Ten days of settled country lead toward Taianfu (Taiyuan) [8]. Towns, villages, vineyards, and cultivated fields follow one another, with trade and manufacture everywhere; none, the account suggests, demands a separate description. The road passes from one community to the next without a break in the signs of work and exchange. Ramusio's version adds Acbaluc, a large handsome city at the hunting preserve's edge, five days along this route. Only the Kaan, his family, and those registered with the Grand Falconer could hunt inside the preserves; outside them, any gentleman could. The Kaan rarely came this way, the hares multiplied until they ruined crops, and when news reached him he brought his court and killed game beyond counting [9].

Taianfu is both the kingdom and its capital, a large centre of trade. Its workshops supply the Emperor's army with essential equipment. Wine is shipped away from the town. The Book makes it the only place in Cathay that produces any, which is the sort of thing a man selling wine might be glad to have said in front of a buyer [10]. Mulberry trees and silkworms support a substantial silk trade [11].

A week farther west brings the road to Pianfu (Pingyang, near Linfen), another large city whose residents make their living through trade and silk [12]. The districts between the two cities are full of towns and boroughs, and their merchants carry goods as far as India and other foreign regions, returning with new wares and profits. From Pianfu, two days' travel leads to Caichu (site uncertain), a castle built by an earlier king remembered as the Golden King. Its palace has a grand hall painted with portraits of the country's former rulers; each king, in turn, added his own likeness [13].

Ramusio's version adds the Golden King's girls and their carriage. They served him at court and pulled him around the fortress in a small vehicle when he went out for air [14].

What else can a man at this castle show you? He can tell you the Golden King once trusted the wrong people, and he can do it in such a way that you know the moment when that trust runs out. Prester John wants to get him out of the fortress [15]. The king's strong position frustrates his enemy, until seventeen of Prester John's courtiers volunteer to capture him. They present themselves as foreigners seeking service, win the king's trust, and remain with him for nearly two years. On an outing with few attendants, the party crosses a river; once they are alone with him, the men draw their weapons and demand that he go with them or die. His first thought is to remind these men how well he has treated them. They have taken care that there is no one else there to tell it to. You can imagine the king thinking back to the day he welcomed them, without finding a single moment at which he might have known [16].

Prester John receives his captive with satisfaction and orders him to tend cattle. The king is treated well but remains a herdsman for two years. Then Prester John summons him, dresses him in fine robes, and asks whether he now understands that he could never stand against him. The Golden King agrees. Prester John sends him back with horses, harness and an escort. It is a fine way to receive a guest if that guest has already spent two years in the field with your cows. The Golden King goes back an ally [17]. A name may have come down from the Jin, the dynasty remembered in the word for gold. The histories checked by Yule and Cordier provide no matching episode [18]

Twenty miles west of the castle, the road reaches Caramoran (the Yellow River; Mongol Qara Mören). The river is so broad and deep that Polo's account rules out a bridge; it runs all the way to the encircling Ocean. Many cities and walled towns line its banks, and merchants carry ginger and silk along its busy waters [19]. Game birds are plentiful: three pheasants cost a Venetian groat, or an asper, a coin worth a little more [20]. Ramusio's version adds vast stands of thick riverbank canes, useful to local people for many purposes [21].

Two days beyond the crossing is Cachanfu (Puzhou), a city of merchants and gold-woven textiles [22]. Polo groups its inhabitants with the “Idolaters” of Cathay, a label his account applies broadly to the region's non-Muslim peoples. There is little else he singles out before moving on. Eight days farther west, through trading towns, mulberry groves, and game-rich plains, lies Kenjanfu (Xi'an, ancient Chang'an), capital of a once powerful kingdom [23]. The route crosses settled ground rather than wilderness: fine trees and gardens appear alongside the market towns, and mulberry plantations mark the silk districts. The villages and boroughs form a continuous sequence of working communities, with fields and hunting grounds between them.

Kenjanfu now belongs to Mangalai (Mangala), a son of the Great Kaan, who made him its prince [24]. Silk production and military workshops thrive, and everyday goods are cheap. The city still carries the weight of its former kingdom, but its present ruler is a Kaan's son governing a realm within the empire. Outside the city, Mangalai's palace stands on a plain of lakes, streams, and springs. A wall five miles around encloses large halls and chambers decorated with beaten gold. The prince's troops are stationed nearby, where they can enjoy the royal hunting grounds. The account credits Mangalai with ruling justly and winning the affection of his people [25].

— Kenjanfu's 781 Nestorian stele is absent from Polo's account. Yule dates its rediscovery by workmen to 1625. Set that event beside the date on the stone and there is a long interval during which a traveller could have come to the city. We do not know whether the stone stood where Polo could see it when he came [26].

Three days west of Mangalai's palace, the road reaches the mountains of Cuncun (the Qinling–Hanzhong mountains). Towns and villages give way to steep valleys and forests where people farm, hunt, and trap animals. Polo lists lions, bears, lynxes, deer, and other game, whose pelts and meat provide people with a living. Inns punctuate the route through the woods, even as the road climbs and drops through difficult terrain. Most manuscripts assign twenty days to these mountains, while one tradition gives a shorter journey [27]. Later descriptions of the road tell of timber beams projecting from cliff faces, poles used to move the track around rock, and rest houses spaced along the way [28].

At the end of the mountain crossing comes Acbalec Manzi (probably the Hanzhong area), a level province of towns and villages under the Kaan's rule. Its people trade and make goods, but ginger is the great source of their livelihood: it travels throughout Cathay and supports the province's people. Wheat, rice, and other grains are also plentiful and inexpensive; a man with a load of ginger can find enough for his own dinner without having to carry it along [29]. Ramusio's text names the provincial capital Acbalec Manzi and provides the gloss “White City of the Manzi Frontier” [30]. The plain takes two days to cross, with its villages and cultivated land spread along the route.

Beyond it, the road enters another stretch of mountains, valleys, and forests. For twenty days the route continues west through settlements and hunting country, where people farm, keep livestock, and pursue game. Musk deer are especially common. At the end of this stage the traveller reaches a plain in the province of Sindafu (Chengdu), near the border of Manzi [31].

Sindafu was once a rich kingdom. The account gives its capital the same name and estimates the city at about twenty miles around. According to local history, a king nearing death divided it among his three sons. Each son ruled a section enclosed by its own wall, while one outer wall surrounded the whole city. Their father made each a king, but the Great Kaan later conquered their realm and took it from them [32].

A large river runs through the city, carrying abundant fish and a multitude of vessels. Polo calls it Kian-suy (the Min River system, which joins the Yangtze) and says it reaches the Ocean after a voyage of eighty or a hundred days. Polo makes it half a mile wide, enough to seem more like a sea than a river [33]. The ships and the merchandise moving up and down it are so numerous, the account warns, that a reader might not believe the tally.

The city's bridge crosses this waterway. Stone piers and marble columns support a timber roof painted from end to end; the bridge is seven paces wide and half a mile long. Shops line it, but they are wooden structures set up in the morning and taken down at night, so the bridge changes character over the course of a day [34]. A man coming out again in the evening can pass right over the place where the busiest stall stood that morning. The Kaan's *Comercque*, his customs house, collects tolls there. The daily revenue, Polo estimates, exceeds a thousand pieces of fine gold [35]. The people of Sindafu make sendals and other fine cloth. You see why a man who sits in one place to take his share may find the bridge as good a spot as any [36].

Leave the toll collector to his day’s work and go five days into the country towards Tebet (eastern Tibet). A man who has had his choice of shops on the bridge will be looking for something as simple as a length of bamboo before he makes his bed [37].
