# Chapter II-6
## West and Southwest: Pulisanghin to Sindafu

**[V]**

---

Marco Polo's western assignment begins with a ride out of Cambaluc (Khanbaliq, modern Beijing). The Great Kaan sent him west on imperial business; the account places the journey at about four months. Polo follows the roads he travelled and returns to them later in the volume. [1]

Ten miles from the capital stands Pulisanghin (Lugou Bridge, now the Marco Polo Bridge), a crossing over a broad river. The name is Persian for “Stone Bridge”; in foreign speech at court it came to name the river as well. Merchants bring goods upriver from the sea, the account says. The stone span measures three hundred paces long and eight wide, room for ten mounted men abreast. Polo counts twenty-four arches and as many watermills. Marble forms the bridge and its parapet: at each interval a column rises from a lion's back, another lion crowns the top, and grey marble slabs close the gap to the next pair. No traveller can step off the paved crossing into the river. The account lingers on the repeated pattern, as though the bridge's length were measured out in stone beasts and carved uprights. [2]

The bridge is not just a crossing in the account. It marks a change of scene: after the open approach from Cambaluc, travellers arrive among river traffic and the services that support it. The stonework carries the eye along its repeated lions and columns, while the river below links the road to merchants arriving from far away.

On the far bank are inns, vineyards, gardens, fields, and springs. About thirty miles to the south-west lies Juju (Zhuozhou), a busy city with Buddhist monasteries, workshops, and a trade in silk-and-gold cloth and fine sendals. The account also stresses the city's many hostelries, signs of a road used by merchants and other travellers. A mile beyond it the road divides: one branch heads west through Cathay, the other south-east toward Manzi. This fork will matter again when the journey turns south; for now, the western branch carries the story onward. [3]

Ten days of settled country lead toward Taianfu (Taiyuan). Towns, villages, vineyards, and cultivated fields follow one another, with trade and manufacture everywhere; none, the account suggests, demands a separate description. The road passes from one community to the next without a break in the signs of work and exchange. Ramusio's version adds Acbaluc, a large handsome city at the hunting preserve's edge, five days along this route. Only the Kaan, his family, and those registered with the Grand Falconer could hunt inside the preserves; outside them, any gentleman could. The Kaan rarely came this way, the hares multiplied until they ruined crops, and when news reached him he brought his court and killed game beyond counting. [4]

Taianfu is both the kingdom and its capital, a large centre of trade. Its workshops supply the Emperor's army with essential equipment. Vines produce so much wine that Polo's account calls this the only wine-growing place in Cathay, with wine shipped across the country. Mulberry trees and silkworms support a substantial silk trade. [5]

A week farther west brings the road to Pianfu (Pingyang, near Linfen), another large city whose residents make their living through trade and silk. The districts between the two cities are full of towns and boroughs, and their merchants carry goods as far as India and other foreign regions, returning with new wares and profits. From Pianfu, two days' travel leads to Caichu (site uncertain; Jiangzhou is one proposal), a castle built by an earlier king remembered as the Golden King. Its palace has a grand hall painted with portraits of the country's former rulers; each king, in turn, added his own image.

Ramusio's version adds the Golden King's girls and their carriage. They served him at court and pulled him around the fortress in a small vehicle when he went out for air. [6]

The castle folk also tell a story of the Golden King and Prester John. The king's strong position frustrates his enemy, until seventeen of Prester John's courtiers volunteer to capture him. They present themselves as foreigners seeking service, win the king's trust, and remain with him for nearly two years. On an outing with few attendants, the party crosses a river; once they are alone with him, the men draw their weapons and demand that he go with them or die. The king pleads that he has treated them honourably, but they take him to Prester John.

Prester John receives his captive with satisfaction and orders him to tend cattle. The king is treated well but remains a herdsman for two years. Then Prester John summons him, dresses him in fine robes, and asks whether he now understands that he could never stand against him. The Golden King agrees. Prester John gives him horses, military gear, and an escort to return home. The king remains his ally thereafter. The final exchange turns defeat into a lesson in rank: the Golden King admits the other's greater power, and Prester John restores him with the equipment of a ruler. A courtly humiliation followed by a generous release gives the story its neat ending. It may preserve a memory of the Jin, or “Golden,” dynasty, but no matching episode appears in the histories checked by Yule's editors. [7]

Twenty miles west of the castle, the road reaches Caramoran (the Yellow River; Mongol Qara Mören). The river is so broad and deep that Polo's account rules out a bridge; it runs all the way to the encircling Ocean. Many cities and walled towns line its banks, and merchants carry ginger and silk along its busy waters. Game birds are plentiful: three pheasants cost a Venetian groat, or an asper, a coin worth a little more. Ramusio's version adds vast stands of thick riverbank canes, useful to local people for many purposes. [8]

Two days beyond the crossing is Cachanfu (Puzhou), a city of merchants and gold-woven textiles. Polo groups its inhabitants with the “Idolaters” of Cathay, a label his account applies broadly to the region's non-Muslim peoples. There is little else he singles out before moving on. Eight days farther west, through trading towns, mulberry groves, and game-rich plains, lies Kenjanfu (Xi'an, ancient Chang'an), capital of a once powerful kingdom. The route crosses settled ground rather than wilderness: fine trees and gardens appear alongside the market towns, and mulberry plantations mark the silk districts. The villages and boroughs form a continuous sequence of working communities, with fields and hunting grounds between them.

Kenjanfu now belongs to Mangalai (Mangala), a son of the Great Kaan, who made him its prince. Silk production and military workshops thrive, and everyday goods are cheap. The city still carries the weight of its former kingdom, but its present ruler is a Kaan's son governing a realm within the empire. Outside the city, Mangalai's palace stands on a plain of lakes, streams, and springs. A wall five miles around encloses large halls and chambers decorated with beaten gold. The prince's troops are stationed nearby, where they can enjoy the royal hunting grounds. The account credits Mangalai with ruling justly and winning the affection of his people. [9]

— Kenjanfu's 781 Nestorian stele is absent from Polo's account. Workmen rediscovered it in 1625. Neither that date nor the omission establishes whether the monument was visible during Polo's stay; the silence is worth noting, not proof that he saw no Christians in China. [10]

Three days west of Mangalai's palace, the road reaches the mountains of Cuncun (the Qinling–Hanzhong mountains). Towns and villages give way to steep valleys and forests where people farm, hunt, and trap animals. Polo lists lions, bears, lynxes, deer, and other game, whose pelts and meat provide people with a living. Inns punctuate the route through the woods, even as the road climbs and drops through difficult terrain. Most manuscripts assign twenty days to these mountains, while one tradition gives a shorter journey. Later descriptions of the road tell of timber beams projecting from cliff faces, poles used to move the track around rock, and rest houses spaced along the way. [11]

At the end of the mountain crossing comes Acbalec Manzi (probably the Hanzhong area), a level province of towns and villages under the Kaan's rule. Its people trade and make goods, but ginger is the great source of their livelihood: it travels throughout Cathay and supports the province's people. Wheat, rice, and other grains are also plentiful and inexpensive; the account presents this as a rich agricultural district as well as a commercial one. Ramusio's text names the provincial capital Acbalec Manzi and glosses it as the “White City of the Manzi Frontier.” The plain takes two days to cross, with its villages and cultivated land spread along the route.

Beyond it, the road enters another stretch of mountains, valleys, and forests. For twenty days the route continues west through settlements and hunting country, where people farm, keep livestock, and pursue game. Musk deer are especially common. At the end of this stage the traveller reaches a plain in the province of Sindafu (Chengdu), near the border of Manzi. [12]

Sindafu was once a rich kingdom. The account gives its capital the same name and estimates the city at about twenty miles around. According to local history, a king nearing death divided it among his three sons. Each son ruled a section enclosed by its own wall, while one outer wall surrounded the whole city. Their father made each a king, but the Great Kaan later conquered their realm and took it from them. [13]

A large river runs through the city, carrying abundant fish and a multitude of vessels. Polo calls it Kian-suy (the Min River system, which joins the Yangtze) and says it reaches the Ocean after a voyage of eighty or a hundred days. Its width, half a mile, makes it seem more like a sea than a river. The ships and the merchandise moving up and down it are so numerous, the account warns, that a reader might not believe the tally.

The city's bridge crosses this waterway. Stone piers and marble columns support a timber roof painted from end to end; the bridge is seven paces wide and half a mile long. Shops line it, but they are wooden structures set up in the morning and taken down at night, so the bridge changes character over the course of a day. The Kaan's *Comercque*, his customs house, collects tolls there. The daily revenue, Polo estimates, exceeds a thousand pieces of fine gold. Sindafu's people also make sendals and other fine cloth. [14]

Five days beyond Sindafu, the road crosses plains and valleys dotted with farming villages. Their people live by agriculture, while lions, bears, and other wild animals still range through the countryside. At the end of this leg the route reaches the province of Tebet (eastern Tibet). From there the road turns toward Caindu (Xichang) and Carajan (Yunnan, around Dali and Kunming).
