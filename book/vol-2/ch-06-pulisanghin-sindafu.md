# Chapter II-6
## West and Southwest: Pulisanghin to Sindafu

> *“Now you must know that the Emperor sent the aforesaid Messer Marco Polo… on business of his into the Western Provinces.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 35 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

Leave the white court boots behind at the palace door, buckle on riding spurs, and ride ten miles west out of Cambaluc (Beijing) along the imperial flagstone road. Here Volume II turns onto the first great provincial spoke of its wheel: the Great Kaan’s errand sends Marco westward into distant provinces bordering Manzi (southern China), a four-month journey by his reckoning [1]. At the end of that first ten-mile stage rises a crossing made to make a Venetian stop his horse. One limestone lion sits perched atop its marble post above the muddy current, and another carved lion crouches beneath the column base.

Pulisanghin (the Lugou Bridge) keeps the Persian name for a stone bridge, and in the Book that label slides smoothly from the masonry piers onto the whole rushing river beneath. Trade rides that current right up from the sea [2]. Across its flat deck, ten mounted troopers can gallop abreast between carved marble panels guarding pack-mules from tumbling into the stream [3]. Polo counts twenty-four grey arches spanning the water, matched by twenty-four watermills turning beneath the piers. Stand on the riverbanks today and only eleven main stone arches cross the bed—yet a rider counting repeated stone bays in winter glare might easily let the span double in memory [4].

Across the bridge, thirsty horses find sweet well-springs, garden plots, vineyards, and bustling roadside inns. Beyond those pleasant meadows rises Juju (Zhuozhou), a prosperous market city south-west of the imperial capital [5]. Down its shaded streets, merchants unpack silk and gleaming gold-threaded cloth beside fine taffetas stamped with competing workshop seals [6]. One mile past Juju’s outer gate, pause and mark the fork in the stone highway. The left-hand road swings south-east toward wealthy Manzi, while our track holds straight west. Keep that crossroads pinned in your memory—we shall ride back into this exact fork from the far southern mountains seventy days down the line [7].

Ten steady days westward carry pack-mules across an unbroken plain of walled towns, timber farms, orchards, and vineyards toward Taianfu (Taiyuan) [8]. Five marches down that trunk road, the journey halts beside a handsome walled town called Acbaluc sitting right on the boundary of an imperial hunting ground [9]. Inside the marked chase, no farmer or squire dared cast a hawk save the Great Kaan’s kinsmen and enrolled court falconers. Because decades passed between royal visits, wild hares multiplied unchecked across the meadows until they chewed peasant crops bare. When word reached court, Cublay (Kublai) rode out with his hunters for a giant harvest drive that piled game high across the grass [10].

Taianfu greets riders as both an ancient province and its thriving capital seat. Its smoke-blackened smithies forge iron mail, leather harness, spearheads, and heavy gear for the Great Kaan’s frontier armies [11]. Grape wine flows freely from stone press-houses out toward distant markets. Polo even boasts that Cathay grows vines nowhere else—the exact puff a local vintner might whisper beside an oak tun [12]. Rows of pruned mulberry trees shade nearby dykes, feeding brisk domestic looms with bright silk [13].

A seven-day ride westward leads into Pianfu (Pingyang, near Linfen), another prosperous city humming with shuttles and bright silk skeins [14]. Between these twin hubs sprawl smaller boroughs whose bolder factors pack silk overland all the way to Indian fairs, returning with ivory, pearls, and rare spices along dusty caravan trails [15]. Two further days bring the traveller below the sheer stone bastions of Caichu (a hilltop castle whose site stays unfixed), raised long ago by the remembered Golden King [16]. Inside its chief keep stretches a tall painted chamber lined with solemn portraits of every king who held those towers before him, each ruler adding his own likeness to the gallery wall [17]. Down on the palace terraces, delicate maidens waited upon the Golden King and harnessed themselves lightly to draw his small carriage across courtyard flags whenever he rode out for evening air [18].

Yet any old soldier loitering by Caichu’s postern knows a sharper tale—not of silk carriages, but of how misplaced trust undid a fortress. Long ago, Prester John waged bitter war to drag the Golden King down from his cliffs, only to find the castle impregnable [19]. Then seventeen resolute squires of Prester John’s household volunteered to win him by guile. Riding up as foreign exiles begging honest wage, they served the Golden King dutifully for nearly two years until he trusted them past suspicion [20]. Out riding for sport one afternoon across a small stream with few grooms, those conspirators spurred close round his horse and drew cold blades. They swore they would cut him down on the bank unless he rode north as Prester John’s prisoner [21].

Prester John greeted his startled captive with grim satisfaction and sentenced the fallen lord to tend cattle out on windy pastures for two full years. Treated decently yet bowed to a herdsman’s crook, the king learned humility under open sky before soldiers brought him back to the royal tent. Prester John ordered rich cloth-of-gold robes laid across his shoulders and asked plainly whether he understood at last how helplessly his crown stood against greater power. Once the chastened prince swore fidelity, his host gifted him fresh warhorses, fine harness, and an honourable guard home to Caichu as a loyal ally [22]. Even though surviving Chinese chronicles of the Jin (“Golden”) dynasty hold no trace of that pasture captivity [23], you can feel why the castle’s storytellers loved it: it turns an impregnable mountain fortress into a moral fable about what a crown is worth without loyal hands around it.

Twenty miles west beyond the castle, pack-mules reach the muddy banks of the Caramoran (the Yellow River; Mongol Qara Mören). Deep, wide, and furiously swift, Polo judges the river too fierce for any bridge, rolling steadily out toward the distant Ocean Sea [24]. Brick-walled cities crowd along its flood plains, where flat barges pole raw silk and bales of dried ginger down the brown current [25]. Game swarms through nearby reeds—so thick that three plump pheasants sell for one Venetian groat, or the slightly weightier silver asper [26]—while dense stands of thick riverbank canes serve a dozen daily farm uses [27].

Two days across the yellow ferry rises Cachanfu (Puzhou), lively with merchant stalls and looms weaving gold-shot brocade [28]. Polo labels its townsfolk “Idolaters” after Cathay’s usual rule, offering brief notice of markets before swinging straight west onto the imperial post road [29]. Eight easy stages ahead, marching past tidy garden boroughs, green mulberry hedges, and open hunting grounds, stands Kenjanfu (Xi’an, ancient Chang’an), historic capital of a once mighty kingdom [30]. Village follows market town along the well-watered valley in unbroken rhythm, showing that imperial tax rolls run smoothly down this fertile corridor [31].

Kenjanfu now obeys Mangalai (Mangala), a capable son of the Great Kaan crowned as prince of this western territory [32]. Iron smithies, busy silk looms, and cheap grain stalls pack the old streets under his watchful garrison, and the former royal seat prospers steadily inside its imperial frame [33]. Ride out of the city across a plain watered by lakes, streams, and springs. Behind a high battlemented wall five miles in circuit stands Mangalai’s own palace, gleaming with vaulted halls lined in beaten gold [34]. Cavalry regiments camp beside royal deer parks near his gate, winning the young prince loud popular praise across the Book for fair justice and mild rule [35].

Pause outside those five-mile battlements for a sobering measure of time. Marco makes not a single mention of Kenjanfu’s famed Christian stele, carved in 781 and unearthed by workmen only in 1625 [36]. Look at those two dates—eight and a half centuries apart—and consider how easily a stone monument can sleep under rubble or slip past a visitor’s notice when no guide points to the wall. A traveller riding through an imperial capital sees the gilded palace and the busy armoury; what lies buried behind a monastery courtyard may wait centuries for a spade.

Three marches west beyond Mangalai’s palace lakes, flat cornland tilts steeply skyward into the jagged crags of Cuncun (the Qinling–Hanzhong mountains). Market towns vanish behind pine ridges where hardy hill farmers hunt mountain lions—actually leopards or tigers—alongside bears, lynxes, and forest deer for thick winter pelts [37]. Cozy timber inns still dot the narrow cliff track through wild woods. While major copies measure this rugged crossing at twenty arduous days, one rival tradition trims the pass substantially shorter [38]. Step out in your mind onto those terrifying cliffside gallery tracks (*zhandao*) anchored into sheer rock holes with timber beams and cross-planks, guiding breathless pack-horses high above foaming gorge rapids [39]. Look straight down between two wet pine planks at white water boiling three hundred feet below your stirrups, and you understand why a single pass can feel like twenty days in one rider’s memory and half that in another’s.

Step down from wet timber scaffolding onto level valley floor again, and two green days carry mules across Acbalec Manzi (probably the Hanzhong area), a prosperous plain squarely under the Kaan’s tax clerks [40]. Craftsmen hammer brass and weave cotton inside its neat villages, yet dry spicy ginger fuels the entire district’s fortune, packing out along caravan roads across northern Cathay [41]. Heavy wheat harvests and white winter rice grow so plentifully that a ginger pedlar travels light without packing his own flour [42], and the province shares its capital’s name—the “White City of the Manzi Frontier” [43].

Beyond Acbalec Manzi’s quiet orchards, the pack-train climbs once more into lonely twenty-day ranges of dark fir ridges, deep river gorges, and thick forests. Scattered valley hamlets live by farming buckwheat and herding goats, while hunters snare prized musk deer creeping through cool mountain birch woods [44]. At the twentieth dusk, cold granite cliffs open suddenly onto a wide watered garden plain surrounding Sindafu (Chengdu), lying close along the historic gate to Manzi [45].

Once upon a time, the Book recalls, Sindafu crowned an independent southern kingdom whose capital measured fully twenty miles around. Dying in ripe old age, an ancient king split his city among three jealous sons, and each brother fortified his own quarter behind a private inner wall inside the great outer circuit [46]. Yet walls cannot divide brothers forever against northern cavalry. Years later the Great Kaan’s armies stormed those gates and folded all three quarters back beneath one imperial seal [47].

Cutting straight through Sindafu’s heart surges the deep blue water Polo names as Kian-suy (the Min River system, joining the upper Yangtze), rolling eighty or a hundred days onward until it greets the eastern Ocean Sea [48]. Polo puts its breadth at half a mile within the walls—looking far more like a sheltered sea harbour stuffed with darting fish and rocking cargo masts than a landlocked river [49]. Across that humming channel stretches Sindafu’s crowning pride: a covered bridge on stone piers, half a mile long in the Book’s reckoning and seven paces wide, carried on carved marble columns beneath a brightly painted timber roof [50]. Morning carpenters nail open wooden shop-stalls down both flanks of the roadway to trade all day, then take the booths down again in the evening. Over one bustling gate sits the Kaan’s *Comercque*, collecting over a thousand pieces of fine gold a day while city weavers pack bales of glazed sendal for distant rivers [51].

Stand on that painted timber deck for a moment before the evening bell. Beneath your boots, river barges slide between stone piers; beside your elbow, a stallholder folds away his silk bolts and counts his copper cash; overhead, the customs clerk tallies the day’s gold. A city can hardly display its whole commercial soul more vividly than on a single crowded roofed bridge.

Drop a few copper cash onto the bridge toll-clerk’s table, leave Sindafu’s crowded painted roof behind, and point your pack-mule west into lonely hills bound five days onward toward Tebet (eastern Tibet). Before night falls twice down that wet rocky ascent, a merchant spoiled by covered silk booths along the bridge will be tearing down green cane stalks just to sleep warm beside a snapping bamboo fire [52].

## Notes

[1] ~ Dispatched westward by the Great Kaan, Marco estimates the westward journey at roughly four months [@yc, 2.35 n. 1]. Mangalai’s rule helps date the itinerary to the late 1270s, though modern commentators treat the tour as composite observation rather than one unbroken diary.

[2] ✓ *Pul-i-Sangīn* renders the Persian for “stone bridge,” transferring from the celebrated span onto the Hun / Yongding River below [@yc, 2.35 n. 2]. Yule compares Rashīd al-Dīn’s use of the name for the river. Polo describes merchants coming up from the sea and leaves the type of boat unspecified.

[3] ✓ Ten horsemen riding abreast across marble-panelled parapets guarded by carved stone lions accurately preserves the grandeur of the historic Lugou crossing [@yc, 2.35 n. 2]. Built in 1189–1192 and rebuilt several times since, the span still carries hundreds of miniature marble lions along its railings.

[4] ✗ Counting twenty-four arches and twenty-four watermills beneath the bridge contradicts historical surveys of the Lugou crossing [@yc, 2.35 n. 2]. The historic span rests on eleven main arches (later sources note thirteen openings across extended approaches), making Polo’s round figure a brisk visual multiplier.

[5] ✓ Juju is Zhuozhou (Cho-chau), standing roughly thirty miles south-west of Beijing along the old imperial post trunk [@yc, 2.36 n. 1]. Rashīd al-Dīn names the identical post station on the road out from Cambaluc.

[6] ~ Fine silks, gold-woven stuffs, and light taffetas stocked in Juju’s market streets belong to Polo’s commercial survey [@yc, 2.36 nn. 1–2]. Well-watered suburbs, vineyards, and bustling inns fit a prosperous road station feeding capital caravans.

[7] ✓ One mile south of Juju, the great stone trunk road split into a western branch toward Shanxi and an eastern highway toward Manzi [@yc, 2.36 n. 3]. Chapters II-9 and II-10 rejoin this exact fork when circling back from southern China.

[8] ~ Ten marching days across continuous villages, cornfields, and orchards set the pace westward from Juju to Taiyuan [@yc, 2.36–2.37]. The uniform praise of trade and handicrafts keeps the narrative gliding briskly across North China.

[9] ~ Ramusio-only in Yule. Ramusio inserts a handsome city called Acbaluc five days along the western road, sitting beside the Great Kaan’s hunting boundary [@yc, 2.37 n. 1]. Yule compares Zhengding (*Chêng-ting*), though he notes *Acbaluc* (“White City”) served as a recurring Turco-Mongol regional place label.

[10] ? Ramusio-only in Yule. Restricting hunting rights strictly to enrolled falconers and describing hares multiplying until Cublay led a great cull appear solely in Ramusio’s 1559 text [@yc, 2.37 n. 1]. No independent Yuan decree corroborates that specific hare campaign outside Zhengding.

[11] ✓ Taianfu is Taiyuan (T’ai-yüan fu), historic capital of Shanxi and a major centre for iron weapons, harness, and silk manufacture [@yc, 2.37 nn. 2–3]. Its arms workshops historically stocked northern Mongol garrisons.

[12] ✗ Claiming Taianfu as the sole wine-producing district across all Cathay overstates Shanxi’s vintage renown [@yc, 2.37 n. 3]. Yule documents medieval and classical vineyards flourishing in Ningxia, Shandong, and near Cambaluc itself, showing Marco generalized a local boast into an empire rule.

[13] ✓ Abundant mulberry gardens and silkworms thriving round Taiyuan belong to the Book’s reliable resource list [@yc, 2.37 n. 3]. Silk exports balanced the prefecture’s heavy metalwork trade.

[14] ✓ Pianfu is securely identified with Pingyang (modern Linfen) in southern Shanxi, sitting roughly seven marches down the Fen valley [@yc, 2.37 n. 4]. Its wealthy silk market matches the region’s historical importance under early Mongol rule.

[15] ~ Long-distance merchants carrying Pianfu wares all the way toward Indian fairs form Polo’s sweeping commercial claim [@yc, 2.37]. No local merchant ledger establishes direct Linfen-to-India overland caravans on that single name.

[16] ? Caichu’s exact location remains unresolved across editors’ maps [@yc, 2.38 n. 1; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 1, “Caiciu”]. Yule and Cordier considered Jizhou (*Ki-chau*), Jiezhou (*Kiai-chau*), and Taiching-kwan, while Pelliot tentatively favoured Jiangzhou (絳州) yet acknowledged that Polo’s two-day stage lengths suit none cleanly.

[17] ? Hanging portraits of earlier dynasties inside a grand hall echoes genuine Chinese royal-gallery traditions without authenticating Caichu’s site [@yc, 2.38]. Rustichello frames the portrait hall mainly as the stage for the Golden King’s downfall.

[18] ? Ramusio-only in Yule. Adding court maidens harnessed to pull the Golden King’s light courtyard carriage derives solely from Ramusio’s expansion [@yc, 2.38 n. 2]. The vignette dresses an obscure Shanxi hill castle in Renaissance courtly exoticism.

[19] ~ Attributing an ancient siege of the Golden King to Prester John belongs to Eurasian frontier folklore gathered along caravan tracks [@yc, 2.38]. Later medieval Europeans routinely cast “Prester John” as the great Christian rival of Central and East Asian kings.

[20] ~ Seventeen loyal courtiers infiltrating a rival household for nearly two years uses a classic romance motif [@yc, 2.38–2.39]. The slow patient deception shifts the story from fortress architecture to personal betrayal.

[21] ~ Seizing the unguarded prince beside a quiet ford isolates him just far enough from his own escort to leave resistance hopeless [@yc, 2.38–2.39]. Polo keeps the episode in third-person legend ascribed explicitly to castle locals.

[22] ~ Two years tending cattle in disgrace followed by royal robing and honourable release closes Rustichello’s moral parable on arrogance humbled [@yc, 2.39]. Such ceremonial reconciliation fits steppe literary etiquette better than audited military annals.

[23] ✗ Yule notes “Golden King” echoes the *Jin* (“Golden”) emperors of northern China, perhaps Emperor Xuanzong besieged by Mongols in 1214 [@yc, 2.38 n. 1]. Yet searches through Chinese Jin histories by Wylie and Cordier turned up no trace of any capture-and-herding episode.

[24] ✓ Caramoran reproduces the Mongol *Qara Mören* (“Black River”), Marco’s standard title for the unbridged lower–middle Yellow River [@yc, 2.40 nn. 1–2]. Its swift muddy volume precluded permanent bridges here until modern engineering.

[25] ✓ Cities crowding the fertile river corridor shipped silk bales and ginger along navigable reaches toward northern fairs [@yc, 2.40]. Commercial navigation complemented overland wagon tracks through Shanxi and Shaanxi.

[26] ~ Selling three fat pheasants for a single Venetian groat—carefully shifted in alternative texts to a slightly larger silver asper—illustrates roadside game abundance [@yc, 2.40 n. 3]. Polo repeatedly converts frontier plenty into memorable small-coin tavern rates.

[27] ~ Ramusio-only in Yule. Thick riverbank canes harvested along the Yellow River banks appear in Ramusio’s bracketed addition [@yc, 2.40]. Reed and cane stands historically served local basketry, matting, and riverbank dyke reinforcement.

[28] ✓ Cachanfu corresponds cleanly to Puzhou (*Pu-chau* / Yongji) standing close to the great Yellow River elbow [@yc, 2.40]. Gold-woven cloth and busy bazaars suit an old strategic river crossing.

[29] ~ Calling all locals “Idolaters” reflects the Book’s blanket religious sorting across non-Muslim Cathay [@yc, 2.40]. Polo lumps Buddhists, Daoists, and popular temples cleanly under one medieval catchall term.

[30] ✓ Kenjanfu renders the Mongol/Yuan name *Jingzhao fu* (*King-chao*), designating Xi’an (ancient Chang’an) eight stages west of the river bend [@yc, 2.41 nn. 1–3]. Its rich plains and historical memory of ancient Tang and Han sovereignty fit cleanly.

[31] ~ Continuous mulberry hedges, flourishing market gardens, and game parks track Marco’s transition down the fertile Wei River basin [@yc, 2.41]. The eight-day spacing aligns tolerably with overland stages into central Shaanxi.

[32] ✓ Mangalai (Mangala), third son of Cublay, genuinely governed the Anxi / Shaanxi frontier province before dying in 1280 [@yc, 2.41 n. 4]. His real historical tenure provides a precious chronological anchor inside Book II.

[33] ✓ Military armouries, fine silk weaving, and cheap provisions match Anxi’s role as the western garrison base controlling Sichuan and Gansu approaches [@yc, 2.41]. The city's workshops equipped the frontier armies stationed under Mangalai's command.

[34] ~ Plentiful springs, lakes, and a gilded palace enclosed within a five-mile circuit wall reflect Mangalai’s princely seat outside Xi’an [@yc, 2.41]. The Book supplies the circuit of the wall. It gives no distance from the city to the palace.

[35] ~ Praising Mangalai’s gentle justice and popular affection follows the Book’s standard portrait of dutiful Chinghisid governors [@yc, 2.41]. Court reputation here substitutes for independent provincial tax registers.

[36] ? Xi’an’s famous 781 Nestorian Christian stele was rediscovered by workmen in 1625 and stands silent in Polo’s pages [@yc, 2.41 n. 3; @yc, 2.35 n. 1]. Rockhill further notes that even the contemporary Nestorian monks Rabban Sauma and Mar Yahballaha passed across China omitting Chang’an, leaving open whether the slab stood buried or unnoted around 1278.

[37] ~ Cuncun denotes the steep Qinling–Hanzhong mountain barrier where wild timber forests sheltered leopards, tigers (“lions”), bears, and deer [@yc, 2.42]. Fur trapping and mountain inns kept narrow caravan trails open year round.

[38] ? Major manuscript families assign twenty days to crossing Cuncun’s forested ridges, though one tradition records a notably shorter interval [@yc, 2.42 n. 1]. Such numeric discrepancy points to differing stage estimates or northern vs central passes.

[39] ? Later travellers described plank-road cliff galleries (*zhandao*, such as Baoxie, Lianyun, Ziwu, and Tangluo passes) carried on timber beams socketed into limestone faces [@yc, 2.42 n. 1]. See [Map A10, the west road](ch-06-pulisanghin-sindafu.md#fig-a10-west-road) for alternative Qinling corridors and the northern tract placement.

[40] ✓ Acbalec Manzi corresponds regionally to the rich Hanzhong area guarding the approach out of the Qinling passes [@yc, 2.43 n. 1]. Heavy yields of wheat and winter rice confirm the plain’s contrast with rocky mountain stages behind.

[41] ~ Shipping dried ginger from Hanzhong farms northward across Cathay records a lively regional trade crop [@yc, 2.43 n. 1]. Yet pinning commercial origin down to one single town remains approximate.

[42] ~ Plentiful grain making rations dirt-cheap for ginger pedlars captures the abrupt transition from rugged passes down into warm river bottoms [@yc, 2.43]. For a caravan coming out of twenty days in the Qinling forests, the Hanzhong plain read first as a market where men and pack beasts could eat cheaply again.

[43] ~ Ramusio-only in Yule. The capital’s name is in the base account. Ramusio glosses the Turco-Mongol *Aq-Baligh* correctly as “White City of the Manzi Frontier” [@yc, 2.43 n. 1]. The exact walled town inside the Hanzhong basin stays open.

[44] ~ Twenty further days traversing lonely ridges rich in prized musk deer bring riders down out of Qinling–Daba highlands [@yc, 2.43 n. 2]. Scent-gland hunting formed one of western China’s most lucrative lightweight mountain exports.

[45] ✓ Exiting twenty-day gorges onto a lush irrigated plain matches the celebrated Chengdu basin surrounding Sindafu [@yc, 2.44 n. 1]. Calling its twenty-mile perimeter an imperial gateway anticipates the wealth of Manzi ahead.

[46] ? Local memory of a dying king dividing Sindafu among three sons, each walled separately inside one grand circuit, echoes the Three Kingdoms capital tradition [@yc, 2.44 n. 1]. No late-Song tripartite brother-partition matches Polo’s neat three-wall legend literally.

[47] ✓ Chengdu’s twenty-mile circuit walls and conquest by Mongol forces in the mid-thirteenth century rest on solid historical fact [@yc, 2.44 n. 1]. Mongol campaigns shattered Song Sichuan defences decades before Bayan’s final lower Yangzi sweep.

[48] ✓ Polo’s *Kian-suy* traces the Min and tributary river waters rushing through Chengdu down toward the upper Yangtze trunk [@yc, 2.44 n. 2]. Sailing eighty to a hundred days downriver to the eastern sea offers a realistic medieval merchant travel estimate.

[49] ~ Claiming a half-mile river channel inside Chengdu exaggerates combined canal branches and flood channels across the flat delta [@yc, 2.44 n. 2]. Dense working boat traffic on the Min system nonetheless left western observers astounded.

[50] ✓ Chengdu’s long covered timber-and-stone bridge—spanning five hundred paces on marble piers with bright painted eaves and collapsible wooden daily shop-booths—preserves a celebrated urban landmark [@yc, 2.44 n. 3]. Yule notes comparable roofed bridges stocked with daily timber stalls across traditional Sichuan towns.

[51] ~ Collecting over a thousand gold bezants per day at the bridge *Comercque* alongside bustling glazed sendal weaving captures vibrant local customs receipts [@yc, 2.44 nn. 3–5]. Daily gold figures act as vivid market scale rather than an audited tax sheet.

[52] ~ Five farm-country stages westward out of Sindafu carry the reader off the bridge stalls straight into bamboo-rich hills bordering Tebet [@yc, 2.44–2.45]. That wild bamboo belt opens the rugged highland road of Chapter II-7.
