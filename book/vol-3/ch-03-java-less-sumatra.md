# Chapter III-3
## Java the Less: Ferlec to Fansur — Sumatran Kingdoms

> *“When you leave the Island of Pentam and sail about 100 miles, you reach the Island of JAVA THE LESS… And in that kingdom [Samara] Messer Marco Polo was detained five months by the weather… In this kingdom of Fansur grows the best Camphor in the world called Canfora Fansuri.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 3, chs. 9–11 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

In the shoals by Pentam (probably Bintan), the ship had to lift its rudder. Put it down again for the next hundred miles. There’s an island beyond that passage which the Book calls Java the Less (Sumatra), and which it makes two thousand miles round at least [1]. Less is a name you can give it because you have another Java to reckon with. It takes a considerable length of coast to get from the first king named in the Book to the last one.

Out at night, somebody who knows the sky of the more northerly seas may look for the Pole Star. In the Book he won’t find it here [2]. It is a small thing to lose from the whole sky until you’ve used it to tell you where north is. If you’ve come from much farther north, you’re used to finding it a convenient distance above the horizon. Here the horizon is where you’d have to look for it, and the conditions right down there matter. Polo repeats the observation at Samara (Samudera Pasai) and adds that he can’t make out the northern constellation he calls the Maestro either. The Book numbers eight kings for the island and has a language for each. It will do business with six of them. The other two lie on the side Polo says he never visited [3]. There is plenty for a merchant in the part we’re offered, including fragrant wood, spikenard and spices whose names might never have been heard around a European quay.

At Ferlec (Perlak) the first people worth watching are the merchants. Polo has Muslim traders coming often enough to bring the townspeople over to Islam [4]. The relationship continues when a ship leaves and another one takes its berth. In the hills he gives the people a quite different religion, chosen, he claims, by the first thing they see when they get up in the morning. He also passes on an allegation of cannibalism [5]. His account knows how trade changes a town. It has rather less to tell us about how it came by its stories of the people farther inland.

Basma (possibly the Pasai area) has a ruler of its own and people who declare themselves subjects of Cublay (Kublai) Kaan. Polo makes a useful distinction here. A thing worth showing at court might be sent to the Kaan occasionally. That leaves the cost of levying regular tribute, and his officers are very far away [6]. The Book can put the ruler’s name in a man’s mouth long before it can put a tax collector on the beach.

Among the elephants of Basma, the Book finds another animal nearly as large. It has bristly hair, feet that remind the teller of an elephant and a low head like a boar’s, with a thick black horn [7]. He leaves the creature in the mud it likes to lie in. It’s a rhinoceros, though the number of horns and some of its other properties have given people who know the animals something to argue about. A unicorn is the creature somebody in Europe thinks he knows, at least well enough to imagine a young woman soothing it with her lap. Polo has no patience with that part of the story [8]. You may believe he is right without putting him personally in front of the beast.

In Polo’s account its tongue is rough enough to do harm. Ramusio has it pin a person with its knees before the tongue comes into play [9]. If you’re trying to decide what sort of rhinoceros this is, even a reliable description of the head would be worth considerably more than that scene. The Book remembers a thing it can start an argument about and keeps the old name until someone finds it a better one.

— I’d gladly keep the mud in the picture. After the horn and the story of the maiden, there’s an animal with its own business to get on with.

There are black goshawks and monkeys of different kinds. One kind of little monkey gives the Book an opportunity to warn its audience about a purchase. A man can strip away most of the hair, dry and stuff the body and colour it with saffron, then offer the result as a tiny human being from India [10]. In the story the merchant who has paid for it hopes to make a profit by bringing home a marvel. Polo would have him look at what somebody has done to the monkey before he spends his money.

---

At Samara, the wind puts a stop to the voyage. Polo has to stay for five months [11]. At sea you can choose the size of your ship and the cargo in it. It takes a wind with a different mind from the one you need to put all that money to use getting you nowhere. Five months is a long time to have to keep people fed in a place where a man might have reckoned on buying a few provisions and putting out again.

The French account and the V version have the travellers set up wooden defences [12]. In the Zelada Latin and Ramusio, the party is about two thousand strong and there are ditches on the landward side, with the sea at the ends of the works [13]. The reason the Book gives for the preparations is fear of people it casts as man-eaters. What the party makes for its own security and what it imagines about people outside the camp belong to the same telling.

Look at the party from the other side of the timber. In Ramusio’s account there is ample wood for the works, and the people who live here trust the newcomers enough to bring them food [14]. The fish is excellent, Polo says, and the people eat rice. For a ship’s company that has to live here for months, the difference between a locked gate and a market matters more than the thickness of a single plank.

A pot hung where a branch has been cut can be full by the next day. From a palm that looks like a little date palm the Book gets a drink in white and red. When the palm has stopped yielding, it has the people water its roots and get more from it [15]. You don’t need a cellar of grapes if you know which tree can supply a pot. Coconut gives the visitor a drink he can get without a day’s wait. In the passage Yule takes from Ramusio, the liquid in it is clearer than you might expect and tastes better than wine [16]. A man can have a long time to learn what people eat and drink while he’s waiting for a fair wind. You can make two different reckonings of the same five months. For a passenger it’s a delay. For a man who has to keep a lot of people fed, it’s a season in which families who live near the shore have something to sell nearly every day.

The Book puts a rich king in Samara and tells of people who claim to be the Kaan’s subjects. It makes them people of the religion it calls idolatry [17]. There is evidence for an early Muslim ruler in the region, close in time to the proposed voyage. There is also a later traveller’s account of a Muslim court. Neither lets us date what Polo heard by assuming everyone round the harbour became Muslim on the same day.

---

Dagroian (a NE Sumatra coastal polity, site uncertain) is the next kingdom the Book puts on the coast. It has its own ruler and language, but it professes allegiance to the Kaan too [18]. The story Polo has for a listener here begins with a family asking whether a sick person will get well. If the judgement goes against him, the Book has the family send for people to end his life. It says his kin eat him as part of the funeral [19]. The reason given concerns the worms that would feed on what was left and die for want of more. Their deaths, according to the story, would weigh on the dead man. It’s a cruel kind of care in the telling, and the telling is what we have.

The bones go into a chest and into a cave beyond the reach of animals [20].

A captive from abroad, in the Book, is killed and eaten if he cannot pay a ransom [21]. Here the thing that can save him is money in his own hand. The two stories tell on different fears. Neither establishes how the people of this coast treated their dead or their prisoners.

At Lambri (Lamuri), come back to something you could buy. Polo gives this coast camphor and plenty of a wood called brazil. In his account the growers move the seedlings and leave them to grow three years before pulling them up [22]. He claims to have brought some seed to Venice and had it sown there. Nothing came up, and he guessed the place was too cold [23]. It’s the sort of experiment you might try if you knew what the wood was worth and had spent long enough watching people grow it. It tells at least as much about how desirable the dye was as it does about the seed.

| Name or property | Asian sappanwood | American brazilwood |
|---|---|---|
| Geography | Asian source of red dyewood; Polo calls the wood of Lambri brazil | Distinct Atlantic-forest tree from South America |
| Dye | Contains brazilin | Also contains brazilin |
| Limit of the comparison | Sappanwood is the usual reading of the trade name; Polo’s seed gives no specimen | A shared dye and name can belong to different plants |

If you ask the Book what else lives inland, a rhinoceros is now one of the more ordinary things it can give you. Polo repeats a tale of people in the hills with bare tails a palm long [24]. Ships can put into the same harbour for many years and there can still be a sale for a good story about what’s beyond it. A piece of wood on the deck and a tail in a tale have very different journeys home.

At Fansur (Barus), the best camphor is worth its weight in gold by the Book’s reckoning [25]. Let a man who’s been to the scales tell you what he eats after he’s come away from them. There is rice, with flesh and milk, and the drink from trees again. And inside a great trunk with quite thin bark is something you can make into bread. What the Book calls flour is the food we know as sago, made from the pith [26]. The work is somewhere between the tree and the bread. A man who is used to watching flour come from a mill can still recognise what his host does with it when it reaches the hearth. Polo puts himself and his company among the people who have tried the bread [27]. Having heard about pulling up trees for their dye and taking a drink from a cut branch, we can end by putting a different part of a tree to use for supper.

The bread is finished. To get a bearing for the next islands, go back up the coast to Lambri. There’s one small island the Book names as it leaves Java the Less. See how long you can keep it in view.

## Notes

[1] ~ The Book distinguishes the island from Java by calling it Java the Less. The name and the circuit belong to the navigation and descriptions available to Polo, as discussed by Yule [@yc, 3.9 n. 1].

[2] ✗ The northern star is low from this latitude and can be hard to see in haze. The scale in [Diagram C10, the Pole Star](../media/diagrams/C10-pole-star.pdf) distinguishes low visibility from a claim of absolute invisibility [@yc, 3.9; @yc, 3.10 n. 2].

[3] ~ The eight kings make a scheme for the Book’s description. It acknowledges leaving two of the kingdoms out because Polo had not been there, while the six accounts mix reports and claims of personal experience [@yc, 3.9 n. 2; @yc, 3.11].

[4] ✓ Yule identifies Ferlec with Perlak and cites evidence for the early Islamisation of the region. The picture of merchants converting townspeople belongs to the Book’s account of how it came about [@yc, 3.9 n. 3].

[5] ? The Book’s sweeping claims about the inland people include allegations of cannibalism and a story about worshipping the first thing seen in the morning. Yule’s comparisons give the allegations a context and leave the local practices behind them uncertain [@yc, 3.9 n. 3].

[6] ~ Yule compares the names of different polities and discusses the identification of Basma. The Book distinguishes the claimed allegiance from tribute and mentions presents sent from the island [@yc, 3.9 n. 4].

[7] ~ The body, mud and horn point to a rhinoceros, with the species uncertain. Talbot suggests the description could draw on both Sumatran and Javan animals, an interpretation of the language in the account. Kovesi reads the animal as Sumatran [@yc, 3.9 n. 5; @talbot1959unicorn; @kovesi2025rhinoceros].

[8] ✓ The Book explicitly rejects the story of the unicorn tamed by a maiden. The description in Yule and in Eusebi’s edition of the French text leaves open whether Polo personally saw the animal [@yc, 3.9 n. 5; @eusebi2018devisement].

[9] ~ Ramusio-only in part in Yule’s edition. The rough tongue already appears in Yule’s base narrative, while the pinning with the knees is bracketed. Eusebi’s French edition restores some tongue wording from V, and Talbot suggests the lower incisors could account for the description [@yc, 3.9 n. 5; @eusebi2018devisement; @talbot1959unicorn].

[10] ~ The saffron and the way the hair is dressed belong to Polo’s report of a commercial fraud. He uses the account to deny that the alleged miniature people were travellers brought back from India [@yc, 3.9].

[11] ~ The Book dates the enforced stay at Samara by its duration, five months, and blames the winds. Identifying it with the Prologue’s stop at “Java” during Cocachin’s voyage is a reconstruction. See [Chapter III-9, the return voyage](ch-09-cocachin-return.md) [@yc, 3.10 n. 1; @yc, Prol. 18].

[12] ~ Wooden defences appear in the French text and in V, as shown in Simion’s comparison. The shared detail belongs to the account of the landing [@simion2019devisement, § 3.2.3, p. 141].

[13] ~ Zelada and Ramusio. The figure of about two thousand and the ditches occur in both versions in the printed comparison. The figures and ground plan remain the witnesses’ account of the camp [@yc, 3.10; @simion2019devisement, § 3.2.3, p. 141].

[14] ~ From Ramusio in Yule’s edition. The plentiful wood and the islanders supplying the party are in Yule’s brackets. Their presence across other witnesses has yet to be checked [@yc, 3.10].

[15] ~ Tapping palms for a drink is part of the daily life the Book describes during the stay. Polo gives the practice of watering the root when the flow weakens, and Yule compares several accounts of palm products [@yc, 3.10 n. 3].

[16] ~ From Ramusio in Yule’s edition. Yule brackets the size, appearance and praise of the coconut liquid. The base passage already supplies the nuts themselves [@yc, 3.10].

[17] ~ The island polity’s identification with Samudera places the story close to the rise of a documented Muslim dynasty. An inscription records Malik al-Salih’s death in 1297 and there is debate about whether the extant markers are contemporary or replacements. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa reports a Muslim court decades later (Lee’s translation, ch. XXII) [@yc, 3.10 n. 1; @lambourn2008tombstones; @ismail2022batuaceh].

[18] ? The identification of Dagroian is unresolved. Yule compares names and descriptions associated with the northern coast without settling the position of the reported kingdom [@yc, 3.10 n. 4].

[19] ? The judgement about the sick person and the explanation about worms give the reported rite its particular form. Yule compares stories in several traditions, which leaves both the literal account and its connection to any local practice uncertain [@yc, 3.10 n. 5].

[20] ? The burial in chests comes from the same account as the funeral. The Book has a similar description of setting remains in inaccessible caves in its account of Coloman [@yc, 3.10 n. 5; @yc, 2.58].

[21] ? The ransom story is a separate allegation about captured outsiders. It belongs to the Book’s maritime tales of the hazards of landing [@yc, 3.10 n. 5].

[22] ~ Lambri is generally associated with Lamuri. “Brazil” is a trade name for a red dyewood, usually understood here as Asian sappanwood. The American tree called brazilwood also contains brazilin [@yc, 3.11 n. 1; @yc, 3.9 n. 2].

[23] ~ The claim about bringing back seed and trying it at Venice comes from the Book. Polo puts the failure down to the temperature, with the reason left as his inference [@yc, 3.11].

[24] ✗ The claim about tails is a geographical marvel. Yule assembles accounts of the motif from other travellers and literary traditions [@yc, 3.11 n. 2].

[25] ~ Arabic accounts, cited by Yule, give Fansur a reputation for camphor. The exchange for an equal weight of gold is Polo’s way of pricing the best grade [@yc, 3.11 n. 3].

[26] ✓ The flour made from inside a tree fits sago processing. The description leaves the exact species of palm open [@yc, 3.11 n. 4].

[27] ~ Eusebi’s edition of the French text gives the claim of eating the bread in the travellers’ first person. That is a separate claim from the account of the source of the flour [@yc, 3.11; @eusebi2018devisement].
