# Chapter III-3
## Java the Less: Ferlec to Fansur — Sumatran Kingdoms

> *“When you leave the Island of Pentam and sail about 100 miles, you reach the Island of JAVA THE LESS… And in that kingdom [Samara] Messer Marco Polo was detained five months by the weather… In this kingdom of Fansur grows the best Camphor in the world called Canfora Fansuri.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 3, chs. 9–11 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

Down sinks the heavy axial rudder once your junk clears the pale shoals off Pentam (probably Bintan) and Malaiur (possibly Malayu in the Jambi region). A hundred miles across open sea lifts Java the Less (Sumatra) dark green along the horizon, an island Marco measures at two thousand miles or more around [1]. Sailors name it “the Less” only because greater Java lies astern. Along this sweeping, cloud-capped shoreline crowd eight separate kingdoms, each guarded by its own crowned king and its own spoken tongue. Precious spices, fragrant lignaloes, and costly spikenard fill its ports—rare goods, Marco notes with pride, that scarcely ever reach Christian markets back home.

Out on the high poop deck after sunset, one steady comfort has slipped completely off the rigging: the Pole Star has dropped below the rim of the sea [2]. Losing that familiar northern lamp above the bowsprit teaches any Mediterranean helmsman in half a watch how far south the monsoon carries him. Later at Samara (Samudera Pasai), Marco checks the night sky again and watches even the bright seven-star Wain sailors call the *Maestro* dip clean out of sight [3]. Out of eight kingdoms ringed round this coast, the Book walks you briskly through six and leaves the far two politely blank because Marco admits he never laid anchor there [4]. Within the six harbours he touches, merchants unpack fragrant woods, camphor, and rich red dyewoods scarcely ever seen upon Venetian quays.

At the start of the coast lies Ferlec (Perlak), where cargo ships and Muslim preachers arrive on the same breeze. Frequent sea merchants sailing out of western ports have converted Ferlec’s busy townspeople cleanly to Islam [5]. Gaze past palm thatch toward steep green ridges inland, however, and Marco sketches a much wilder country. Lawless hill-dwellers live like wild beasts without order, eat clean and unclean meat including human flesh, and worship whatever bird, dog, or ox crosses their eyes at daybreak for the rest of that day [6]. His notebook sees plainly how regular shipping turns a harbour bazaar toward mosque prayer, while his tales of fierce highlanders behind the mist echo the nervous dockyard yarns sailors swap beside hanging lamps.

Westward lies Basma (possibly the Pasai area), where an independent lord speaks his own tongue yet proclaims himself a vassal of Cublay (Kublai) Kaan [7]. Hold back before picturing Mongol tax-collectors wading ashore over coral sand with inkpot and seal. Now and then a rare white falcon or curious wild animal sails north to Cambaluc (Beijing) as an exotic curiosity, Marco explains wryly, yet no steady tax coin ever crosses the ocean because imperial bailiffs live far too distant to gather it [8].

Inside Basma’s soggy jungles roam herds of wild elephants alongside another thick-skinned giant nearly as tall. Picture this: stiff black buffalo hair caked thick with river mud, broad round feet like an elephant’s, one stout black horn rising on its brow, and a heavy boar-like snout pressed perpetually into creek silt [9]. Meet the Book’s real unicorn—an ugly, short-tempered rhinoceros whose muddy bulk mocks every graceful white tapestry hung in Venice. It never gores with its horn, Marco insists, tearing skin instead with a tongue studded in long stiff prickles. Nor could any pure maiden ever coax this swamp monster to slumber inside her lap [10]. In Ramusio’s fuller scene, an enraged beast knocks a victim flat and crushes him beneath heavy knees before that spiny tongue goes to work [11]. Whichever manuscript sits open on your desk, Java the Less leaves Marco with a stubborn mud-wrestler rather than an ivory maiden’s lap-pet.

— Keep that creek mud firmly in view. Every painter in Europe could draw a snow-white horn resting inside silk skirts; only a dockside trader remembers how rankly a cornered rhinoceros smells of river slime.

Basma’s dark forest trees also shelter large jet-black goshawks as big as ravens, prized by court fowlers, alongside noisy troops of monkeys. One small monkey species suffers a brazen harbour fraud [12]. Crooked dealers trap tiny human-faced monkeys, pluck away all fur except the chin beard and chest hair, smoke-dry and stuff each delicate skin, rub it bright yellow with saffron, pack the corpse neatly inside a cedar box, and hawk the shrunken body to gullible captains as a dried Indian pygmy [13]. Do not count silver coin before turning that stitched hide toward daylight, Marco cautions every buyer—nobody across all India ever bred miniature folk no taller than a spice chest.

---

Further west at Samara, shifting monsoon squalls bolt the sea gate tight. Dark rain-clouds pile over headlands for weeks on end, and contrary gales pin Marco’s ships inside the bay for five whole months [14]. Every merchant captain can choose stout fir planking, tarred cordage, and salt-beef casks before clearing Zayton (Quanzhou). No sailor on earth bargains with a monsoon gale once the sky breaks. Five trapped months ashore turn a brief watering stop into a sprawling, anxious beach settlement where ships’ companies must haul timber, build walls, watch the bush, and feed hundreds of hungry mouths every noon while waiting for the sky to clear.

In the Franco-Italian manuscript and the Venetian V witness, cautious shipmates drag fallen trees off the shingle to pile up stout timber defences [15]. Both the Zelada Latin and Ramusio swell that stranded force to two thousand men digging deep ditches from sea to sea across a sandy cape, fortified behind five tall wooden watchtowers [16]. Hear the dull thud of axes biting wet wood and the scrape of iron spades across coastal sand in every surviving witness. Fear of inland man-eaters drives the ditch-diggers hard before dusk, turning restless deckhands into diligent fort-builders before night drops over the palms, and keeping sentries awake till dawn.

Over those timber breastworks in Ramusio’s sunnier pages, armed panic relaxes abruptly into lively daily trade. Coastal woods yield ample timber right beside the ditch, and neighbouring islanders grow trusting enough of the strangers to walk right up to the gate balancing woven baskets of silvery sea-fish, fine white rice, and fresh fruit [17]. Watchtowers keep order after dark while sentries pace the planks. By daylight, an open market right outside the trench keeps two thousand mariners healthy until the wind swings round. Nobody pulls cleaner or sweeter fish out of any water on earth, Marco insists, while northern wheat vanishes off shipboard tables in favour of fresh steamed rice.

When salt fish dries every throat, Samara’s tappers walk effortlessly up small date-like palms instead of tending stony hillside vineyards. Barefoot in the crown, a tapper slices a green bough and hangs an earthen jar beneath the wound. Within a day and a night, sweet red or white palm-toddy brims the clay lip—so bracing that Ramusio swears it heals dropsy, consumption, and spleen—and if sap slows down, splashing buckets of fresh water round the roots coaxes fresh liquor upward again [18]. Prefer cool refreshment without waiting for dawn? Split a thick green coconut as round as a man’s head. Ramusio lingers fondly over the cup, boasting its clear water drinks sweeter than fine wine before a spoon scrapes pale crisp nut-meat from the shell [19]. Five trapped months read very differently across two ledgers: to a restless passenger they drag like wasted weather; to a ship’s purser they form five steady months of fish-stalls, toddy-pots, and dry logs.

Marco puts a rich king in Samara who hails Cublay as overlord while his folk worship idols [20]. Yet close to that exact decade, an inscribed royal gravestone marks Muslim ruler Malik al-Salih’s death at Samudera in 1297, and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa walked into a thriving Muslim sultanate there fifty years later [21]. Strait ports shifted sovereigns and raised mosques faster than a monsoon-bound traveller watching beach fences could survey every village behind the palms.

---

Down the coast lies Dagroian (a NE Sumatra coastal polity, site uncertain), another independent kingdom bowing lazily toward Cambaluc [22]. Here Marco sets down a chill bedside oracle. When sickness strikes a house, relatives summon temple sorcerers to announce whether the patient can recover. If they foresee healing, attendants leave him quietly to mend. Should their omen seal his doom, kinsmen fetch special stranglers who pile heavy cloths across the sick man’s face until breath stops. Next the kin boil his body in an iron cauldron and strip every rib cleanly down to the marrow so not a grain of moisture lingers to breed maggots—lest any hungry worm should starve and torment the dead man’s soul [23]. Once the feast finishes, clean white bones go into neat hardwood chests carried high into lonely mountain caves where wild animals never reach [24]. Meanwhile any unransomed foreign sailor washed off the reef faces a brisker death round cooking fires [25]. Two stark tales sit side by side—one spun from eerie care toward departed ghosts, the other warning captains never to step ashore with an empty purse in their belt.

Bright market daylight returns at Lambri (Lamuri). Plantations here drip fragrant camphor and rear orderly fields of red brazilwood (Asian sappanwood). Growers set tender shoots in damp loam and nurse them three full years before dragging up bright crimson roots for dyers’ vats [26]. Practical to the last ounce, Marco boasts that he packed a pouch of brazilwood seed home to Venice and sowed it in warm courtyard earth, blaming cold northern winters when not a single shoot broke ground [27]. Even when climate defeated him, shipping foreign dye-seed across two oceans shows how sharply a Venetian merchant weighed cargo mark-ups against garden dirt.

| Dye | Contains brazilin | Also contains brazilin |

Behind Lambri’s ridge, sailors whisper one last mountain marvel: shy highland men sporting smooth hairless tails a full palm long and thick as a hound’s [28]. A log of red dyewood stands rough inspection back on Rialto; a tailed mountaineer thrives strictly beside swinging galley lamps.

The six kingdoms close at Fansur (Barus), renowned across eastern shipping logs for the purest white camphor on earth. Its *Canfora Fansuri* smells so clear and sharp that buyers weigh every spoonful grain for gleaming grain against fine gold coin in Marco’s ledger [29]. Away from the goldsmith’s scale, supper bakes beside the palms. Loggers fell giant wide-trunked palms wrapped in thin grey bark, split the tall trunks down the centre, and scoop out massive cores stuffed with fibrous white pith. Next women stir that pith through wide troughs of clear stream water until heavy white starch sinks cleanly to the bottom. Once drained and kneaded over hot stone hearths, that palm-pith flour bakes into crisp flat loaves tasting wonderfully like warm barley bread [30]. Right beside that oven, the Franco-Italian manuscript sets third-hand hearsay firmly aside and puts warm bread in the travellers’ own mouths, insisting Marco and his fellows ate sago cakes themselves and found them delicious [31].

Off Lambri’s headland, beyond the sago ovens of Fansur, floats a tiny marker islet pointing straight across open swell toward naked canoe-traders, mastiff-faced islands, and a steep granite peak scaled by riveted iron chains.

## Notes

[1] ~ Java the Less denotes Sumatra, distinguished from Great Java behind it [@yc, 3.9 n. 1]. Polo's two thousand miles reflects mariners’ coastal reckoning rather than true survey mileage.

[2] ✗ Claiming the Pole Star vanishes completely mistakes low-latitude horizon haze for absolute invisibility, since northern Sumatra still lies four to five degrees north of the equator [@yc, 3.9; @yc, 3.10 n. 2]. See [Diagram C10, the Pole Star](ch-06-mutfili-kesmacoran.md#fig-c10-pole-star).

[3] ~ Repeating at Samara that the *Maestro* (the Plough / Ursa Major) likewise disappears extends the astronomical observation along the northern Sumatran coast [@yc, 3.10 n. 2]. At five degrees north, the lower stars of the constellation dip below the northern horizon during part of the year.

[4] ~ Dividing the island among eight kings while claiming to have visited six—and candidly setting aside the far two—pairs local market notes with clear limits on the fleet's landfalls [@yc, 3.9 n. 2; @yc, 3.11]. All six named polities cluster around the northern and north-eastern littoral of Sumatra.

[5] ✓ Ferlec corresponds to Perlak on the Aceh coast [@yc, 3.9 n. 3]. Polo notes a striking social divide there: foreign Muslim merchants had recently converted the port townsfolk to Islam, whereas the inland villagers kept their older animist customs.

[6] ? Charging Ferlec’s hill-dwellers with worshipping the first thing seen each morning and eating human flesh echoes coastal merchant rumour without independent confirmation [@yc, 3.9 n. 3]. Port traders across the archipelago routinely painted interior hill peoples as savages.

[7] ~ Basma plausibly points near Pasai (Pacem) on the northern coast [@yc, 3.9 n. 4]. Yule compares local Malay polity names while showing how fluidly such royal titles sat along the Sumatran shore.

[8] ✓ Separating rare diplomatic curiosities sent to Cambaluc from regular tax tribute realistically measures the outer limit of Yuan overseas authority [@yc, 3.9 n. 4]. Northern Sumatran rulers sent occasional envoys with local marvels to court without submitting to an imperial tax register.

[9] ~ Mud-wallowing bulk, elephant feet, hog-like head, and a single black forehead horn describe a rhinoceros [@yc, 3.9 n. 5]. Talbot considers both Sumatran and Javan traits behind the language, whereas Kovesi identifies the beast with the Sumatran species [@talbot1959unicorn; @kovesi2025rhinoceros].

[10] ✓ Attributing injury to the prickly tongue rather than the horn and dismissing Europe’s maiden-tamed unicorn sit inside Yule’s base narrative [@yc, 3.9 n. 5]. Both Yule and Eusebi’s French edition leave open whether Marco saw the beast personally or described a captured specimen [@eusebi2018devisement].

[11] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Base witnesses carry the harsh rasp-like tongue (linked by Talbot to the animal's sharp lower incisors and traced in V by Eusebi), while Ramusio adds pinning prey beneath its heavy knees [@yc, 3.9 n. 5; @eusebi2018devisement; @talbot1959unicorn]. Both details dispel the graceful unicorn of European bestiaries.

[12] ✓ Basma’s black goshawks and small monkeys appear in the Book’s regional wildlife tally beside the rhinoceros [@yc, 3.9]. The tropical fauna caught the eye of a court official accustomed to Cublay's northern falconry and menageries.

[13] ~ Shaving, smoke-drying, stuffing, and saffron-staining small monkeys to sell to gullible western buyers as dried Indian pygmies exposes an actual maritime curio fraud [@yc, 3.9]. Polo takes evident pleasure in warning European collectors how easily port taxidermists manufactured monsters.

[14] ~ Samara’s five-month weather halt anchors Polo’s stay on northern Sumatra [@yc, 3.10 n. 1]. Tying that monsoon wait directly to the Prologue’s “Java” stop on Cocachin’s return voyage is an editorial reconstruction [@yc, Prol. 18]. See [Chart C11, the monsoon and voyage calendar](ch-09-cocachin-return.md#fig-c11-monsoon-cocachin).

[15] ~ Timber camp defences appear already in both the Franco-Italian F manuscript and the Venetian V witness, as Simion shows [@simion2019devisement, § 3.2.3, p. 141]. The stockade was not invented in the sixteenth century.

[16] ~ Zelada- and Ramusio-only. Both the Zelada Latin and Ramusio raise the landing force to roughly two thousand men behind sea-anchored ditches and five wooden blockhouses [@yc, 3.10; @simion2019devisement, § 3.2.3, p. 141]. Keeping the two witness layers distinct shows how the camp fortification grew across manuscript families.

[17] ~ Ramusio-only. Plentiful beach timber and local villagers trusting the camp enough to trade fish and rice daily enter Yule’s brackets from Ramusio [@yc, 3.10]. The addition turns a tense palisade watch into a practical harbour market.

[18] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Tapping palm boughs into clay pots for white and red toddy and watering sluggish roots belong to the base account, whereas Yule brackets Ramusio’s medical praise against dropsy, phthisis, and spleen [@yc, 3.10 n. 3]. Fermented palm wine was an everyday staple for crews waiting out the monsoon.

[19] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Base texts mention coconuts the size of a man’s head, while Ramusio supplies the sweeter-than-wine liquid praise and white nut-meat [@yc, 3.10]. The fuller gloss explains a tropical fruit to Italian readers who knew only dried imported nuts.

[20] ~ Describing Samara’s townsfolk as idolaters loosely under Cublay captures Polo’s passing impression from the beach camp [@yc, 3.10 n. 1]. Compare Note 21 on the royal Islamic transition taking place at Samudera in this very decade.

[21] ~ Royal tombstones record Muslim ruler Malik al-Salih’s death at Samudera in 1297, though Lambourn and Ismail debate whether the extant stones are contemporary imports or later replacements [@yc, 3.10 n. 1; @lambourn2008tombstones; @ismail2022batuaceh]. Half a century later Ibn Baṭṭūṭa visited an established Muslim court at the same port.

[22] ? Dagroian stays unpinned along north-eastern Sumatra despite Yule’s and Pelliot's linguistic comparisons [@yc, 3.10 n. 4; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, pp. 613–615]. None of the proposed coastal rivers or districts matches the name with certainty.

[23] ? Oracular death sentences, suffocation, ritual feasting on the deceased, and fear of starving corpse-worms weave classic funerary marvels without local confirmation [@yc, 3.10 n. 5]. Yule compares similar endocannibalism stories told of remote island tribes from antiquity onward.

[24] ? Sealing cleaned skeletal bones inside wooden chests in high mountain caves echoes the Book’s similar cliff-burial notice for Coloman in [Chapter II-9](../vol-2/ch-09-caugigu-cuiju.md) [@yc, 3.10 n. 5; @yc, 2.58]. Cave ossuaries were known in parts of the archipelago, though Polo attaches them here to a cannibal feast tale.

[25] ? Executing and eating unransomed foreign castaways stands separately as a harsh maritime tale about hostile shores [@yc, 3.10 n. 5]. Sailors coasting northern Sumatra drew a sharp line between safe treaty ports and unvisited headlands.

[26] ~ Lambri matches Lamuri near Banda Aceh [@yc, 3.11 n. 1]. The three-year transplanted “brazil” bushes reflect Asian sappanwood (*Biancaea sappan*), which shared the red brazilin dye with the South American brazilwood named after it centuries later [@yc, 3.9 n. 2].

[27] ~ Packing dyewood seed back to Venice and failing to raise shoots in cooler lagoon soil stands as Polo’s unverified personal experiment [@yc, 3.11]. See the botanical comparison table in this chapter.

[28] ✗ Palm-long smooth tails on inland hill-folk repeat a classic Eurasian marvel motif gathered by Yule [@yc, 3.11 n. 2]. Coastal informers regularly attributed tails to forest peoples or mistook hanging bark and skin loincloths for anatomy.

[29] ~ Arab geographers cited by Yule confirm Fansur (Barus on Sumatra's north-west coast) as the prime origin of top-grade *Fansuri* camphor [@yc, 3.11 n. 3]. Pricing the crystalline resin at equal weight in fine gold reflects its luxury status in Asian pharmacy.

[30] ✓ Felling wide-girthed palms, scooping out the pith, washing the starch in water troughs, and baking barley-like flatbread accurately describe Sumatran sago preparation [@yc, 3.11 n. 4]. Processing sago starch required careful leaching and decanting before the meal could hit the griddle.

[31] ~ Eusebi’s French edition puts eating the sago bread into the travellers’ first-person plural, claiming direct taste in camp rather than second-hand rumour [@yc, 3.11; @eusebi2018devisement]. The detail anchors the end of the Sumatran stay in ships' stores and mess tables.
