# Chapter III-3
## Java the Less: Ferlec to Fansur — Sumatran Kingdoms

> *“When you leave the Island of Pentam and sail about 100 miles, you reach the Island of JAVA THE LESS… And in that kingdom [Samara] Messer Marco Polo was detained five months by the weather… In this kingdom of Fansur grows the best Camphor in the world called Canfora Fansuri.”*
> — Book III.ix–xi (Yule–Cordier)

**[V/M]**

---

From Pentam and the shoal throat of the Malay world, Marco Polo narrates, a hundred miles more of sailing bring the voyage to an island the Book names Java the Less — though for all that lesser name it has a circuit of two thousand miles or more. This is Sumatra under an older Archipelago name the Arabs and Malays still shared: *Jawa*, *Jawi*, applied both to the great southern island and to this northern giant. Beyond Malaiur’s market the southern Ocean road does not yet open India; it opens eight crowned kings, eight tongues, treasure and spice, and a latitude so far toward the midday sun that the North Star, little or much, is never to be seen. [1]

Upon this Island, he says, there are eight kingdoms and eight crowned kings. The people are all Idolaters — save where Saracen merchants have already begun their work, as Ferlec will show — and every kingdom has a language of its own. The Island hath great abundance of treasure, with costly spices, lign-aloes and spikenard, and many others that never come into our parts. Of the eight, six lie at this side or end of the Island — the northern run — and of those six the Book will speak. Of the other two, on the farther side, Messer Marco Polo never was there, and so the telling will leave them in silence. Soft premise here: not a wonder-catalogue of Chipangu’s gold roof, but a merchant’s and a castaway’s Sumatra. [2]

When you leave the Island of Pentam and sail about a hundred miles, you reach Java the Less, and first among its kingdoms the Book sets Ferlec.

This kingdom, according to the book, is so much frequented by the Saracen merchants that they have converted the natives to the Law of Mahommet — the townspeople only, for the hill-people live for all the world like beasts, and eat human flesh, as well as all other kinds of flesh, clean or unclean. And they worship this, that, and the other thing; for in fact the first thing that they see on rising in the morning, that they do worship for the rest of the day. [3]

So Ferlec stands as the hinge of Islam on this coast: Arab and Gujarati sails already thick enough to remake the towns; a hinterland the Book still paints as beast-custom and morning-idol. Perlak and Diamond Point, the Malay chronicles’ Parlák, and the Batta hill-peoples of later ethnography sit beside this compressed contrast — and the Book catches early the coastal conversion that Ibn Battuta, half a century on, will find triumphant at Samudra. [4]

When you quit the kingdom of Ferlec, Polo continues, you enter upon that of Basma. This also is an independent kingdom, and the people have a language of their own; but they are just like beasts without laws or religion. They call themselves subjects of the Great Kaan, but they pay him no tribute; indeed they are so far away that his men could not go thither. Still all these Islanders declare themselves to be his subjects, and sometimes they send him curiosities as presents. There are wild elephants in the country, and numerous unicorns, which are very nearly as big. [5]

They have hair like that of a buffalo, feet like those of an elephant, and a horn in the middle of the forehead, which is black and very thick. They do no mischief, however, with the horn, but with the tongue alone; for this is covered all over with long and strong prickles, and when savage with any one they crush him under their knees and then rasp him with their tongue. The head resembles that of a wild boar, and they carry it ever bent towards the ground. They delight much to abide in mire and mud. ’Tis a passing ugly beast to look upon, and is not in the least like that which our stories tell of as being caught in the lap of a virgin; in fact, ’tis altogether different from what we fancied. [6]

— That unicorn is a rhinoceros seen by a Venetian eye and named by a Latin memory. Marco *sees* the beast and *rejects* the Physiologus maiden legend, yet keeps the name and “one horn” — though Sumatra’s *Rhinoceros sumatranus* is typically two-horned. The tongue-prickle belief is old and wide. Anyway, next he mentions monkeys and a trade fraud. [7]

There are also monkeys here in great numbers and of sundry kinds; and goshawks as black as crows. And when people bring home pygmies which they allege to come from India, ’tis all a lie. For those little men are manufactured on this Island: a kind of monkey which is very small and has a face just like a man’s; they pluck out all the hair except beard and breast, dry and stuff them, and daub them with saffron until they look like men. But nowhere in India nor anywhere else were there ever men seen so small as these pretended pygmies. [8]

When you leave the kingdom of Basma you come to another kingdom called Samara, on the same Island. And in that kingdom Messer Marco Polo was detained five months by the weather, which would not allow of his going on. And here again neither the Pole-star nor the stars of the Maestro were to be seen, much or little. The people here are wild Idolaters; they have a king who is great and rich; but they also call themselves subjects of the Great Kaan. [9]

When Messer Mark was detained on this Island five months by contrary winds, according to the book, he landed with about two thousand men in his company; they dug large ditches on the landward side to encompass the party, resting at either end on the sea-haven, and within these ditches they made bulwarks or stockades of timber, for fear of those brutes of man-eaters — for there is great store of wood there; and the Islanders having confidence in the party supplied them with victuals and other things needful. There is abundance of fish to be had, the best in the world. The people have no wheat, but live on rice. Nor have they any wine except such as comes from a certain kind of tree: when they want wine they cut a branch and attach a great pot to the stem; in a day and a night they will find the pot filled. This wine is excellent drink, white and red. The trees resemble small date-palms; and when cutting a branch no longer gives a flow, they water the root, and before long the branches again give out wine as before. They have also great quantities of Indian nuts as big as a man’s head, sweet and savoury when fresh, the inside filled with a liquor like clear fresh water, but better to the taste than wine or any other drink that ever existed. [10]

So Samara is the autobiographical hinge of Volume III’s Sumatran run: five months of monsoon detention; two thousand men behind ditch and stockade; Idolater hosts who feed the strangers even as the Book fears man-eaters inland; rice, toddy from the gomuti palm, coconuts like a man’s head; and the same vanished northern sky. Samudra–Pasai, Malik al-Ṣāliḥ’s rising house, Chinese *Sumutala* among the 1286 tribute names, and Ibn Battuta’s later zealous court sit beside Polo’s still-pagan picture — a pre- or early-conversion moment, likely on the return stage around 1292. This wait is the same monsoon logic Sung ships knew at Lambri, and that the Cocachin fleet will one day inherit. [11]

When you leave the kingdom of Samara you come to another which is called Dagroian. It is an independent kingdom, and has a language of its own. The people are very wild, but they call themselves the subjects of the Great Kaan. And the Book will tell a wicked custom of theirs. [12]

When one of them is ill they send for their sorcerers, and put the question whether the sick man shall recover or no. If they say that he will recover, then they let him alone till he gets better. But if the sorcerers foretell that the sick man is to die, the friends send for certain judges of theirs to put to death him who has thus been condemned. These men come, and lay so many clothes upon the sick man’s mouth that they suffocate him. And when he is dead they have him cooked, and gather together all the dead man’s kin, and eat him. And they do suck the very bones till not a particle of marrow remains; for they say that if any nourishment remained in the bones this would breed worms, and then the worms would die for want of food, and the death of those worms would be laid to the charge of the deceased man’s soul. And so they eat him up stump and rump. And when they have thus eaten him they collect his bones and put them in fine chests, and carry them away, and place them in caverns among the mountains where no beast nor other creature can get at them. And if they take prisoner a man of another country, and he cannot pay a ransom in coin, they kill him and eat him straightway. It is a very evil custom and a parlous. [13]

— Cannibal customs here are topos and ritual misreading mixed. Batta law, Herodotus’s Paddaei, Odoric’s Dondin, and the long topos of endo-cannibal care sit against simple atrocity, without pretending the Book’s horror is only a trope. The site is soft — near Pedir, or the tattooed folk of Chinese coast-books. [14] Anyway, soft sail west now: Lambri of the headland, and Fansur of the camphor.

When you leave that kingdom you come to another which is called Lambri. The people are Idolaters, and call themselves the subjects of the Great Kaan. They have plenty of camphor and of all sorts of other spices. They also have brazil in great quantities. This they sow, and when it is grown to the size of a small shoot they take it up and transplant it; then they let it grow for three years, after which they tear it up by the root. And you must know that Messer Marco Polo aforesaid brought some seed of the brazil, such as they sow, to Venice with him, and had it sown there; but never a thing came up — and the Book fancies it was because the climate was too cold. [15]

Now you must know that in this kingdom of Lambri there are men with tails; these tails are of a palm in length, and have no hair on them. These people live in the mountains and are a kind of wild men. Their tails are about the thickness of a dog’s. There are also plenty of unicorns in that country, and abundance of game in birds and beasts. [16]

You then come to another kingdom which is called Fansur. The people are Idolaters, and also call themselves subjects of the Great Kaan; and understand, they are still on the same Island. In this kingdom of Fansur grows the best camphor in the world, called *Canfora Fansuri*. It is so fine that it sells for its weight in fine gold. [17]

The people have no wheat, but have rice which they eat with milk and flesh. They also have wine from trees such as has been told. And another great marvel: they have a kind of trees that produce flour, and excellent flour it is for food. These trees are very tall and thick, but have a very thin bark, and inside the bark they are crammed with flour. And Messer Marco Polo, who witnessed all this, related how he and his party did sundry times partake of this flour made into bread, and found it excellent. [18]

There is now no more to relate of those six kingdoms that lie at this side of the Island. Of the other two on the farther side the Book will tell nothing, for the said Messer Marco Polo never was there. Howbeit the greater part of Java the Less has been told; so now the voyage may quit it, toward a very small Island that is called Gauenispola, and afterward toward the Nicobar and Andaman chain that Volume III has not yet opened.

Thus the Sumatran kingdoms stand in order: Ferlec’s Saracen towns and beast-hills; Basma’s elephant and the unicorn that is a rhinoceros; Samara’s five-month stockade under a starless north; Dagroian’s funerary cannibal charge; Lambri’s brazil seed that would not grow in Venice, and tailed mountain men; Fansur’s gold-weight camphor and the sago tree whose flour the party ate as bread. Soft close here: the monsoon that held two thousand men at Samara is the same logic of fair and foul wind that will one day delay a princess’s fleet; the Kaan’s name claimed without tribute is the polite fiction of distance; and Java the Less, more accurately measured than Java the Great, has been entered under the mixed tag of sight, detention, and report.
