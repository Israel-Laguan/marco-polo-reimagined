# Chapter III-10
## Ca’ Polo, *Milione*, and the City That Would Not Believe

> *“And when the story got wind in Venice, straightway the whole city, gentle and simple, flocked to the house to embrace them… And as it happened that in the story… of the magnificence of the Great Can, he would speak of his revenues as amounting to ten or fifteen millions of gold… so they gave him the nickname of MESSER MARCO MILLIONI.”*
> — Giovanni Battista Ramusio, preface to *Navigationi et viaggi*, vol. 2 (1559), in Yule–Cordier, Introduction, § 5

**[reception]**


---

At the door in Venice (Venezia) the relations want to know who the three men in rough travelling clothes are. Ramusio tells the story more than two centuries after the return. In his telling their kin have taken them for dead. Their faces have altered, their Venetian has nearly gone and their clothes look as foreign as their speech. It is as if Ulysses had come home to Ithaca and found people in the house who wished to know what business he had there. Ramusio gives the Polos a house near San Giovanni Grisostomo and an audience who are hard to persuade that it is theirs [1].

— Ramusio had a man’s word for the tale. Gasparo Malipiero, the old senator who used to tell it, had lived among the buildings of the court and heard it from his father, grandfather and neighbours. Ramusio was young when he heard Malipiero. A story told that close to the door can be very certain of where everybody stood [2].

In the same telling the Polos invite their kin to dinner. In they come in crimson satin. As soon as the guests have washed and taken their seats, the Polos change into crimson damask. They have the satin cut up and given to the servants. After a few dishes they reappear in velvet and give away the damask. At the end of the meal they put on clothes like the guests’ and the velvet goes too. A guest who thought he had seen where this was leading now finds himself looking again at three men who are dressed as he is [3].

Once the table is cleared and the servants have gone, Marco fetches the clothes they arrived in. The three men slit the seams. Rubies, emeralds and other stones fall out where the plates were. In Ramusio’s version that settles who they are. A fortune that could have tied them to a packhorse has travelled stitched into the cloth, for which a thief on the road or a relative at the door would have found little use. The news goes into the streets and the neighbours flock to the house. There is no other account to set beside Ramusio for what was eaten at that dinner or what the household was given to wear [4].

### The name in the city

Ramusio keeps the young Venetians coming round to ask Marco what he saw. Every description of the Kaan’s wealth, it seems, runs into millions, and the young men have the word ready to use the next time they see him. Jacopo d’Acqui offers a different account of the name and puts a million *lire* in the family’s own hands. Fontanini thinks of the many tales that fit inside the book [5]. A tale about numbers told at the door and an entry in a book kept by the city have different things to give us here. Pozza identifies the Marco Polo Milion in a confraternity list of 1319 as the traveller, as Bolognari reports [6].

You can see the uses to which a name that everybody knows might be put. Put it beside the office of a respectable Venetian who can stand surety for a man with a penalty to pay, and the name may distinguish him from other members of his family. Put it beside a man who is telling a story about an empire, and it can give the audience an idea of the kind of sum that will be coming next. Yule uses a 1305 entry about a surety, whose identification has been questioned. What the pronunciation told the first man who used it still escapes us [7].

### A household and a few pounds of musk

The merchant in a 1311 judgment is after some musk. Marco Polo of San Giovanni Grisostomo has given Paulo Girardo a pound and a half to sell, on terms that they share the profit. Girardo sells half a pound and brings back the rest less a *saggio*. The court gives judgment for Polo for the price of the half-pound, the value of the missing fraction and the costs [8]. Here is a sum he can have settled by weighing. Once the agent hands over the unsold part, the merchant needs to put it back on the scales, compare it with what he gave out and ask what has happened to the money for what was sold. We even have the price Yule gives from the judgment, six *lire* of grossi to the pound. It takes a customer willing to pay that much before a little missing weight is worth going after. At the far end of the route someone paid something for the musk before the merchant in Venice had his share to argue for. A later case concerns rights to use stairs and porticoes near his house [9]. There is property from Donata to take into account. In May 1323 the dispute goes before the Doge and the officials hearing the case. It matters which part of the house comes through which family, even to someone who uses the stair every day. Anyone looking for Marco’s business in Venice can see how much a quarrel worth taking to court could depend on a very small space or a very small weight.

His wife was Donata, whom Gullino identifies as the daughter of Vitale Badoer. Their wedding year is uncertain [10]. The documents give the household the three daughters named in Marco’s will: Fantina, Bellela and Moreta [11]. Fantina married Marco Bragadin. Bolognari identifies Bertuccio Querini as Bellela’s husband through Gallo’s edition of records concerning masses. Gullino gives Moreta two marriages, first to Ranuzzo Dolfino and later to Tommaso Gradenigo [12]. A will of 1319 is reported to name another woman, Agnese, as the daughter of a Marco Polo. The identification of her father with the traveller has been proposed and remains uncertain [13]. It makes a considerable difference which Marco is named when a new paper turns up with one name on it. The same concern applies to a nickname jotted on the back of a list.

### What people took away

Thibault de Cepoy was in Venice on business for Charles of Valois and his wife, whose claim to the empire of Constantinople needed allies. Cepoy and Pierre le Riche concluded a treaty with Venice in December 1306 [14]. In August 1307, according to a note in two surviving copies, Marco gave the knight a copy of his book [15]. Picture the man who wants to know about the empire beyond the one he has come to Venice to discuss. He has asked the man who was there for his account. It is a record of what one reader wanted and a claim about what the author wanted in return, to have things he had seen made known beyond his own city. We get the month through the note, as well as the names. To the man making a copy the distinction between a book a friend has lent him and something that came from the traveller is worth setting down on the page. The note makes a further claim, that the copy the French knight received was the first taken of the work. It belongs to what the note’s writer wanted people who saw that copy to understand about it.

The manuscript note follows the book into the hands of Cepoy’s son Jean, who has a copy made for Valois and gives copies to friends who ask for them [16]. Yule was interested in the note because it says whose hands the book went through. A copy kept by one man can account for part of the road a text took between two places. Were you to receive another one from a friend of his, the work might have been changed already by someone whose name you never have occasion to learn. And a man given a copy at the house of a patron can have different reasons for opening it from the man who asked the traveller for it in the first place.

In Bologna, the Dominican Francesco Pipino put a version into Latin, probably in the 1310s and while Marco was still alive. Ramusio put the work in 1320 and Yule suggests 1315 [17]. Read the beginning as someone asking why a friar would go to the trouble of translating a merchant’s tales. Pipino vouches for the man’s Christianity, and the countries described in the book are places his readers can hope to know more about for purposes of their own [18]. To a reader in a religious house, the names of peoples who might hear the Gospel can mean more than the number of days a merchant needs between their towns. With a version in Latin the friar can introduce it to a reader whose route to those towns goes through a library. In his chronicle he draws on the Book for Mongol wars and the destruction of the Old Man of the Mountain. In a Modena manuscript he identifies the Venetian whose work he has translated [19]. A story can leave the territory of a knight’s romance for a religious reader’s library in the time it takes to translate a manuscript.

Pietro d’Abano found a man he could ask about the southern sky. In the *Conciliator*, a work of medicine, he disputes the notion that the equatorial regions cannot be inhabited. Marco, he tells us, describes a great faint object with a tail, and draws its shape. He can also give Pietro the height above the horizon at which he reckons to have seen the southern pole, by likening it to the length of a soldier’s lance [20]. The sight is still debated. Bottin proposes a meeting in Venice, around 1300–03, and considers how to read the celestial description [21]. To the man making an argument about people actually living where a reader was taught they could not live, the detail of what they can see from there can do more than enliven a description.

The poet who made *Bauduin de Sebourc* had different things to get out of the Book. Yule heard echoes of it in the poet’s Old Man of the Mountain and in the precious things burned for the use of the dead. A stone from a church in a story told about Samarcand turns up in an adventure in the country of Baudas [22]. Try putting the knight, the friar and the poet at the same table and reading aloud to them. They would not all ask the reader to keep the same place. Among the early readers we can name, the interest in what can be learnt from a Venetian’s journey coexists with an appetite for what can be made out of it. It is harder to know how many people who could read a book of travels ever had a copy of this one in their hands [23].

### January 1324

A priest and notary named Giovanni Giustiniani takes Marco’s will on 9 January 1324. Under the Venetian reckoning, in which the year begins in March, it is still 1323. The man making the will is ill. He appoints Donata, Fantina, Bellela and Moreta to carry out his wishes and chooses San Lorenzo for burial [24]. He leaves provision for Donata and his daughters and makes gifts to religious houses and others. One clause frees a man named Pietro, described as of the Tartars, remits what he has gained by his work and leaves him a hundred Venetian *lire* in denari [25]. The writer has told us what is to happen to Pietro and leaves us to wonder how he came into the household.

Jacopo d’Acqui brings people to the dying man and has them ask him to withdraw what they take for the more unlikely things in his book. Marco says he has told less than half of what he saw [26]. A reader who wanted him to take back a story must think what to say to a man who would have him sit down for a longer book. It is a line that could be repeated to people who had never seen the manuscript. The account of the death comes to us through Jacopo, and the particular request and reply depend on his testimony. There is an earlier assurance of the truth of the travels associated with the elder Maffeo that Bolognari sets beside it [27].

Marco died on 8 or 9 January 1324. The surviving will and the chronology of the inventory give the limits within which the documentary editors place his death [28]. In the inventory drawn up after his death there is a large gold tablet of command among quilts, a garment, musk, aloe wood and rhubarb. A gold *bocheta* set with stones and pearls is a jewel in the new reading of the inventory [29]. One man has just set down the people he expects to carry on the affairs of his household. Another has had to go round the house and put a price on the things in it. A book may tell you why a messenger is glad to have a tablet in his hand. Here the tablet is an article to be counted along with the other things someone will have to take care of.

Yule puts the burial in San Lorenzo, in accordance with the will. What could once be seen at the old church included the sarcophagus of Marco’s father, whose name a visitor could read. The church was rebuilt from its foundations in 1592. The place of Marco’s grave cannot now be pointed out with the assurance with which he named the church to the man making his will [30].

— People who come looking for his face have put their faith in pictures made well after his death. Some of the figures with his name attached tell us a good deal about when they were made and what people hoped the traveller would look like. A visitor who can say who painted a picture has still some way to go before he can say who sat for it [31].

By 1375, someone with a brush in hand had found a way to give those names a place where a reader could see them.
