# Chapter III-10
## Ca’ Polo, *Milione*, and the City That Would Not Believe

> *“And when the story got wind in Venice, straightway the whole city, gentle and simple, flocked to the house to embrace them… And as it happened that in the story… of the magnificence of the Great Can, he would speak of his revenues as amounting to ten or fifteen millions of gold… so they gave him the nickname of MESSER MARCO MILLIONI.”*
> — Giovanni Battista Ramusio, preface to *Navigationi et viaggi*, vol. 2 (1559), in Yule–Cordier, Introduction, § 5

---

Tradition has it that when Messer Nicolo, Messer Maffeo, and Messer Marco had at last come again to Venice (Venezia), the Prologue’s long arc was closed, and another story — half memory, half legend — began to gather about their door. The Book has told the princess voyage as autobiography; the city’s welcome belongs to a different order of testimony. Soft caution at the start: this chapter’s richest tableau is also its least documentary. [1]

According to Giovanni Battista Ramusio, writing two centuries and more after the event, the three gentlemen suffered the fate of Ulysses at Ithaca: recognised by nobody. Through the duration and hardships of their journeys they were quite changed in aspect, and had got a certain indescribable smack of the Tartar both in air and accent, having indeed all but forgotten their Venetian tongue. Their clothes were coarse and shabby and of a Tartar cut. They proceeded to their house in the confine of San Giovanni Grisostomo (the parish of St John Chrysostom) — the house then a lofty and handsome palazzo, afterward known as the Corte del Millioni. Finding it occupied by relatives who flatly refused to believe that these shabby strangers were the very gentlemen of the Ca’ Polo long counted among the dead, the three devised a scheme. [2]

— Ramusio heard the tale as a youngster from the aged senator Gasparo Malipiero, whose house stood midway among the buildings of that Corte; Malipiero had it from father, grandfather, and old neighbours — a Venetian street’s memory, not a notary’s parchment. Yule called it interesting tradition, like a bit out of the Arabian Nights; no archive has yet made it into fact. [3] Anyway, next tradition mentions the feast.

They invited a number of their kindred to an entertainment prepared with great state in that house. When the hour arrived for sitting down to table, they came forth clothed in crimson satin fashioned in long robes such as people in those days wore within doors. When water for the hands had been served and the guests were set, they took off those robes and put on others of crimson damask, whilst the first suits were by their orders cut up and divided among the servants. Then after partaking of some of the dishes they went out again and came back in robes of crimson velvet, and when they had again taken their seats, the second suits were divided as before. When dinner was over they did the like with the robes of velvet, after they had put on dresses of the ordinary fashion worn by the rest of the company. Then, all servants ordered to retire, Messer Marco, as the youngest of the three, rose and brought forth the three shabby dresses of coarse stuff which they had worn when they first arrived. Straightway they took sharp knives and began to rip up some of the seams and welts, and to take out of them jewels of the greatest value in vast quantities — rubies, sapphires, carbuncles, diamonds, and emeralds — stitched up in those dresses so artfully that nobody could have suspected the fact. For when they took leave of the Great Kaan they had changed all the wealth that he had bestowed upon them into this mass of jewels, well aware of the impossibility of carrying so great an amount in gold over a journey of such extreme length and difficulty. The exhibition of that huge treasure threw the guests into fresh amazement; and now they recognised that these were in truth those honoured gentlemen of the Ca’ Polo, and so all paid them the greatest honour. When the story got wind in Venice, the whole city, gentle and simple, flocked to the house to embrace them. [4]

On Messer Maffeo, the eldest in Ramusio’s telling, they conferred an office of great dignity; whilst the young men came daily to ask Messer Marco about Cathay and the Great Can, which he answered with such courtesy that every man felt himself his debtor. And as he would speak of the Can’s revenues as ten or fifteen *millions* of gold, and likewise of other vast wealth always in *millions*, they gave him the nickname of Messer Marco Millioni — a thing Ramusio claimed to have noted also in the Public Books of the Republic. The Court of his House has from that time been known as the Court of the Millioni. [5]

For the sobriquet *Il Milione* — and the House and the Book that took it after him — stands in documents as well as in street talk. Different writers have given different explanations. Some, beginning with his near-contemporary Fra Jacopo d’Acqui, ascribed it to the family’s having brought home a fortune of a million of *lire*, in fact to their being millionaires; Sansovino and later Venetian antiquaries followed that wealth-nickname. More far-fetched is Fontanini’s notion that the name was given to the Book as containing a great number of stories, like the *Cento Novelle* or the *Thousand and One Nights*. But there can be little doubt that Ramusio’s is the natural explanation: the young wits of the city bestowed it on Marco because of his frequent use of a word then unusual, in his attempts to convey the vast wealth and magnificence of the Kaan’s treasury and court. Soft dispute already: *Milione* as wealth, as catalogue of tales, or — in later mouths — as “a million lies.” The insult reading is popular and late; the archives prefer fortune or the traveller’s own arithmetic of millions. What they confirm is the style itself: on the tenth of April 1305 the Great Council names as surety the *Nobilis Vir Marchus Paulo Milioni* — the Noble Marco Polo the Million. Long after his death, it is alleged, Venetian masques still put forward a Marco Milioni who told Munchausen-like stories to divert the vulgar. Soft sting: such, if true, was the honour of the prophet among his own populace. [6]

Between nickname and legend lay the ordinary life of a Venetian merchant of substance. Soft turn now: from Ramusio’s crimson robes to musk, stairs, and a wife’s name in a will. When Marco married is uncertain, but no doubt early in the fourteenth century; by January 1324 he had two married daughters besides one unmarried. His wife’s Christian name was Donata; her family is not assured, though a Loredano connexion has been suspected. Under 1311 stands the only paper that shows him plainly as a practical trader: a Court of Requests judgment for the Noble Marco Polo of San Giovanni Grisostomo against Paulo Girardo of Sant’Apollinare. Marco had entrusted him, on half profits, a pound and a half of musk at six *lire* of *grossi* the pound; Girardo sold half a pound, returned a pound short by a *saggio* — one-sixth of an ounce — and accounted for neither. The Judges cast him in the amount with costs, and gaol if unpaid. Soft merchant breath: the man who had spoken of the Kaan’s millions sued for a missing sixth of an ounce of musk. Again in May 1323, probably within a year of his death, Ser Marco appears — perhaps only by attorney — before the Doge respecting stairs and porticoes touching his house and his wife’s property in San Giovanni Grisostomo. The Corte del Milione was not only a nickname; it was masonry and contested thresholds. [7]

His daughters were Fantina, Bellela, and Moreta. Fantina married Marco Bragadino; Moreta, Ranuzzo Dolfino; Bellela’s husband’s name does not appear in the short series that survives. Soft family colour: three heirs in the female line, no son of the Traveller’s body to carry the name forward in the male. Ramusio, compressing and erring in detail, spoke of two girls only — Moreta and Fantina — and of a father Nicolo who, fearing Marco’s long Genoese captivity, took a new wife and begot three sons; the archives know instead Nicolo’s illegitimate sons Stefano and Zannino, and an uncle Maffeo whose own death fell between 1309 and 1318. Soft correction without killing the romance: the house lived, traded, litigated, and married as Venetian houses do, while the Book took a life of its own. [8]

For the Book did not wait in a single chest. Soft rise of the manuscript trail: within the Traveller’s lifetime a copy left Venice for France by a knight’s hand. Charles of Valois, brother of Philip the Fair, having married Catharine, heiress of the titular Latin emperors of Constantinople, set up his claim to the Empire of the East and was prompted by Pope Clement V. In 1306 Charles and his wife sent as envoys to Venice a noble knight, Thibault de Cepoy (Thibaut / Thiebaud de Chepois), along with an ecclesiastic of Chartres called Pierre le Riche; they executed a treaty of alliance dated the fourteenth of December of that year. During Thibault’s stay he seems to have made acquaintance with Marco Polo and to have received from him a copy of his Book. A curious note preserved on two existing manuscripts — the Paris text Fr. 5649 and the substantially identical Bern copy — records the gift in August of 1307: the Lord of Cepoy requested a copy from Sire Marc Pol, burgess and resident of Venice; and the said Sire Marc Pol, being a very honourable person, of high character and respect in many countries, because of his desire that what he had witnessed should be known throughout the world, and also for the honour and reverence he bore to the most excellent Prince Charles, Count of Valois, gave and presented to the Lord of Cepoy the first copy that was taken of his said Book after he had made the same. Soft pride of the note: very pleasing it was to him that his Book should be carried to the noble country of France and there made known by so worthy a gentleman. From that copy Messire John, Thibault’s eldest son and later Sire de Cepoy, after his father’s decease had a copy made and presented that first French-made copy to Monseigneur de Valois; thereafter he gave copies to such of his friends as asked for them. Soft fan of transmission: Venice to Cepoy; Cepoy into France; son to Valois; friends thereafter — one documented stem of the French tradition. [9]

Meanwhile another stem took Latin and clerical dress. Friar Francesco Pipino of Bologna, a Dominican, turned a version of the Book into Latin in the later years of Marco’s life — Ramusio thought about 1315–1320 — and prefaced it with assurances of the traveller’s Christian faith and of the work’s usefulness against the incredulous. Soft clerical spread: Pipino’s Latin moved among friars and chroniclers as the French moved among courts and knights. Pipino’s own Chronicle drew Tartar wars and the destruction of the Old Man of the Mountain from Polo; a Modena manuscript of that Chronicle still names Marcus Paulus the Venetian, who passed nearly twenty-seven years in the Tartar countries and whose book Pipino had translated out of the Lombardic vernacular. Giovanni Villani of Florence, when alluding to the Tartars, would later send the curious reader to Friar Hayton and also to the Book called *Milione* made by Messer Marco Polo of Venice, who tells much about their power and dominion, having spent a long time among them. Soft caution of Larner and of honest count: contemporary or near-contemporary references by name are fewer than later fame suggests — Cepoy, Pipino, Jacopo d’Acqui, Villani, Pietro d’Abano, John of Ypres — and the medieval readership, though real, was never the universal European possession that print-age myth prefers. Delight and distrust travelled together. [10]

Among those who knew the man, not only the Book, stands Pietro d’Abano (Peter of Abano), celebrated physician and philosopher of Polo’s own generation. In a medical discussion controverting the old notion that the Equatorial regions were uninhabitable, Pietro writes that in the country of the Zinghi there is seen a star as big as a sack; he knows a man who has seen it, and who told him it had a faint light like a piece of cloud and is always in the south. Soft witness of science: “I have been told of this and other matters by Marco the Venetian, the most extensive traveller and the most diligent inquirer whom I have ever known.” Marco saw that star under the Antarctic; he described it as having a great tail, and drew a figure of it; he told also that he saw the Antarctic Pole at an altitude above the earth apparently equal to the length of a soldier’s lance, whilst the Arctic Pole was as much below the horizon; from that place they export camphor, lign-aloes, and brazil; the heat is intense and the habitations few; and these things he witnessed in a certain island at which he arrived by sea. Soft join to Volume III’s southern ocean: the physician’s star is the traveller’s Canopus-or-Magellanic wonder spoken in a Paduan consulting room — distrust of equatorial emptiness answered by a Venetian who had sailed where the northern stars sank. [11]

Elsewhere the Book entered fiction without its author’s name. The poetical Romance of *Bauduin de Sebourc*, ascribed to a rather early date in the fourteenth century, shows a thorough acquaintance with Polo and borrows themes largely from him — funeral burning of goods and servants for the dead man’s use in the other world; the Old Man of the Mountain’s paradise of spice-rivers, silk, and maidens; the stone of the Christian church at Baudas (Baghdad) that recalls Polo’s Samarkand miracle; and other colours lifted from the Traveller’s pages into bantering romance. Soft double life of the text: friars cite *Milione* for Tartar dominion; poets strip it for adventure; physicians ask the man about stars; and the city that nicknamed him still half refused to believe. [12]

We catch sight of our Traveller only once more in the flesh. Soft descent now toward the will. It is on the ninth of January, 1324 — by the Venetian calendar, which began the year on the first of March, the document’s “1323” is our 1324 — and he is labouring with disease, under which he is sinking day by day. He has sent for Giovanni Giustiniani, Priest of San Proculo and Notary, to make his Last Will and Testament. In the Name of the Eternal God Amen: Marcus Paulo of the parish of St John Chrysostom, finding himself to grow daily feebler through bodily ailment, but by the grace of God of a sound mind and of senses and judgment unimpaired, constitutes as his Trustees Donata his beloved wife and his dear daughters Fantina, Bellela, and Moreta. Soft bequests of a Christian merchant: the proper tithe; above the tithe, two thousand *lire* of Venice denari distributed — twenty *soldi* of Venice *grossi* to the Monastery of St Lawrence where he desires to be buried; three hundred *lire* to his sister-in-law Ysabeta Quirino that she owes him; forty *soldi* to each of the Monasteries and Hospitals all the way from Grado to Capo d’Argine; remissions and gifts to the Convent of Santi Giovanni e Paolo of the Order of Preachers and to named friars; five *lire* to every Congregation in Rialto and four to every Guild or Fraternity of which he is a member; twenty *soldi* to the notary that he may pray the Lord in the testator’s behalf. Soft human particular: he releases Peter the Tartar, his servant, from all bondage as completely as he prays God to release his own soul from all sin and guilt, remits whatever Peter may have gained by work at his own house, and over and above bequeaths him a hundred *lire* of Venice denari. The residue of the two thousand he directs to be distributed for the good of his soul according to his trustees’ discretion. To Donata, over and above her settlement, eight *lire* of Venetian *grossi* annually during her life, with the linen and household utensils and three beds garnished. All other property movable and immovable he bequeaths to Fantina, Bellela, and Moreta equally, Moreta first receiving the same as each of the others hath received for dowry and outfit. Soft anathema of the close: if any one shall presume to infringe this Will, may he incur the malediction of God Almighty and abide bound under the anathema of the 318 Fathers, and furthermore forfeit five pounds of gold to the Trustees. [13]

We do not know how many days or weeks Marco survived the making of this will; from a scanty series of documents commencing in June of the following year we know that by then he had been some time dead. Soft burial: he was buried, no doubt, according to his declared wish, in the Church of San Lorenzo (St Lawrence); Sansovino bears confused testimony to the fact. There does not seem to have been any monument to Marco himself, though the sarcophagus which he had caused to be erected to his father Nicolo existed till near the end of the sixteenth century in the porch leading to the old church, and bore the inscription *SEPULTURA DOMINI NICOLAI PAULO DE CONTRATA S. IOANNIS GRISOSTEMI*. The church was renewed from its foundations in 1592, and then, probably, the sarcophagus was cast aside and lost, and with it all certainty as to the position of the tomb. Soft fade of the family: Bellela was deceased by 1333; Moreta by then married; Donata dead by 1336; Fantina assessed still as a resident of San Giovanni Grisostomo on the forced loan for the Genoese war of 1379. The male line of the Traveller’s own body had never begun; collateral Polos and the nickname on a courtyard outlived the man. [14]

Of portraits there is none with any claim to authenticity. Soft dispute of relics and images: the quaint figure in the earliest printed editions makes no such pretension; a picture long set down to Titian is probably a work of about 1600; the medallion in the Sala dello Scudo of the Ducal Palace is imagination painted by Francesco Griselini in 1761; statues, medals, mosaics — Genoa’s Municipio sets Polo opposite Columbus — are honours of later centuries, not likenesses taken from life. False relics and pious attributions gather as they gather about any famous name; the will’s parchment and the Cepoy note are sterner witnesses than marble. [15]

And what of the city’s belief? Soft last turn: Jacopo d’Acqui, who garbled the sea-fight that brought Marco to a Genoese prison, yet kept a precious deathbed colour — that because there were many great and strange things in that Book which were reckoned past all credence, friends asked him to correct it by removing everything that went beyond the facts; to which his reply was that he had not told *one-half* of what he had really seen. Soft double verdict of Venice and of Europe: distrust enough to demand a deathbed retraction; delight enough to copy the Book into French for Valois, into Latin for friars, into romance for Bauduin’s readers, and into a physician’s argument about the habitability of the south. The Corte del Milione kept its name; the man kept his nickname in the Signory’s books; the family thinned into Bragadino and Dolfino marriages; the sarcophagus of Nicolo vanished in a rebuilding; and the Book — distrusted, delighted in, borrowed, translated — outlived the lagoon’s refusal to believe. [16]

Thus reception closes the travellers’ lives without closing the Traveller’s afterlife. Ramusio’s feast remains tradition, beloved and unreliable as fact; *Milione* remains a sobriquet whose insult-reading is later than its ledger-reading; Cepoy and Pipino mark real medieval circulation even as Larner’s caution forbids us to inflate that readership into ubiquity; the will of 1324 and San Lorenzo’s lost certainty mark a Christian merchant’s end; d’Abano’s star and Bauduin’s borrowings mark early readers who used the man and the Book for science and for play. Maps, Columbus’s postils, and the making of a modern witness belong to the next chapter; for this one it is enough that Ca’ Polo came home to a city that would not quite believe, and that disbelief itself became part of the legend the Book had to survive.
