# Chapter III-10
## Ca’ Polo, *Milione*, and the City That Would Not Believe

> *“And when the story got wind in Venice, straightway the whole city, gentle and simple, flocked to the house to embrace them… And as it happened that in the story… of the magnificence of the Great Can, he would speak of his revenues as amounting to ten or fifteen millions of gold… so they gave him the nickname of MESSER MARCO MILLIONI.”*
> — Giovanni Battista Ramusio, preface to *Navigationi et viaggi*, vol. 2 (1559), in Yule–Cordier, Introduction, § 5

### The doorstep in San Giovanni Grisostomo

At the courtyard door in Venice (Venezia), startled cousins stare through the crack and ask what three ragged strangers in coarse Tartar coats want with Ca’ Polo. Giovanni Battista Ramusio sets down this famous homecoming scene more than two centuries after 1295 [1]. In his telling, Venetian kin had long given all three men up for dead. Sun, wind, and twenty-four years abroad had altered their faces; Tartar phrases tangled their Venetian speech; and greasy wool coats smelled of ship-tar rather than silk. Like Ulysses stepping back into Ithaca, three travellers stood unrecognised inside their own ancestral house in San Giovanni Grisostomo [2]. Picture the awkwardness on the stone doorstep: three greying merchants claiming their own parlour while younger cousins look them up and down and wonder whether they are impostors.

— Ramusio named his witness plainly. As a boy he heard the story from an aged senator, Gasparo Malipiero, who had lived among those very courtyards and heard it from his father, grandfather, and neighbours—one crisp Venetian street memory handed down door to door [3].

To settle who they were, Ramusio’s Polos invited every sceptical kinsman to a grand dinner. When guests arrived, the three hosts swept into the hall dressed in floor-length crimson satin. Once hands were washed and first platters cleared, they stepped aside, changed into robes of crimson damask, and ordered the satin cut up and shared among the servants. Halfway through the feast they reappeared in glowing crimson velvet, giving away the damask. As dessert ended, they put on plain cloth cut like their guests’ own coats and distributed the velvet too. Every eye round the long table blinked in bewilderment at three ordinary Venetians sitting dressed like everyone else [4]. Who gave away three fortunes in crimson silk over a single family supper?

Then Marco rose, fetched the battered, patched Tartar travelling coats they had worn to the door, and laid a knife along the seams. Out tumbled rubies, sapphires, diamonds, and emeralds flashing across the white tablecloth [5]. No thief on Persian roads and no greedy cousin at the door had guessed what fortune rode stitched inside greasy linings. Word flew down the canals, and half Venice crowded into the courtyard to embrace the men they had taken for beggars—though Ramusio’s 1559 preface remains our sole witness for that theatrical supper and slashed coats [6].

### The name in the city

Ramusio keeps young Venetians trooping back to the house to hear Marco tell of Cambaluc (Beijing)’s palaces and treasuries. Whenever his reckoning of the Great Kaan’s revenues climbed into ten or fifteen millions of gold, listeners grinned behind their hands and tagged him *Messer Marco Milioni*—the man of millions [7]. Other writers offered rival roots: Jacopo d’Acqui credited a million *lire* in family fortune, while Giusto Fontanini heard incredulous readers mocking a million tall tales [8]. Archival ink settles at least that the name stuck early in Venice itself: Marco Pozza identifies “Marco Polo Milion” in a 1319 confraternity roll as the traveller, as Bolognari reports [9]. What began around supper tables or counting-houses soon became the tag by which Venice remembered him.

Notice how differently a nickname works across two civic rooms. Inside a storytelling parlour, *Milione* turns an empire’s tax ledger into an affectionate tease. Inside a city magistrate’s ledger, a sharp surname separates one Marco Polo from half a dozen cousins bearing the same baptismal name. Yule printed a Great Council entry of 1305 where a “Marcus Paulus Milion” stood surety for a fine, though modern archival work questions whether that 1305 surety was our traveller [10]. Even a famous nickname can blur two kinsmen across seven centuries of faded parchment.

### A household and a few pounds of musk

Six years later, parchment catches Marco squarely at his scales. In a 1311 Venice court judgment, Marco Polo of San Giovanni Grisostomo sued his agent Paulo Girardo over a pound and a half of fine musk entrusted for joint sale [11]. Girardo sold half a pound and returned the unsold pound short by one *saggio*. Judges of the *Petizion* ordered Girardo to pay Marco the sold half-pound’s share, the missing *saggio*—a sixth of an ounce—and court costs, valuing musk at six *lire* of grossi per pound on pain of imprisonment [12]. Think of what kind of price makes a merchant haul a broker to court over a single missing *saggio*’s pinch of dried scent. Twelve years later, in May 1323, another suit reached the Doge and judges over stairways, porticoes, and chambers beside his house, tangled up with property rights brought through his wife Donata [13]. Here is no mythic wanderer floating above daily Venice, but a sharp-eyed householder weighing ounces and guarding boundary stones months before his death.

Archival charters sketch his domestic roof far soberer than Ramusio’s jewel table. His wife Donata—identified by Giuseppe Gullino as daughter of Vitale Badoer, married at an uncertain date after the return—bore three daughters named in Marco’s will: Fantina, Bellela, and Moreta [14]. Fantina wed Marco Bragadin; Bertuccio Querini is identified as Bellela’s husband by Bolognari through Gallo’s mass records; and Moreta married first Ranuzzo Dolfino and later Tommaso Gradenigo [15]. A separate 1319 will also names an Agnese as daughter of a Marco Polo—perhaps an earlier child of the traveller, though identification remains open [16]. Every fresh Venetian parchment proves how crowded San Giovanni Grisostomo was with Polos sharing Christian names. Fathers, uncles, and nephews carried the same baptismal names down the same canals, leaving archivists to untangle every marriage and boundary deed by hand.

### What people took away

While Venice haggled over stairs and musk weights, French knights carried his book north. In December 1306, Thibault de Cepoy and Pierre le Riche sealed a treaty in Venice for Charles of Valois’s claim to Constantinople [17]. According to a famous note preserved in two manuscripts (Paris BnF fr. 5649 and Bern), Marco personally presented Cepoy with a copy of his travels in August 1307—described by the note’s writer as the very first copy ever taken—so his deeds might spread beyond Venice [18]. Cepoy’s son Jean later had copies drawn for Valois and friends, leaving us a rare dated trail of how a Genoese prison book entered French noble libraries [19]. From a Venice parlour, the manuscript crossed the Alps into the hands of Valois princes and court chroniclers. No longer merely an old traveller’s supper-table stories, the *Book* began circulating among knights who dreamed of eastern campaigns and royal courts.

Clerical readers wanted a different doorway. In Bologna, the Dominican friar Francesco Pipino translated the book into crisp Latin during Marco’s own lifetime—probably in the 1310s, between Yule’s 1315 guess and Ramusio’s 1320 [20]. Pipino’s preface vouches earnestly for Marco’s Christian faith, commending Asian lands to friars eager for souls to convert rather than silk prices to compare [21]. Pipino mined Marco for Mongol wars and the Old Man of the Mountain in his own Latin chronicle, and in a Modena manuscript explicitly named the Venetian traveller behind his pages [22]. Nearly sixty surviving manuscripts show how briskly Pipino’s Latin turned an adventurer’s tale into a serious monastery reference. Once dressed in Dominican Latin, Marco’s geography sat comfortably beside sermons and world chronicles, read aloud in refectories and copied across European houses.

Scholars tested Marco’s eye against the stars too. In his medical *Conciliator*, Pietro d’Abano asked Marco what lay beneath the southern equator—long thought uninhabitable—and recorded Marco’s sketch of a great faint tailed star alongside his measurement of the southern pole’s height as one soldier’s lance [23]. Filippo Bottin places that conversation in Venice around 1300–03, weighing whether Marco had sketched a comet or the Southern Cross [24]. Meanwhile the French romance *Bauduin de Sebourc* borrowed Marco’s Old Man of the Mountain and burning grave-goods outright [25]. Put knight, friar, physician, and romance poet round one table, and each opened the *Milione* to a different page. Yet John Larner reminds us that medieval readership, while lively across six tongues, remained finite beside Mandeville’s wider tide [26]. Even so, those early copies carried Marco’s name from Venetian alleys into monastic scriptoria and northern courts.

### January 1324

On 9 January 1324—still dated 1323 in Venice’s March-starting calendar—the priest-notary Giovanni Giustiniani climbed the stairs to take the will of an ailing Marco Polo, preserved today on its original parchment in the Marciana Library [27]. Marco named Donata, Fantina, Bellela, and Moreta as executors, chose burial at San Lorenzo beside his father, provided for his household and local churches, and freed his Tartar servant Pietro—remitting everything Pietro had earned and leaving him a hundred Venetian *lire* in denari [28]. That quiet bequest to a freed servant from Asia says as much about Marco’s household as any gilded feast. No longer the young envoy riding across Cathay or the sea-captain guiding a Mongol princess homeward, the dying merchant settled every small debt and household obligation in neat notary ink.

Around that sickbed, the Dominican chronicler Jacopo d’Acqui placed worried friends begging the dying traveller to retract his book’s wilder marvels [29]. Marco’s famous reply rings sharp as flint: he had not told the half of what he had truly seen. Jacopo’s testimony stands as early fourteenth-century witness rather than a sworn transcript, yet Bolognari sets beside it an earlier truth-assurance linked with elder Maffeo before a confessor [30]. Even as he lay dying in San Giovanni Grisostomo, the family’s reputation hung between Venetian disbelief and the sober oath of merchants who insisted they had understated rather than invented Asia.

Marco died on 8 or 9 January 1324, roughly seventy years old by the Prologue’s chronology [31]. An estate inventory preserved in a 1366 court copy produced during his daughter Fantina Bragadin’s estate lawsuit lists everyday wealth and eastern keepsakes side by side: quilts, garments, musk, aloe wood, rhubarb, a large gold tablet of command from the Magnificent Tartar, and a jewelled gold *bocheta* newly read by modern editors where an older transcript saw a headdress [32]. Look at that household list: medicinal rhubarb and eastern musk lying in chests beside Venetian linen and a Mongol tablet of gold. In 1592, builders tore down old San Lorenzo to its foundations and raised a new church, erasing the portico sarcophagus where Niccolò lay and the paving slab over his son’s grave so completely that no trace of the family tomb survived [33].

— Visitors still search Venice for Marco’s true face in portraits painted centuries after his death. An artist can sign a canvas and date the frame cleanly, yet no surviving likeness was ever drawn from the living traveller [34]. Every beard and fur cap on gallery walls belongs to later Venetian imagination rather than a fourteenth-century sketch from life.

By 1375, however, a Majorcan workshop with gold leaf and sea-charts in hand found a grander way to picture the world Marco’s book had opened. Across painted vellum, a traveller who left no portrait of his own face gave Europe a new, enduring portrait of Asia, from Sarai’s caravan tracks to the busy spice ports of the southern sea.

[1] ~ Ramusio-only. The ragged homecoming in San Giovanni Grisostomo comes solely from Ramusio’s 1559 preface, more than two centuries after 1295 [@yc, Introduction § 5]. The earliest Franco-Italian and Latin texts close the voyage simply with the arrival at Venice in 1295.

[2] ✓ Documentary records independently confirm the Polo family houses and property dealings around the Corte Sabbionera in the Venetian parish of San Giovanni Grisostomo [@agazzi2024casa]. The archival deeds anchor the family's domestic setting after their return from the East.

[3] ~ Ramusio-only. Ramusio names his friend Senator Gasparo Malipiero, an elderly neighbour by San Giovanni Grisostomo, as his source for the homecoming story [@yc, Introduction § 5]. Malipiero reportedly heard the anecdote from his father and grandfather in the parish.

[4] ~ Ramusio-only. Changing from crimson satin to crimson damask, then crimson velvet, and finally plain Venetian cloth while cutting up the rich robes for the servants belongs solely to Ramusio’s supper story [@yc, Introduction § 5]. The triple costume change echoes folkloric recognition banquets.

[5] ~ Ramusio-only. Slitting the seams of the patched Tartar coats to spill rubies, sapphires, diamonds, and emeralds onto the tablecloth completes Ramusio’s theatrical scene [@yc, Introduction § 5]. Once the gemstones appear on the board, the sceptical cousins immediately embrace the three travellers as kinsmen.

[6] ~ No fourteenth-century Venetian deed or contemporary chronicle corroborates the banquet or the slashed coats outside Ramusio [@yc, Introduction § 5]. Yule and modern scholars treat the tale as vivid neighbourhood legend rather than family record.

[7] ? Ramusio derives *Milione* from Marco’s repeated talk of Cublay Kaan's millions of gold bezants in imperial revenue [@yc, Introduction §§ 5, 37, 44]. Yule leans toward this explanation while noting that alternative archival derivations remain open.

[8] ? Jacopo d’Acqui linked *Milione* to a million *lire* of family wealth, whereas later writers such as Fontanini read it as mocking a million tall stories [@yc, Introduction §§ 5, 37, 44]. A surname root derived from an ancestor named *Emilione* has also been proposed in Venetian archival scholarship.

[9] ✓ Pozza identifies “Marco Polo Milion” in a 1319 confraternity roll as the traveller, as Bolognari reports alongside a fourteenth-century Florentine Pipino manuscript title containing *Milion* [@bolognari2024chronica, pp. 38–39]. Those records show the byname already attached to Marco and his book in his own generation.

[10] ? Yule cited a 10 April 1305 Great Council resolution naming a “noble Marcus Paulus Milion” standing surety for a wine-smuggling penalty [@yc, Introduction § 44]. Modern study of homonymous Polo cousins cautions against equating that 1305 surety uncritically with the traveller [@bolognari2024chronica, pp. 38–39].

[11] ✓ A Venetian court judgment of 9 March 1311 records Marco Polo suing his commission agent Paulo Girardo over a pound and a half of musk entrusted on a half-profit contract [@yc, Introduction § 46]. The lawsuit offers a direct glimpse of Marco continuing to trade in high-value Asian aromatics on the Rialto.

[12] ✓ Yule quotes the court award pricing the musk at six *lire* of grossi per pound and ordering Girardo to pay for the missing *saggio* plus legal costs or face imprisonment [@yc, Introduction § 46]. Even a single *saggio*—a sixth of an ounce—was worth taking to the judges of the Petizion.

[13] ✓ A May 1323 document before the Doge and judges records household boundary and property arrangements involving stairs, porticoes, and chambers in San Giovanni Grisostomo [@yc, Introduction § 46]. The deed shows Marco and his wife Donata managing their shared courtyard property months before his death.

[14] ✓ Marco’s 1324 will names his wife Donata and their three daughters, Fantina, Bellela, and Moreta [@bartoli2019edizione, doc. 33; @yc, Introduction § 7]. Gullino identifies Donata as the daughter of the merchant Vitale Badoer, married at an uncertain date after Marco's return [@gullino2015marco].

[15] ✓ Gullino records Fantina’s marriage to Marco Bragadin and Moreta’s marriages to Ranuzzo Dolfino and Tommaso Gradenigo [@gullino2015marco]. Bolognari cites Gallo’s 1326–27 mass records linking Bellela to Bertuccio Querini, while leaving Bellela’s exact year of death open [@bolognari2020convent, p. 19].

[16] ? A reported 1319 testament names an Agnese as daughter of a Marco Polo [@ferrarin2022agnese]. Orlando treats her identification as an older daughter of the traveller from a possible earlier union or household tie as an open question pending further archival study [@orlando2025novita].

[17] ✓ Yule dates the treaty negotiated at Venice by Thibault de Cepoy and Pierre le Riche on behalf of Charles of Valois to 14 December 1306 [@yc, Introduction § 45]. Thibault's diplomatic stay in Venice created the occasion for acquiring a French copy of Marco's book.

[18] ~ Two French manuscripts (Paris, BnF, fr. 5649, and Bern) carry the colophon note stating that Sire Marc Pol gave “the first copy” of his book to Thibault de Cepoy in Venice in August 1307 [@yc, Introduction § 45]. The note preserves a rare dated record of the author handing his work to an aristocratic patron.

[19] ~ The same colophon traces Thibault’s son Jean de Cepoy presenting a copy to Charles of Valois and making further transcripts for friends [@yc, Introduction § 45]. That presentation channel helped launch the courtly Old French tradition of the Book across northern Europe.

[20] ~ Ramusio assigned Francesco Pipino’s Latin translation to 1320 and Yule suggested about 1315 [@yc, Introduction §§ 43, 60, 76]. Gadrat-Ouerfelli documents a 1310–1322 window (citing Dutschke), deriving from a Venetian-dialect exemplar akin to VA and surviving in sixty manuscripts [@gadrat2022dominicans, p. 287; @dutschke1993francesco, pp. 216–217].

[21] ✓ Pipino’s Latin preface vouches for Marco’s upright Christian character—alongside his father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo—and recommends the book to edify faithful readers and inspire missionary zeal [@yc, Prologue, Pipino’s preface]. Turning the vernacular travelogue into church Latin opened libraries and monasteries across Christendom to the text.

[22] ✓ Pipino also drew directly on Polo in his Latin *Chronicon* and named the Venetian author explicitly in a Modena manuscript witness [@yc, Introduction §§ 43, 76]. His double use of the book shows how quickly Dominican scholars absorbed Marco's eastern geography.

[23] ✓ Yule prints the passage from Pietro d’Abano’s *Conciliator differentiarum* recording his conversation with Marco Polo about the equatorial sky [@yc, Introduction § 77]. Marco sketched a faint southern star shaped like a sack with a tail and described the Southern Pole's elevation as the length of a soldier's long lance.

[24] ? Bottin dates the meeting between Pietro d’Abano and Marco in Venice or Padua around 1300–03 and weighs whether the sketch represented a comet, the Magellanic Clouds, or the Southern Cross [@bottin2008pietro, pp. 428–429, 436–440]. Both the exact date of the conversation and the astronomical target remain scholarly reconstructions.

[25] ~ Yule traces direct narrative borrowings from Polo—including the Old Man of the Mountain, the synthesis of Eastern marvels, and Tartar funerary rites—in the fourteenth-century French verse romance *Bauduin de Sebourc* [@yc, Introduction § 78]. Vernacular poets mined the travelogue for exotic episodes while Marco was still alive.

[26] ~ Larner surveys the Book's fourteenth-century circulation across six languages and notes roughly 150 surviving Polo manuscripts against some 300 for Sir John Mandeville [@larner1999marco, chs. 6–7]. Early readers prized the work as courtly marvel and moral geography as much as practical trade manual.

[27] ✓ The notary-priest Giovanni Giustiniani drew up Marco’s Latin will on 9 January 1324 (1323 *more veneto*), naming Donata and the three daughters as trustees and choosing the Benedictine church of San Lorenzo for burial [@bartoli2019edizione, doc. 33; @yc, Introduction § 47]. The original parchment survives in Venice's Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana.

[28] ✓ The will emancipates Marco’s servant Pietro “of the Tartars” (*Petrus famulus meus de genere Tartarorum*), grants him all he had earned in the household, and leaves him one hundred Venetian *lire* [@moule1938description, vol. 1, p. 539; @bartoli2019edizione, doc. 33]. How and when Pietro entered Marco's service is left unstated in the testament.

[29] ~ Jacopo d’Acqui supplies the famous story that friends begged the dying Marco to prune back his book for the good of his soul, and that he answered he had not told one-half of what he had really seen [@jacopo1848chronica; @yc, Introduction § 37]. The scene stands as early fourteenth-century Lombard Dominican tradition rather than a bedside transcript [@bolognari2024chronica].

[30] ? Bolognari compares Jacopo’s deathbed anecdote with Pipino’s preface, where it is the dying uncle Maffeo who solemnly assures his confessor that the book contains the plain truth [@bolognari2024chronica, pp. 36–38]. Both stories defend the Book's veracity through a deathbed oath.

[31] ✓ Documentary editors place Marco’s death on 8 or 9 January 1324 from the will’s execution formula and the subsequent estate records [@bartoli2019edizione, doc. 33; @formentin2024codice; @formentin2024sentenza, p. 193]. He was roughly seventy years old by the Prologue's chronology.

[32] ✓ Preserved in a 1366 court copy produced during Fantina Bragadin's estate lawsuit, the post-mortem inventory lists a golden *paiza* from the Tartar ruler, silk brocades, musk, aloe, rhubarb, and a jewelled gold *bocheta* (clasp or brooch, revising an older *bochcta* headdress reading) [@formentin2024sentenza, pp. 217–221]. Those objects show Marco keeping tangible keepsakes of the Yuan court in his chests until the end of his life.

[33] ~ Yule traces Marco’s burial wish at San Lorenzo, where his father Niccolò already lay beneath a portico sarcophagus, and notes that the 1592 rebuilding of the church erased the grave [@yc, Introduction § 48; @bartoli2019edizione, doc. 33]. No trace of the family tomb survived the sixteenth-century reconstruction.

[34] ? Yule surveys the later painted and engraved portraits of Marco Polo and concludes that none preserves an authenticated likeness taken from life [@yc, Introduction § 48]. The traveller's only surviving self-portrait remains the world he dictated into his Book.
