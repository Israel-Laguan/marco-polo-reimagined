# Chapter III-5
## Maabar: Pearls, Suttee, Horses, and St Thomas

> *“When you leave the Island of Seilan and sail westward… you come to the great province of MAABAR which is styled INDIA THE GREATER… In this manner pearls are fished in great quantities, for thence in fact come the pearls which are spread all over the world… The Body of Messer St. Thomas the Apostle lies in this province of Maabar…”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 3, chs. 16–18 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**


---

A price can be put on what a man brings up from the bottom of the gulf. West of Seilan (Sri Lanka) and across a short stretch of water is Maabar (Coromandel Coast). The Book calls it India the Greater and gives the coast to five kings who are brothers [1]. The order in which it takes us to the ports will occasionally send us back along the shore [2]. Here at the fishery it is possible to see what brings a ship in at the right time of year, and why there will be men from the king’s household waiting when it comes.

Sonder Bandi Davar is the Book’s king at this end of the province. The ruler who fits the name and period in the reconstruction of K. A. Nilakanta Sastri is Jaṭāvarman Sundara Pāṇḍya, whose seventeenth regnal year fell in 1292–93 [3]. Leave him with his share to come. The merchants first have to hire the divers. Polo has the fleets gather at Bettelar, then go into the gulf from the beginning of April to the middle of May. At anchor the men get out of the large vessels and into little boats [4].

The diver goes down to where the oysters are. Polo puts him in water as deep as twelve fathoms. Ramusio gives him a bag to carry at the waist and has him come up for air before going down again [5]. Try measuring the shift as he measures it. A great deal of it must be spent on the way to the bottom and the way back up. Some of the things he can get off the seabed in the time he has will give his employer a pearl. At the surface it still has to be found in the flesh. A merchant who has been counting what he paid out in wages has quite a wait before he knows what the day’s fishing was worth.

When the catch is brought in, a tenth goes to the king in the Book’s account [6]. There is another payment. The men called Abraiaman are to keep the big fish from hurting the divers and have a twentieth of the take. Their charm is good for the day. When they undo it at night the men and the sharks are back in their usual relation to each other [7]. The Book has the fishery move elsewhere after May and open again in September and the first half of October [8]. See how many sorts of business have to come together at the piece of water where the boats are anchored, and how quickly they have to go off together when there is nothing more to bring up.

---

Go from the fishery to the man who gets his part of it. A fine cloth covers the king about the waist, while on his chest he wears a fortune. The Book puts rubies and sapphires and emeralds in his collar. There’s also a silk string with 104 beads of pearl and ruby, for the prayers he says morning and evening. Ramusio adds the word he repeats as he tells the beads, *Pacauta* [9]. The count is in the Book and the figure on which a comparison sheds light is different. Yule quotes Caldwell on a Vaishnava rosary of 108 beads [10].

Would you take a fine pearl out of Maabar if you had one? The Book prohibits the export of pearls over half a *saggio* and has the king announce several times a year that he will pay double what he is told a stone has cost. A merchant who makes his money on the difference between prices will see how readily that brings the good ones to the king [11]. He is said to spend well over a hundred marks of silver on a fine imported horse [12]. A boat can carry a jewel in a small bag. It will take more to land the animal and keep it in good condition.

The horses come by sea from Kisi (Kish), Hormos (Old Hormuz), Esher (Ash-Shihr), Dufar (Dhofar) and Aden in the Book. Out of the more than two thousand the king wants each year, Polo says, by the end of the year a hundred won't be left [13]. The whole trade makes sense as something a man who owns a ship might want a part in. The size of the losses, how the animals are fed and whether there are farriers to care for them are different things to find out.

— Waṣṣāf gives us a contract to move horses from Kisi to Maabar and describes how their condition deteriorates. Polo thinks the dealers keep farriers away to preserve the demand. If you are one of the men who have paid to get a shipload across the sea, you have some reason to hope the buyer will want another shipload. That helps explain the allegation. The Book has yet to establish what anyone did to keep a farrier off the ship [14].

Polo gives boiling rice and meat for the horses among the reasons for their deaths. He also makes a mare from good stock give birth to an animal which is useless for riding [15]. These are a seller’s or a buyer’s explanations as passed on by the Book. The contract gives an independent view of how a shipment was organised. There is still room for a question about what the next batch of horses would need by the time it got ashore.

The company that rides about with the king has its own business on the day he dies. The Book calls them his Trusty Lieges and puts them on the pyre with him. In the story, men who have followed him from place to place have one more journey on which he will want them [16]. Polo has his heirs leave his treasure untouched so that each will have to accumulate his own. Waṣṣāf gives a large treasury at the death of the ruler, with less to tell us about that rule [17].

Alongside it the Book describes widows who die on their husbands’ pyres. It records the praise they receive [18]. The praise belongs to the people who are able to stand around the pyre and go home from it. The Book also tells of a different death, in which a man under sentence goes through the streets declaring the god for whom he is going to die [19]. It gives him kin who will accompany him and a crowd to hear him do it.

---

Sit down to eat in the Book’s Maabar and the meal can tell other people a great deal about you. Polo notices bathing twice a day [20]. Ramusio adds eating with the right hand and the way a drink is poured into the mouth without touching the vessel to the lips. A stranger who needs a drink can be offered it in his cupped hands [21]. The Book singles out the position of people called Govy, who are said to eat the meat of an ox when it has died but to abstain from killing it [22].

There is quite a hard line on whom to accept as surety. The Book rules out a person who drinks wine and, to the disadvantage of anybody coming in from a ship, a seafaring man [23]. In Ramusio a creditor with a sufficiently persistent debtor can draw a circle round him. Polo says he once saw even the king stop inside one and stay until he paid what he owed [24]. Picture the man who has come to the road day after day to ask for the money. The story finally gives him an immediate way to tell whether he will go home with it.

The Book puts the rain in June, July and August [25]. What can stop a merchant from starting out on a day that looks perfectly fine? In the Book a sneeze on the road can do it. It also has people write down the hour and the age of the moon at a birth for the astrologers [26]. Ramusio sends thirteen-year-old boys out from home with some twenty or twenty-four groats. During the fishery they can use the money to buy a few pearls on the beach and sell them to a merchant keeping out of the sun [27]. A small profit is worth something to the boys, and a man taking his ease indoors has one less errand in the heat.

At a temple feast, the Book has the girls consecrated there serve a meal to the gods. They leave the dishes for as long as it takes a lord to dine, dance and sing, and then share the food that remains [28]. Ramusio gives them a particular business with the gods. They have to bring a god back into a good temper with a goddess, on whom the good fortune of the people depends [29].

---

There is little to buy in the small town where the Book puts the body of St Thomas. The place is Mailapur (Mylapore), and it draws both Muslim and Christian pilgrims. Polo hears the title *Avarian*, which he takes to mean a holy man [30]. The earth at the shrine is red. The Book has pilgrims take it away to people ill with fever, in the belief that it will cure them [31]. A man coming to buy a pearl will spend time looking at what he is offered. A pilgrim coming for soil already knows what ground he wants.

Polo dates one of the local miracles to 1288. In the story a nobleman takes up the houses near the church for his rice. There’s nowhere for the pilgrims to sleep. They ask him to shift it, and it is only when the saint appears at night with a fork at his throat that he takes the rice away [32]. Take a moment to see what the people looking after the church get in that story. A place to lay down at the end of a pilgrimage matters as much to the keeper of the shrine as to the man who’s been on the road all day.

In Ramusio the people looking after the church have an income from coconut trees. Each tree has a monthly rent of six groats to one of the kings [33]. That is a sum a man visiting them has every chance of asking about and a good deal more reason to remember if he is the man who has to pay it.

The keepers give a story of how the saint died, too. He is praying outside his dwelling with peacocks around him. A hunter shoots at one, misses it and hits Thomas in the side [34]. The people who can tell a visitor which way the arrow came from are the people he meets at the shrine. The burial tradition and its claim about an apostle’s death go back much farther than that visit.

What will a merchant put in the same scales when the ship makes its next port of call?
