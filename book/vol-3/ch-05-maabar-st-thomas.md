# Chapter III-5
## Maabar: Pearls, Suttee, Horses, and St Thomas

> *“When you leave the Island of Seilan and sail westward… you come to the great province of MAABAR which is styled INDIA THE GREATER… In this manner pearls are fished in great quantities, for thence in fact come the pearls which are spread all over the world… The Body of Messer St. Thomas the Apostle lies in this province of Maabar…”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 3, chs. 16–18 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**


---

Point the bow sixty miles west of Seilan (Sri Lanka) across emerald shallows, and Maabar (the Coromandel Coast) swings low into view—a rich mainland realm the Book hails as India the Greater [1]. Five royal brothers share rule across these steaming coastal plains, though Marco’s chapters soon skip northward and double back out of strict shoreline order [2]. At this easternmost gulf reigns Sonder Bandi Davar, whom K. A. Nilakanta Sastri identifies with Jaṭāvarman Sundara Pāṇḍya, whose seventeenth regnal year fell in 1292–93 [3]. Before entering his hall of gems, row out into morning glare to watch hired divers drop down weighted lines.

Every April until mid-May, merchant syndicates muster fleets off Bettelar and steer sixty miles into the shallow pearl gulf between island and mainland. Soundings run from two fathoms deep down to twelve. Tall merchant ships drop stone anchors, while wage-crews hired by separate merchant companies climb aboard light fishing skiffs [4]. Watch a dark-skinned diver clasp a heavy sinker stone, slip cleanly over the gunwale, and sink down twelve fathoms toward limestone oyster beds. In Ramusio’s vivid harbour scene, he packs rough shells into a net bag tied round his waist, bursts back up to gulp air, catches his breath across the boat’s rail, and plunges back down till dusk [5]. Picture an impatient trader counting daily wage silver on deck while wet oyster baskets pile up around wooden tubs. Clerks slit shells open one by one under hot awnings, poking through pale flesh for pea-sized gems or barren grey muscle. From these beds, Marco vows, flow the finest pearls prized across every kingdom on earth, carried by merchant galleys from Cairo to Cambaluc (Beijing).

Royal clerks divide every haul before oysters cool in sun. First the king claims an unyielding tenth of the entire catch as his royal due [6]. Next step forward the Abraiaman fish-charmers, paid one twentieth of the haul to spell away great sharks from tearing divers under dark water [7]. Listen closely to the Book’s exact terms at dusk: Marco notes that *“their charm holds good for that day only”*, dissolving each night *“so that the fishes can work mischief at their will”* [8]. These Abraiaman claim power to charm beasts, birds, and every living thing across land and sea. Come mid-May, pearl oysters vanish cleanly from this gulf. They open three hundred miles distant again only from September through mid-October [9]. Tide, wages, royal tax, and shark-spells lock tight like coastal clockwork before a single pearl rolls across velvet scales.

---

Step ashore now to inspect the sovereign collecting that crisp tenth. Across all Maabar no tailor ever threads a needle or cuts a coat, because king and labourer alike go bare save for a fine loincloth [10]. Round Sonder Bandi Davar’s brown shoulders, however, hangs a rolling kingdom’s fortune. A broad collar blazing with rubies, sapphires, and emeralds rests above three golden armlets thick with pearls, paired with jewelled anklets and pearl-ringed toes worth more than a city’s ransom [11]. Over his chest drapes a fine silk cord carrying 104 large pearls and rubies, slipped gently past his fingers morning and evening for 104 daily prayers handed down by his royal ancestors [12]. Ramusio catches the whispered invocation repeating round that string: *Pacauta! Pacauta!* [13] Where the Book counts 104 beads, Robert Caldwell points out in Yule’s notes a 108-bead Vaishnava rosary behind the custom [14]. Every gem hanging on the king’s chest advertises a coastal court where wealth is worn in open daylight rather than locked behind stone walls.

Keep your own prize gems locked safely inside belted chests. Royal law bans exporting any pearl heavier than half a *saggio* unless smuggled out secretly, while heralds cry several times each year that the king pays twice cost price for every rare jewel brought to court [15]. Small wonder five hundred wives crowd his palaces—even a brother’s handsome wife, seized openly while the prudent brother holds his tongue [16]. Beside the throne ride sworn barons called the Trusty Lieges. They eat at the king’s own table, ride beside his banner, and enjoy high honour across every province. Yet when death lays the king upon a sandalwood pyre, those loyal comrades fling themselves alive into roaring flames to ride beside their lord in the world beyond [17]. Even mountains of royal gold stay sealed untouched by heirs, forcing every new sovereign to pile a fresh treasury [18]—though Waṣṣāf’s Persian chronicle marvels at immense hoarded Pandyan wealth without repeating Marco’s inheritance taboo [19].

Where does half that kingdom’s bullion pour straight back out to sea? Into Arabian cavalry horses—over two thousand fine chargers wanted every year by Sonder Bandi Davar and equal droves by his four brothers, priced at five hundred gold *saggi* or over a hundred silver marks apiece [20]. Out from Kisi (Kish), Hormos (Old Hormuz), Esher (Ash-Shihr), Dufar (Dhofar), and Aden sail packed horse-dhows across monsoon swells. Yet Marco swears that before twelve months pass, scarcely a hundred horses remain alive out of two thousand landed [21].

— Lay Waṣṣāf’s crisp Persian shipping contract directly beside Marco’s shock at empty stables [22]. Both confirm huge Gulf cargoes crossing to Maabar in failing condition. Polo accuses Gulf dealers of deliberately keeping every farrier ashore so annual demand never cools [23]. Yet feeding high-bred stallions boiled rice and cooked meat—alongside failed local stud trials breeding wry-legged colts unfit for war—leaves open whether tropical feed, salt voyages, or sharp horse-traders drove those losses [24].

Fire and steel mark public death as starkly as stable ledgers. Faithful widows step boldly onto their husbands’ blazing funeral pyres amid loud public praise from kin [25]. Stranger still unfolds a condemned criminal’s religious end. Granted leave to perish for a chosen god, he mounts a festive wooden cart bearing twelve sharp knives while kinsmen parade him down crowded streets shouting his vow. Stopping at the death-place, he cries aloud that he slays himself for love of his idol, drives knife after knife straight through both arms, thighs, and belly until life gives out, and wins joyful cremation from weeping kin [26].

---

Sit cross-legged on clean packed earth in Maabar’s heat, and daily customs draw tight boundaries around every meal. Bare infantry carry only shield and lance into battle, killing neither bird nor ox and paying local Muslims to butcher whatever meat anyone eats [27]. Men and women scrub the entire body twice every day before touching rice, scorning unwashed folk like northern heretics [28]. Ramusio sharpens every dining gesture: diners lift rice strictly with the right hand, reserving the left solely for unclean washing, and hold private bronze flasks high aloft so cool water pours cleanly down the throat without lips brushing the rim—never lending a personal cup to a stranger, though hosts willingly tilt water straight into a traveller’s cupped palms [29]. Cattle enjoy sacred shelter: even lower-caste Govy workers never kill an ox, feasting on beef solely when a beast drops dead naturally [30]. Townsfolk plaster cottage walls and floors smooth with cow-dung and sit straight upon packed earth because human flesh springs from soil and returns to it [31].

Credit and street courts obey borders just as sharp. Magistrates disqualify both wine-drinkers and ocean sailors alike as legal sureties [32]. In Ramusio’s unforgettable street tableau, a patient creditor stops any debtor simply by scribing a crisp chalk circle round his boots—leaving the man trapped until he settles the claim or surrenders his goods, and Marco swears he once watched even the king check his horse inside a foreign merchant’s drawn ring until silver clinked into the creditor’s palm [33]. Picture every skipper along the pier grinning quietly behind a barrel while royal hoofs wait on chalk dust.

Heavy rains fall solely across June, July, and August, leaving clear skies nine months a year [34]. Even in dry sunshine, omens rule every morning’s walk: a single sneeze halts travellers mid-step until they sit down and wait out the ill hour, while astrologers chart every newborn baby’s exact birth-hour and lunar sign [35]. Ramusio lingers affectionately over bright thirteen-year-old boys pacing sandy beaches with twenty or twenty-four silver groats tucked inside their belts, snapping up odd bargains off incoming skiffs and flipping pearls indoors to idle merchants who keep out of midday sun for a brisk Venice groat’s gain [36]. At temple festivals, consecrated girls dance, sing, and lay hot rice dishes before carved idols—leaving platters spread long enough for a baron to dine before sharing the leftovers [37]. Why all those steaming pots? Ramusio supplies a sly domestic detail: priests summon temple dancers whenever god and goddess quarrel, coaxing celestial harmony back into heaven so harvest rains never fail [38].

---

Leave crowded pearl stalls behind and follow quiet dusty lanes inland to Mailapur (Mylapore), a quiet little town holding scarce trade wares yet drawing Christians and Muslims alike to the tomb of Messer St Thomas [39]. Muslim pilgrims venerate the apostle as a great prophet under the Arabic title *Avarian*, meaning holy man. Fever-stricken visitors scoop red earth from the martyrdom floor to stir into cool water against stubborn tertian and quartan agues, swearing sick men rise healed [40]. No Govy can even be dragged across the threshold—twenty strong men cannot pin one inside the chapel, Marco marvels, because Govy hunters shed the apostle’s blood.

In 1288, local shrine brethren told Marco a sharp miracle over pilgrim rooms [41]. A pagan baron had packed every church guest-house to the rafters with harvested rice. Weary travellers were shut out into bare fields despite repeated pleas from the keepers. That night St Thomas stood over the baron’s bed, pressing an iron fork hard against his throat, and warned him to empty the pilgrim houses at once or die an evil death [42]. By sunrise every sack was hauled clean away, and the shaken baron stood in the courtyard confessing his terror aloud. Ramusio adds a crisp household balance-sheet beside the shrine lamps: green groves of coconut palms keep the small church alive, though one of the royal brothers taxes them six Venetian groats per tree each month [43]. Even guardians of a martyr’s tomb count rent down to the half-groat.

Ask how Thomas died, and those brethren lead visitors out toward wooded crags where wild peacocks strutted round the saint at prayer [44]. A Govy archer loosed a shaft through branches toward a fan of bright feathers and struck the praying apostle cleanly in the right side. Dying, the apostle still kept his eyes turned toward his Creator, and pilgrims still gather the red earth beneath his tomb as a living relic. Across Maabar, mothers rub dark babies weekly with warm sesame oil to deepen glossy ebony skins—painting holy idols pitch-black and wicked devils chalk-white. Meanwhile, cavalrymen twine stiff wild-ox tail hairs round spear-shaft and horse-mane alike, trusting that rough charm to bring both rider and mount untouched out of war [45].

Step quietly out of Mailapur’s red courtyard and turn north a thousand miles—or five hundred in Ramusio’s coastal reckoning—toward Mutfili (Motupalli) [46], where a wise queen guards shipwreck cargoes and snakes coil beneath valleys glittering with uncut diamonds.
