# Chapter III-5
## Maabar: Pearls, Suttee, Horses, and St Thomas

> *“When you leave the Island of Seilan and sail westward… you come to the great province of MAABAR which is styled INDIA THE GREATER… In this manner pearls are fished in great quantities, for thence in fact come the pearls which are spread all over the world… The Body of Messer St. Thomas the Apostle lies in this province of Maabar…”*
> — Book III.xvi–xviii (Yule–Cordier)

**[R/M]**

---

When you leave the Island of Seilan and sail westward about sixty miles, Marco Polo narrates, you come to the great province of Maabar, which is styled India the Greater; it is the best of all the Indies and is on the mainland. You must know that in this province there are five kings, who are own brothers. The Province is the finest and noblest in the world. At this end of the Province reigns one of those five Royal Brothers, a crowned King named Sonder Bandi Davar (Sundara Pāṇḍya Devar). In his kingdom they find very fine and great pearls; and I will tell you how they are got. [1]

You must know, he says, that the sea here forms a gulf between the Island of Seilan and the mainland. And all round this gulf the water has a depth of no more than ten or twelve fathoms, and in some places no more than two. The pearl-fishers take their vessels, great and small, and proceed into this gulf from the beginning of April till the middle of May. They go first to a place called Bettelar (Patlam, on the Ceylon shore of the Manar gulf), and then sixty miles into the gulf. Here they cast anchor and shift into small boats. The merchants divide into companies and hire divers for those six weeks. Of all the produce they pay the King, as his royalty, the tenth part; and they must also pay those men who charm the great fishes — one twentieth of all that they take — lest the sharks injure the divers below. These fish-charmers are termed Abraiaman; and their charm holds good for that day only, for at night they dissolve it so that the fishes can work mischief at their will. The divers jump from the small boats and go to the bottom, four to twelve fathoms down, remaining as long as they are able. There they find the shells that contain the pearls, put them into a net bag tied round the waist, and mount again — and so all day. [2]

In this manner pearls are fished in great quantities, for thence in fact come the pearls which are spread all over the world. And I can tell you the King of that State hath a very great receipt and treasure from his dues upon those pearls. As soon as the middle of May is past, no more of those shells are found there — though some three hundred miles distant they are also found, in September and the first half of October. [3]

So Maabar opens as ferry-coast and pearl-kingdom: five royal brothers; a gulf so shallow the keel almost scrapes the beds; Abraiaman who bind the sharks by day and loose them by night; a tenth to the crown and a twentieth to the charmers; and a world market of pearls from that brief April window. Soft turn now: from the fishery to the court that wears its dues as a city’s ransom. [4]

You must know that in all this Province of Maabar there is never a Tailor to cut a coat or stitch it, seeing that everybody goes naked! For decency only do they wear a scrap of cloth — men and women, rich and poor, aye, and the King himself, except what I am going to mention. The King goes as bare as the rest, only round his loins a piece of fine cloth, and round his neck a necklace of rubies, sapphires, emeralds, and the like, of great value. He wears also, hanging from the neck down his chest, a fine silk thread strung with one hundred and four large pearls and rubies. The reason (they tell) is that every day, morning and evening, he must say one hundred and four prayers to his idols. Thus did all the Kings his ancestors, and they bequeathed him the string that he should do the like. The prayer consists of these words — *Pacauta! Pacauta! Pacauta!* — repeated one hundred and four times. On his arms he wears three golden bracelets thickly set with pearls, and anklets of like kind, and rings on his toes. So what this King wears, between gold and gems and pearls, is worth more than a city’s ransom. Nobody may take out of the kingdom a pearl weighing more than half a *saggio*, unless secretly; for the King reserves such to himself. Several times a year he proclaims that whoever brings him a pearl or stone of great value shall receive twice what it cost — and thus he gathers all into his hands. [5]

— The number should be one hundred and eight for a Vaishnava rosary; the Book’s one hundred and four is a near-miss mystic count. *Pacauta* is likely *Bagavā* / Bhagavat. Gem display and *japā* are sound. [6] Anyway, next he mentions wives and Trusty Lieges.

Furthermore, this King hath some five hundred wives; whenever he hears of a beautiful damsel he takes her. Indeed he did a sorry deed: seeing that his brother had a handsome wife, he took her by force and kept her; and the brother, being discreet, made no noise about it. About the King attend Barons of great authority, called the King’s Trusty Lieges. When the King dies and they put him on the fire, these Lieges cast themselves into the flames round about his body and burn with him. For they say they have been his comrades in this world, and ought to keep him company in the other. When the King dies none of his children dares touch his treasure. They say, “as our father gathered this, so we ought to accumulate as much in our turn” — and thus an immensity of treasure accumulates in the kingdom. [7]

Here are no horses bred; and thus a great part of the wealth of the country is wasted in purchasing them. The merchants of Kis (Qais / Kish) and Hormes (Hormuz), Dofar (Dhufar) and Soer (Sohar) and Aden collect destriers and other horses and bring them to this King and his four brothers. A horse fetches five hundred *saggi* of gold — worth more than one hundred marks of silver — and vast numbers are sold every year. Indeed this King wants to buy more than two thousand horses every year, and so do his brothers. The reason is that by year’s end fewer than one hundred remain, for they all die off — from mismanagement, for those people know not how to treat a horse, and have no farriers. The merchants not only never bring farriers, but prevent any from going thither, lest that should baulk a trade of such vast gains. They bring the horses by sea aboard ship. [8]

And there is no possibility of breeding horses in this country, as hath often been proved. Even when a great blood-mare has been covered by a great blood-horse, the produce is nothing but a wretched wry-legged weed, not fit to ride. They feed their horses with boiled rice and boiled meat and other cooked food — and that is why the horses die. [9]

— This is one of Polo’s sharpest economic observations. Wassaf and Rashid tell nearly the same story of Gulf horse exports to Maabar, price agreements, and fattening without training. The merchant cartel on farriers is Polo’s pointed addition. Breeding failure in South India is independently corroborated. [10] Anyway, next he mentions customs of death, ox, and manners.

When a man is doomed to die for any crime, he may declare that he will put himself to death in honour of such or such an idol, and the government grants him leave. His kinsfolk set him on a cart with twelve knives and lead him about the city, crying that this valiant man will slay himself for love of the idol. At the place of execution he drives a knife through one arm, then the other, then into his belly, and so on until he kills himself outright; and they burn him with joyful celebration. Many women also, when their husbands are placed on the pile, burn themselves along with the bodies — and such women have great praise from all. [11]

The people are Idolaters, and many worship the ox for its excellence. They would not eat beef for anything, nor kill an ox. But another class, called Govy, are glad to eat beef though they dare not kill; if an ox dies naturally or otherwise, they eat him. They rub their houses all over with cow-dung; and all of them, King and Barons included, sit upon the ground only — because we spring from the Earth and to the Earth must return. Nothing on earth would induce those Govis to enter the place where Messer St Thomas lies buried in this province. Were twenty or thirty men to hold one there, they could not do it — such is the Saint’s influence; for it was by people of that generation that he was slain, as you shall hear. [12]

No wheat grows here, but rice only. They go to battle naked, with lance and shield, and are most wretched soldiers. They will kill neither beast nor bird; for animal food they make the Saracens, or others not of their religion, play the butcher. Every one washes the whole body twice a day. In eating they use the right hand only; the left is reserved for unclean necessities. Each man drinks from his own vessel, held aloft so the drink spouts into the mouth — never touching the lip — and will not give a stranger drink from it, though they will pour into his hands that he may drink from them as from a cup. They are strict in justice and in abstaining from wine; wine-drinkers and seafaring men are never accepted as sureties — for a seafarer, they say, is an utter desperado. Howbeit they look on lechery as no sin. Of debts they have this rule: if a creditor, often put off, can once draw a circle round his debtor, the latter must not pass out until he satisfy the claim or give security — or he dies as a transgressor. And Messer Marco, in this kingdom on his return, witnessed the King himself halted in such a circle by a foreign merchant, and would not stir until the man was paid — whereat the bystanders marvelled that so just a King should thus submit to justice. [13]

— That debt-circle anecdote is Ramusian colour; flag it as such. The custom type is plausible; the autobiographical king-halt is later embroidery on the Geographic line. [14] Anyway, next he mentions climate, omens, boy-traders, and consecrated damsels.

The heat here is sometimes wonderful. Rain falls only three months — June, July, and August; but for that rain, refreshing earth and air, the drought would let no one exist. They have many experts in Physiognomy, and regard omens of bird and beast more than any people in the world: if a man on the road hears a sneeze and deems it ill, he may stop or turn back altogether. At a child’s birth they write the nativity — day, hour, month, and moon’s age — for every act is done by astrology and the advice of diviners. Parents who have male children, once the boys reach thirteen, dismiss them from home with some twenty or four-and-twenty groats. These urchins run all day buying and selling; at the pearl-fishery they purchase five or six pearls on the beach and take them to merchants who keep indoors for fear of the sun, asking what profit the merchant please — and so they grow dexterous traders. [15]

They have certain abbeys of gods and goddesses to whom young girls are consecrated by devoted parents. When the monks would feast their god, they send for these damsels to sing and dance before the idol; the girls prepare meats, set them before the image, and leave them a good while — then dance and sing as long as a Baron might dine. By then, they say, the spirit has consumed the substance, so they remove the viands and eat with great jollity. This they do several times a year until the damsels marry. The monks say the god is vexed with the goddess and will not communicate; without peace, grace fails — so the girls, all but naked, dance the quarrel into concord. Nobles sleep in light cane beds drawn by cords nearly to the ceiling, clear of tarantulas and fleas and seeking air in the great heat; the rest sleep in the streets. [16]

Soft rise now: to the little town where few traders go, and where the Body of Messer St Thomas lies.

The Body of Messer St Thomas the Apostle lies in this province of Maabar at a certain little town having no great population — Mailapur, as later ages name that suburb of the Coromandel shore. Few traders go there, for little merchandize is to be got, and the place is not very accessible. Both Christians and Saracens, however, frequent it in pilgrimage. The Saracens hold the Saint in great reverence, saying he was one of their own and a great prophet, and giving him the title *Avarian*, which is as much as to say “Holy Man.” Christians on pilgrimage take earth from the place where the Saint was killed and give a portion to any sick of a quartan or tertian fever; and by the power of God and of St Thomas the sick man is cured. The earth is red. A fine miracle occurred there in the year of Christ 1288, as I will relate. [17]

A certain Baron, having great store of rice, had filled all the houses that belonged to the church and stood round about it. The Christians in charge were distressed; the pilgrims had nowhere to lay their heads; and though they often begged him to remove his grain, he would not. So one night the Saint appeared with a fork in his hand, set it at the Baron’s throat, and said: “If thou void not my houses, that my pilgrims may have room, thou shalt die an evil death” — and pressed so hard that the Baron thought himself a dead man. In the morning he caused all the houses to be emptied, and told what had befallen him. The Christians rejoiced and rendered thanks to God and to the blessed St Thomas. Other miracles often come to pass there — healing of the sick or deformed, especially among Christians. The keepers of the church live by Indian Nut trees, paying one of the brother Kings six groats for each tree every month. [18]

The Christian brethren relate the Saint’s death thus. He was in the wood outside his hermitage at prayer, and round about him many peacocks — for these are more plentiful here than anywhere. An Idolater of the Govi lineage, gone with bow and arrows to shoot peafowl, not seeing the Saint, let fly at a peacock; and the arrow struck the holy man in the right side, so that he died of the wound, sweetly addressing his Creator. Before he came to that place he had been in Nubia, where he converted much people to the faith of Jesus Christ. The children born here are black enough, and the blacker the more thought of; from birth their parents rub them weekly with oil of sesamé, so that they become as black as devils. They make their gods black and their devils white. Such faith have they in the ox that when they go to war they take hair of the wild-ox and tie it to their horses’ necks, or hang it on shields or in their own hair — and without it nobody goes to battle in good heart. [19]

Thus the western run from Seilan stands in this chapter: Maabar as India the Greater and pearl gulf; Sonder Bandi’s ransom of gems and Trusty Lieges on the pyre; the horse trade from Hormes and Kis that drains a treasury because breeding fails and farriers are kept away; ox and Govy and monsoon and omen; consecrated damsels of the abbeys; Mailapur’s red earth and 1288 fork-miracle; a peacock-arrow martyrdom told by the keepers of the shrine. Soft close here: the Book has not yet opened Mutfili and the diamond valley, nor the westward ports from Cail to Kesmacoran; for this chapter it is enough that the ferry-coast of Maabar has been told — that pearls, suttee, horses, and an Apostle’s tomb have been set in one Venetian breath — and that a merchant’s eye has seen, sharper than many moralists, why the destriers of Arabia die in the rice-stables of the South.
