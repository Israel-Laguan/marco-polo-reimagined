# Chapter III-2
## Chamba, Java, Locac, Pentam: The Southern Ocean Road

> *“When you leave the port of Zayton you sail west-south-west for 1500 miles, and then you come to a country called Chamba, a very rich region… And I can assure you the Great Kaan never could get possession of this Island [Java], on account of its great distance…”*
> — Book III.v–vi (Yule–Cordier)

**[R/M]**

---

From Zayton (Quanzhou), having closed the eastern digression of Chipangu (Japan) and the Sea of Chin, Marco Polo turns the prow — so the Book narrates — toward India by the southern Ocean road: first the rich mainland of Chamba (Champa), then a digression to Java the Great, then the landmark isles of Sondur and Condur, the guarded kingdom of Locac, the wild island of Pentam, and the trading city of Malaiur. Beyond those Java the Less (Sumatra) will open the next chapter. This is the merchant’s chain, not the wonder-catalogue of Chipangu’s gold roof. Chamba the Book places on a personal calendar; Java, Locac, and much of what follows are chiefly pilot-road and merchant report. [1]

You must know, he says, that on leaving the port of Zayton you sail west-south-west for fifteen hundred miles, and then you come to a country called Chamba, a very rich region, having a king of its own. The people are Idolaters and pay a yearly tribute to the Great Kaan, which consists of elephants and nothing but elephants. And the Book will tell how they came to pay this tribute. [2]

It happened in the year of Christ 1278, according to the book, that the Great Kaan sent a Baron of his called Sagatu with a great force of horse and foot against this King of Chamba, and this Baron opened the war on a great scale. Now the King — whose name some texts give as Accambale — was a very aged man, nor had he such a force as the Baron had. And when he saw what havoc the Baron was making with his kingdom he was grieved to the heart. So he despatched messengers to the Great Kaan, saying that he was stricken in years and willing to be the Kaan’s liegeman, and would send every year a tribute of as many elephants as the Kaan pleased, if only the Baron would desist from harrying his kingdom. When the Great Kaan had heard the King’s ambassage he was moved with pity, and sent word to that Baron to quit that kingdom with his army. Thus it was then that this King became vassal of the Great Kaan, and paid him every year a tribute of twenty of the greatest and finest elephants that were to be found in the country. [3]

— A quick note: Chinese annals know *Sotu*, Chen-ching, bruising wars of the early 1280s, and Tughan’s later march through Tong-king. Polo softens a harder Yuan frontier into a neat pity-and-vassalage. Elephant wealth and gifts are real; the neat annual twenty and the mercy-tale are book compression. [4] Anyway, next he mentions other particulars about the King of Chamba.

In that kingdom, he says, no woman is allowed to marry until the King shall have seen her; if she pleases him he takes her to wife; if not, he gives her a dowry to get her a husband. In the year of Christ 1285, Messer Marco Polo was in that country, and at that time the King had, between sons and daughters, three hundred and twenty-six children, of whom at least one hundred and fifty were men fit to carry arms. There are very great numbers of elephants in this kingdom, and they have lignaloes in great abundance. They have also extensive forests of the wood called *bonus*, which is jet-black, and of which chessmen and pen-cases are made. [5]

Thus Chamba stands not only as report but as a claimed sight: the marriage privilege of the king; a brood of hundreds; elephants thronging the land; lignaloes — eagle-wood, the diseased heartwood the trade prized; and ebony named in a Persian word, *abnūs*, that Rustichello’s French half-heard as *bonus*, with pen-cases whose name leans toward the Persian *ḳalamdān*. That Persian vocabulary for Southeast Asian goods marks the linguistic stratum of the Ocean road as Polo heard it among Chinese, Persian, and Malay speakers. Manuscripts give the visit year as 1280, 1285, or 1288; Yule leans toward an Indian-seas mission about 1290, or the return voyage still ahead. Odoric, a generation later, will echo the crowded royal nursery and the tame elephants. [6]

The Ocean road here does what Volume II’s land chapters often did: it pauses for a kingdom the Great Kaan could not pocket, then resumes the bearing that will bring a princess home. Java is wealth reported as absolute independence; Chamba was wealth already half-claimed as tribute. Between those two political colours the Book measures southern Asia for a European ear still learning that Manzi’s ships, not Latin galleys, own this monsoon. Soft digression now: south and south-east, to an island the Book will call the greatest in the world.

When you sail from Chamba, fifteen hundred miles in a course between south and south-east, Polo narrates, you come to a great Island called Java. And the experienced mariners of those Islands who know the matter well say that it is the greatest Island in the world, and has a compass of more than three thousand miles. It is subject to a great King and tributary to no one else in the world. The people are Idolaters. The Island is of surpassing wealth, producing black pepper, nutmegs, spikenard, galingale, cubebs, cloves, and all other kinds of spices. This Island is also frequented by a vast amount of shipping, and by merchants who buy and sell costly goods from which they reap great profit. Indeed the treasure of this Island is so great as to be past telling. And the Book can assure you the Great Kaan never could get possession of this Island, on account of its great distance, and the great expense of an expedition thither. The merchants of Zayton and Manzi draw annually great returns from this country. [7]

— The circumference is vastly overstated — roughly double the truth — and the same figure of three thousand miles appears in Odoric and Conti, inherited from Arab sea-tradition that never knew the southern coast. The spice list mixes what Java grows with what Java markets from the farther Moluccas. Almost immediately after the Venetians’ departure Kublai did attempt the island: envoy Meng-K’i branded; great armament of 1292–93; temporary success and forced re-embarkation; death of the Kaan ending renewal. Polo’s “never could” is either foresight of that failure or a merchant’s shorthand for the unfinished claim. [8] Anyway, the narrative leaves Java and returns to the true line of the voyage.

When you leave Chamba and sail for seven hundred miles on a course between south and south-west, he says, you arrive at two Islands, a greater and a less. The one is called Sondur and the other Condur. As there is nothing about them worth mentioning, let us go on five hundred miles beyond Sondur, and then we find another country which is called Locac. It is a good country and a rich; it is on the mainland; and it has a king of its own. The people are Idolaters and have a peculiar language, and pay tribute to nobody, for their country is so situated that no one can enter it to do them ill. Indeed if it were possible to get at it, the Great Kaan would soon bring them under subjection to him. [9]

In this country the brazil which we make use of grows in great plenty; and they also have gold in incredible quantity. They have elephants likewise, and much game. In this kingdom too are gathered all the porcelain shells which are used for small change in all those regions, as the Book has told before. There is nothing else to mention except that this is a very wild region, visited by few people; nor does the king desire that any strangers should frequent the country, and so find out about his treasure and other resources. [10]

— A textual note: most manuscripts read “when you leave Java” for the Condore departure; Marsden, Yule, and Pauthier restore Chamba — else the route is thrown into the Southern Ocean, and early modern maps invent a phantom southern continent from Locac. Sondur and Condur are the Condore group the Arabs knew as Sundar Fúlát. Locac remains debated: Chinese *Lo-hoh* / *Sien-Lo*, Ligor and the east coast of the Malay Peninsula, the Thai expansion of Rama Khamhaeng almost coincident with Polo’s years. Products — Peninsula gold, sappan brazil, elephants, Gulf cowries — fit. [11] Anyway, next he sails south.

When you leave Locac and sail for five hundred miles towards the south, according to the book, you come to an island called Pentam, a very wild place. All the wood that grows thereon consists of odoriferous trees. Then sail about sixty miles further between those two Islands — between the mainland continuation of Locac and the Island of Pentam. Throughout this distance there is but four paces’ depth of water, so that great ships in passing this channel have to lift their rudders, for they draw nearly as much water as that. And when you have gone these sixty miles, and again about thirty more, you come to an Island which forms a Kingdom, and is called Malaiur. The people have a King of their own, and a peculiar language. The city is a fine and noble one, and there is great trade carried on there. All kinds of spicery are to be found there, and all other necessaries of life. [12]

Thus the southern Ocean road narrows to its throat: Pentam — Bintan / Bentan — wild and sweet-wooded; sixty miles of shoal water where the junks raise the rudder — the Strait of Singapore as later navigators named the Gobernador passage between peninsula and Bintan; then Malaiur, a kingdom and a noble trading city of spice and provision, whose exact seat scholars divide between Palembang’s Malayo, an early Singapore, and a coast opposite the strait. Malacca itself is excluded as not yet founded. Five hundred miles here and sixty there remain schematic — pilot days rounded into hundreds of miles — but the sequence is the sequence of the sea: Condore landmark, Locac coast, Bintan approaches, shoal strait, Malay market, then Sumatra. The Book’s next departure will be from Pentam toward Java the Less: six Sumatran kingdoms, monsoon delay, and the return logic that will carry a princess home. [13]

Soft close here: Chamba’s elephants and claimed year; Java’s overstated circuit and the Kaan’s failed grasp; Condore’s twin names; Locac’s closed gold; Pentam’s shoals and Malaiur’s market — the road from Zayton toward India made visible as a chain of havens, digressions, and disputed names. Volume III will next enter Java the Less kingdom by kingdom; for this chapter it is enough that the southern Ocean road has been opened under the mixed tag of sight and report — Champa possibly under Marco’s own feet, Java and Locac chiefly under other men’s sails.
