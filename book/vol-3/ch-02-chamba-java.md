# Chapter III-2
## Chamba, Java, Locac, Pentam: The Southern Ocean Road

> *“You must know that on leaving the port of Zayton you sail west-south-west for 1500 miles, and then you come to a country called Chamba, a very rich region… And I can assure you the Great Kaan never could get possession of this Island, on account of its great distance…”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 3, chs. 5–6 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**


---

On leaving Zayton (Quanzhou) this time, take the south-westerly course. At the end of fifteen hundred miles by the Book’s reckoning, there is a king whose tribute to the Great Kaan comes on four legs. It’ll take more than a clerk to get it to the emperor. Think of the space and food needed to keep a party of elephants on the move, and how long before somebody sees them coming and knows the king has sent something. Chamba (Champa) is rich in its own right, and the Book makes it pay in elephants alone [1]. The man who sees them file past can tell what each one is worth without knowing how much trouble the king has had in keeping his kingdom. First listen to the story of how he struck the bargain.

A messenger leaving the court of Chamba has to carry the best offer an old king can make. In the Book a Mongol army of horse and foot is doing what it pleases with the country. Polo says Sagatu (Sogetu) came in 1278 [2]. Ramusio calls the king Accambale [3]. His envoys are to say that their lord held his lands peaceably for a great many years. He’ll acknowledge Cublay (Kublai) as overlord and give him as many elephants as he wants, if only he’ll call off his commander [4]. The envoys have to make the offer sound good enough for the emperor to want to be thought generous. They can leave the old king out of sight of the destruction for a moment and have the emperor picture him as he would like to be seen, a man who has lived long enough to prefer quiet to war. The people who would have to come out and drive the elephants have every reason to hope the envoys can put it well.

The Book gives them their way. The emperor takes pity, gives his army somewhere else to go, and gets twenty of the best elephants every year [5]. Now turn to the men who kept the court record. In 1278, Sagatu opened relations and the court invested the king as Prince of Chamba. The invasion began late in 1282. Sagatu withdrew in 1284, and the court spoke of his force as having been routed [6]. The offer of yearly tribute and the delivery of three elephants followed the withdrawal. The number twenty occurs in a different entry for a different country. The court records it as an offer from Mien (Burma) in 1297 [7]. The elephant in the court record is a real piece of business. It takes three of them and a different order of events to carry us quite a way from the details in the Book.

At home, according to Polo, the king sees every woman before she can marry. He has the choice of taking her for himself and gives a dowry to help her find a husband if he lets her go [8]. The Book credits the household with 326 children and can find at least 150 sons fit to carry arms. Odoric, who came later, also passes on a report of a king with hundreds of children [9]. Try taking the numbers from whoever has to keep count at the court. There is a long way from how big a family looks to a visitor to who can tell him the number of people in it.

Polo dates his own visit to 1285 in Yule’s text. Other witnesses have 1280 and 1288, and it is the latter date Yule entertains for a visit on a voyage before the end of Polo’s service at court [10]. A mention in the Book that Marco was in a country gives you a thing to work on. It would be very convenient if the copy in your hand and the copy on the next desk gave you the same year. The three readings would put him in rather different circumstances. Set the earliest one beside the Yuan dates and he would be there before the invasion. Set the middle one beside them and the commander is already on his way to his death in another campaign. The year can determine what the visitor would have had to ask about when he came ashore.

A merchant looking for something to buy finds fragrant lignaloes here [11]. There’s also the wood Polo calls *bonus*. Put a chessman made of it beside a pen-case of the same make and you’ll understand why he wants to tell us how black it is. The name is close to the Persian word for ebony. The things people make from it have their own journey to make once they are finished, and the name has travelled already by the time it reaches this account [12].

---

Ask the mariners about Java and they give the Book a ship’s bearing between south and south-east from Chamba and fifteen hundred miles to sail. The island, they say, is the greatest in the world. It is more than three thousand miles round in the reading Yule uses [13]. That would be a lot of coast to see out of one ship. The Book has a king there who owes tribute to nobody. People who come here out of Manzi (South China) take back a good return each year in the Book’s account [14]. An emperor considering an expedition which might occupy ships for many months has a different sum to do from a merchant coming to this quay with a cargo. The trader can go home with a profit and return another year. The emperor needs his army to get there in useful shape in the first place.

At the quay there is black pepper, nutmeg, spikenard, galingale, cubeb and clove to buy. The Book says the island produces them [15]. Take the man who buys a load and ask him where the cloves grew. He may be able to name the ship that brought them to the port and have rather less to say about the tree. Yule thinks of Java as a market for cloves and nutmeg and Hall describes a trade in which rice from Java was exchanged for spices from farther east. The list gives us a merchant’s view of what he can get, and the way each spice reached his hands needs evidence of its own [16].

For the emperor the cost is a different matter. The Book credits the distance and expense with keeping the island out of his hands [17]. The Yuan record describes a real expedition in 1292–93. The envoy whose face was branded is called Meng Qi in Gao Xing’s biography [18]. The ships and men went out under Shi Bi, Ike Mese and Gao Xing. The court histories give different totals for the ships and distinguish preparations from the force that sailed [19]. One of the commanders gets his men ashore, they take Daha, and a former ally, Wijaya, turns on his escort. By the time of the return to Zayton, Shi Bi’s biography reckons that the expedition had lost more than three thousand dead [20]. That includes the losses on the whole undertaking. There’s a great deal of work in getting a fleet so far from home. Once it lands, the men have to learn whom they can trust to go down the road with them.

---

We have a choice of bearings to make. The copies Yule knew tell us to go from Java to the next two islands. He follows Pauthier and puts us back in Chamba. Give the pilot seven hundred miles to the south and west from there and you get Sondur and Condur (Pulo Condore), the larger and the smaller island as the Book has them [21]. The Book hurries past them with nothing else it wishes to tell. On the deck where a man has spent days needing something to come into view, it might be enough to know which of the two will heave up first. Beyond them is Locac (probably Lopburi), a name that has sent readers looking in very different places. The Book gives the king a country so hard to get into that he can afford to keep it to himself. Ramusio specifies that it’s on the mainland [22].

Polo’s greatest interest after the strength of the position is in what a trader might get out of it. There is gold, he has heard, with elephants and game in the country. There is also brazilwood. The Book assumes its audience knows the dye, which is a small measure of how far the trade already reaches. Someone who knows what a piece of it can do for the colour of a length of cloth might be quite as glad to meet the man who supplies that as the man who promises gold [23]. There are even the small shells the Book has already shown changing hands as money [24].

The Book would have us think the best thing for the king is to keep a stranger from seeing these riches for himself [25].

— It’s worth stopping on the name. Pelliot’s reading is the one we’ve just used. Yule’s eye was drawn towards Sukhothai by an inscription dated to 1292, which seemed almost contemporary to him. The authenticity of the inscription has since been questioned. Locac gives us the name as the Book uses it, with rather more argument than you could put on a sign pointing down the road [26].

From Locac the Book goes five hundred miles south to Pentam (probably Bintan). It gives the island a crop of fragrant trees that hardly allows room for any other kind. Pelliot’s reading puts us near the Singapore Strait [27]. Come nearer and watch what happens to the rudder. The water is just four paces deep along this passage, by Polo’s measure. It takes only a little less than that to float the ship. They have to lift the rudder to get through [28]. Think how long sixty miles can be when every little change of depth matters. Somebody who has just paid for a cabin can come up to see why the ship is taking such care. The man who knows where the shoal ends has something worth far more than a good story about what grows on the shore.

Another thirty miles in the Book brings up Malaiur (possibly Malayu in the Jambi region). The traders there can supply spices as well as what you need to go on with the voyage. Polo puts a king there and gives his people a language of their own [29]. The places that have been proposed would lead us on different tracks. What the trader who knows the market tells you is that this is a very good place to be short of provisions. In a ship supplied for the next leg, you can afford to look up from the cargo again.

We’re through the shoals now. Before long the next piece of land will give a man rather more kings to reckon with on one island.

## Notes

[1] ~ Yule’s text gives fifteen hundred miles between the ports. The tribute in elephants is the Book’s account, while the gifts in the Yuan record have their own chronology [@yc, 3.5 n. 1].

[2] ✗ The Book dates Sagatu’s invasion to 1278. The Yuan record for that year gives a diplomatic mission and investiture [@yc, 3.5; @yuanshi1370, juan 210].

[3] ~ From Ramusio in Yule’s edition. The bracketed form of the king’s name is Accambale [@yc, 3.5].

[4] ~ The old king’s message makes the offer of elephants the price of calling off the invading army. The speech belongs to Polo’s account [@yc, 3.5].

[5] ✗ The Book gives an order to withdraw motivated by pity and a yearly tribute of twenty elephants. The sequence and figure differ in the Yuan record [@yc, 3.5; @yuanshi1370, juan 13, 210].

[6] ✓ The campaign began late in 1282. The Yuan history records Sagatu’s withdrawal in the third month of 1284 and describes his routed force. Sagatu died in the Annam campaign in 1285 [@yuanshi1370, juan 13, 129, 210].

[7] ✓ A request for recall, a promise of tribute and a gift of three elephants follow the withdrawal in the court record. The yearly offer of twenty tame elephants is recorded for Mien in 1297 [@yuanshi1370, juan 13, 210].

[8] ~ The Book explains the scale of the king’s household through the right it says he exercises over marriages. The claims about first seeing the women and giving a dowry are details of that account [@yc, 3.5].

[9] ~ The count of children and of sons fit for arms belongs to Polo’s account. Odoric reports a later story of some two hundred royal children in Chamba [@yc, 3.5 n. 2].

[10] ? Pauthier and Ramusio have 1280, the Geographic Text has 1285, and the Geographic Latin has 1288. Yule prefers 1288 and connects it tentatively with a mission to the Indian seas before a return to court about 1290 [@yc, 3.5 n. 2].

[11] ✓ Yule describes the importance of Chamba as a source of eaglewood. The fragrant wood specified by Polo is a trade article mentioned by other witnesses [@yc, 3.5 n. 3].

[12] ~ Yule connects the name for the ebony to the Persian *abnūs*. He also finds a possible Persian word behind the Book’s term for the pen-case [@yc, 3.5 n. 3].

[13] ✗ Yule prints three thousand miles from the Geographic Text and notes five thousand in Pauthier. He regards even the lower figure as roughly twice the circuit [@yc, 3.6 n. 1].

[14] ~ The Book assigns the island its own king without an overlord and makes the trade highly profitable for merchants of the Chinese coast. This is its description of the state of affairs at the time [@yc, 3.6].

[15] ~ The list is presented as a list of produce in the Book. Yule distinguishes his understanding of production from the range of goods that could be traded there [@yc, 3.6 n. 1].

[16] ~ Yule calls Java a market for cloves and nutmeg. Hall discusses Java’s exchange of rice for spices further east, which offers an explanation for their presence without deciding where each item on the list was cultivated [@yc, 3.6 n. 1; @hall2011history, pp. 226–227].

[17] ~ Polo explains the emperor’s inability to take the island by the distance and cost of an expedition. Yule treats this chapter as a digression from the route to India and considers possible earlier travel separately [@yc, 3.6 n. 1].

[18] ✓ The Java entry names the envoy Meng by his office and describes the marking of his face. The name Meng Qi is given in Gao Xing’s biography [@yuanshi1370, juan 162, 210].

[19] ~ The histories name the same three commanders and give different figures for the fleet. One records an initial figure of twenty thousand men and says only five thousand went [@yuanshi1370, juan 17, 162, 210].

[20] ✓ The Yuan account includes the taking of Daha, the attack by Wijaya and the withdrawal in 1293. The toll of more than three thousand in Shi Bi’s biography is reckoned by the time the expedition returned to Zayton [@yuanshi1370, juan 17, 162, 210].

[21] ~ Yule follows Pauthier’s substitution of Chamba for Java and explains Marsden’s case for the bearing. He associates the two island names with the Pulo Condore group [@yc, 3.7 nn. 1–2].

[22] ~ The Book gives the place a king and his own authority. Ramusio supplies the brief specification of mainland location that Yule brackets [@yc, 3.7].

[23] ~ The brazilwood, gold, elephants and game are given as local resources by the Book. Yule considers how the evidence for the commodities might bear on the identification [@yc, 3.7 n. 3].

[24] ~ The Book calls the small shells porcelain and gives them as a source of currency. See [II-7, where they change hands](../vol-2/ch-07-tebet-carajan.md) [@yc, 3.7].

[25] ~ In the Book the king tries to prevent visits in order to keep strangers from discovering his wealth. This is Polo’s explanation of why so few people go [@yc, 3.7].

[26] ? Pelliot favours Lopburi. Yule uses an inscription of Sukhothai dated 1292–93, which he thought almost exactly contemporary with Polo’s voyage. Its authenticity was later questioned [@pelliot1959notes, “Locac”; @yc, 3.7 n. 3].

[27] ~ Pelliot identifies Pentam with Bintan and proposes a route through the Singapore Strait. Polo’s distance and the claim about the trees are particulars of the account [@pelliot1959notes, entry 313; @yc, 3.8 n. 1].

[28] ~ The span of shallow water, the four paces and the action with the rudder are Polo’s. Yule compares reports of similar navigation and discusses which passages fit [@yc, 3.8 n. 2].

[29] ? The Book describes an island kingdom with supplies and a trading city. Malayu in the Jambi region is one proposal for the identification, while Yule discusses other possibilities [@yc, 3.8 n. 3; @hall2011history].
