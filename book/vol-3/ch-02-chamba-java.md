# Chapter III-2
## Chamba, Java, Locac, Pentam: The Southern Ocean Road

> *“You must know that on leaving the port of Zayton you sail west-south-west for 1500 miles, and then you come to a country called Chamba, a very rich region… And I can assure you the Great Kaan never could get possession of this Island, on account of its great distance…”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 3, chs. 5–6 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**


---

Haul up the heavy stone anchor off Zayton (Quanzhou) and let the ship fall away south-west down the coast. Fifteen hundred miles by the pilot’s log brings your prow into Chamba (Champa), a rich lowland kingdom whose yearly tax to Cambaluc (Beijing) travels overland on four thick legs [1]. Stop a moment to picture that tax column before a single clerk opens his ledger. Twenty towering war-elephants require teams of mahouts, mountains of bamboo fodder, broad river barges, and months of patient marching north along dusty roads. Nobody watching twenty royal beasts swing out through coastal palms mistakes Chamba’s master for an ordinary governor. Listen first to the Book’s tale of how he struck that peace bargain.

Across Chamba’s burned stockades and trampled ricefields, Mongol cavalry and infantry sweep everything before them under a commander Marco calls Sagatu (Sogetu), arriving by the Book’s count in 1278 [2]. Ramusio’s edition supplies the aged monarch’s name, Accambale [3]. Out ride Accambale’s breathless envoys past blackened bamboo fences to kneel before Cublay (Kublai) Kaan. Their lord is an old man, they plead, who has governed his realm in peace for many long years without picking a quarrel. Only call off Sagatu’s horsemen, and Accambale will own Cublay as overlord, place his territories at the court’s absolute disposal, and send every year as many fine elephants as the emperor asks [4]. Every polished phrase flatters an emperor sitting thousands of miles away who prefers acting mercifully toward a yielding greybeard to bleeding treasure in tropical bush. Out along Chamba’s scorched hills, peasants creeping back toward ruined villages have every reason to pray those messengers speak persuasively.

Cublay softens in the Book’s telling, orders Sagatu to march his army against other lands, and receives twenty of Chamba’s biggest and finest elephants every single year [5]. Lay the official Chinese court annals beside Marco’s war story, however, and both dates and totals twist sharply out of joint. In 1278 Sagatu had opened peaceful diplomacy and invested Chamba’s sovereign as a tributary prince. The real military campaign struck late in 1282, bogged down amid monsoon rain, jungle stockades, and burning fever, and ended with Sagatu’s battered regiments retreating in the spring of 1284 before he fell fighting in Annam a year later [6]. Envoys followed that retreat bearing formal peace pledges and a one-time gift of three live elephants. Meanwhile the standing annual pledge of twenty tame beasts sits inside another court roll altogether—a later diplomatic promise from Mien (Burma) recorded in 1297 [7]. Real elephants walk steadily through Yuan chancery files, yet three sent north after a stinging withdrawal paint a far sterner war than twenty yielded to imperial pity.

Stepping inside Chamba’s palace gates, Marco shifts from battle smoke to the royal nursery with his usual appetite for staggering sums. No maiden in the kingdom may marry, he declares, until she has first stood inspection before the king. Whomever catches the sovereign’s eye enters his household; each girl he passes over receives a modest wedding chest out of royal silver to help her win a husband [8]. Counting up that immense domestic establishment, the Book credits the king with 326 children, among them at least 150 sons old enough to lift shield and spear. A generation later, Friar Odoric picked up a very similar port rumour counting two hundred royal children round Chamba’s court [9]. Imagine a tired palace clerk trying to tally that noisy yard full of princes for a foreign trader scribbling on the steps.

Marco places his own landing in Chamba in 1285 in Yule’s main text, whereas competing manuscript witnesses print 1280 and 1288—the last being Yule’s cautious choice for a late imperial sea mission [10]. Three scribbled numerals across three dusty manuscripts rebuild the entire waterfront before your boots touch sand. Arrive in 1280, and Fujian shipwrights have not yet launched Sagatu’s war-junks. Step ashore in 1285, and the Mongol baron lies freshly killed on the northern river frontier. Anchor in 1288, and bruised towns have settled back into shipping timber and elephants. One tiny pen-stroke determines whether smoke still hangs above the palms when the traveller reads the shore.

Turn down toward the harbour scales instead, where fragrant wood and glossy craftwork keep steadier company than dates. Brokers lean over piled billets of sweet lignaloes carried out from forest valleys [11] beside dark heavy logs of a timber Marco names *bonus* [12]. Pick up a smooth carved chessman beside a slim turned inkhorn pen-case of *bonus*, and you watch the Venetian merchant’s son weigh lustre and close grain across his fingers. Persia’s own word for ebony sits right inside that harbour name, already borne across two oceans before a Genoese prisoner wrote the dictation down.

---

Back aboard ship, pilots swing their prows between south and south-east for fifteen hundred miles across deep blue swell until Java lifts dark green along the sky [13]. Seasoned mariners swear it is the widest island on earth, measuring over three thousand miles around in the Franco-Italian text [14]. That vast circuit would keep a swift ocean junk beating down one unbroken coast from midsummer well into deep winter. Yet Java answers to no mainland overlord. Its treasury overflows beyond counting, and bold shipping syndicates crowding in from Manzi (South China) load roaring profits off its timber wharves year after year [15]. An emperor eyeing an unsubdued island does slower arithmetic than a merchant syndicate. A private skipper buys pepper, clears the cape ahead of bad weather, and sleeps safely behind Zayton’s sea-walls. An emperor must keep twenty thousand fighting men fed across weeks of unpredictable monsoon swell.

Walk Java’s crowded timber jetties and open the heavy woven spice-sacks one by one: sharp wrinkled black pepper, fragrant brown nutmegs wrapped in crimson mace, hairy spikenard roots, sliced galingale, tailed cubebs, and aromatic dried cloves [16]. Lean closer to a harbour clerk tipping baskets onto the beam-scale and ask him which hillside grove grew those dark nail-like cloves. He is far likelier to nod eastward toward arriving outriggers than point up the misty volcanoes behind his town. Where the Book presents every spice cleanly as Java’s native crop, Yule reads the great island as an ocean clearing-house, and Kenneth Hall traces Javanese rice fleets sailing east to barter grain for Moluccan cloves and nutmeg [17]. What a busy quay shows Marco is the woven sack passing from balance pan to plank, not the distant outer shore where the flower bud dried in the sun.

Cublay never brought this island under tribute, the Book explains calmly, because vast distance across high seas and ruinous freight costs kept his armies away [18]. Turn barely a decade forward in Chinese annals, however, and an imperial armada genuinely crosses those waves in 1292–93 after Java’s ruler brands the cheek of a Mongol envoy whom Gao Xing’s biography names Meng Qi [19]. Three battle-hardened generals—Shi Bi, Ike Mese, and Gao Xing—assemble a mixed expedition variously recorded from an initial twenty-thousand levy down to five thousand troops actually sailing [20]. Once ashore, imperial columns break local lines and storm the walled city of Daha. Then their crafty Javanese ally Wijaya turns sharply upon his own Mongol escort and harries broken regiments back down to the boats, costing Shi Bi’s command more than three thousand dead before battered hulls limp back into Zayton [21]. Distance and expense never stopped the Great Kaan’s fleet from weighing anchor; treachery on muddy inland roads ruined the venture once it landed.

---

Now the pilot’s sea-bearing slips between competing manuscripts. Where Yule’s main French witness drops south-west directly from Java, Yule adopts Pauthier’s reading back to Chamba so that seven hundred miles south-south-west carries your ship cleanly past Sondur and Condur (Pulo Condore), twin green islands rising crisp above the delta shoals [22]. Marco spares neither rock more than a lookout’s hail before holding course. After empty weeks beneath glaring sky, two unmistakable wooded peaks off the beam earn their ink line in the log without needing a king or a pepper bazaar.

Five hundred miles south-east beyond those twin islands stretches Locac (probably Lopburi), guarded so securely by treacherous shallows and rugged ridges that no invader enters easily to trouble its peace—while Ramusio adds a crisp geographical note placing Locac firmly on the mainland [23]. Shut harbour gates never stop Marco from reckoning the riches piled behind the palms. Rumour heaps bright alluvial gold in staggering abundance alongside wild game, tame and wild elephants, and thick groves of red-dyed brazilwood [24]. Even small glossy cowry shells—the “porcelain” coinage already changing hands across Carajan (Yunnan)’s inland fairs—circulate briskly in the markets here, tying river plains straight into Indian Ocean small change [25]. In Marco’s telling, Locac’s suspicious ruler bars every road precisely so foreign skippers never smell out his goldfields [26].

— Pause a moment beside that stubborn name Locac. Paul Pelliot maps it squarely onto an ancient kingdom along Siam’s lower plain, whereas Yule backed Sukhothai after reading a stone inscription dated 1292–93 that seemed thrillingly close to Marco’s passage—though modern epigraphers have raised sharp doubts over that stone’s date [27].

Heave the stone anchor off the sand and drop five hundred miles south from Locac to Pentam (probably Bintan), a wild island forested from shore to crest with sweet-smelling timber beside the narrow throat of the Singapore Strait [28]. Step right back to the high sternrail now and watch deckhands brace their bare heels against the windlass bars. Look down over the taffrail into milky green surf. For sixty anxious miles between those low wooded islands, pale water shoals to barely four paces deep—leaving scarcely a finger’s clearance beneath a heavy junk’s oak keel. Every master knows what one submerged coral shelf will do to a loaded ship. So the helmsmen slacken the main sheet, rig heavy block-and-tackle gear across the poop, and haul the giant axial rudder clean upward out of its pintles until it rides safely clear of the sand [29]. Picture how slowly sixty miles crawls past at half sail while every bright ripple over yellow sand makes the sailing master roar for the sounding lead. A trader who paid bright silver for a private fir cabin back at Zayton discovers right here that his voyage depends less on locked cabin doors than on a bare-chested leadsman singing depths from the prow.

Thirty miles clear of the sandbanks rises Malaiur (possibly Malayu in the Jambi region), a handsome island city ruled by its own king and humming with spices and daily ship-stores [30]. Cool water butts, salted sea-fish, white rice, and fragrant spice bales rattle down busy wharves while traders bargain in a tongue all their own. For a crew exhausted from nursing a lifted rudder over sixty miles of shallows, an honest provisioning wharf beats every distant fable of alluvial gold.

Watch the lead-line sink cleanly into deep blue water past those narrows. Ahead looms an island two thousand miles around, parcelled among eight jealous kings and stripped bare of the Pole Star—where our crew will dig trenches across sandy beaches and wait out five squally months for the monsoon wind to swing home.

## Notes

[1] ~ Fifteen hundred miles west-south-west from Zayton to Chamba reflects coastal sailing estimates [@yc, 3.5 n. 1]. The annual elephant tribute compresses years of contested Cham–Yuan diplomacy into one dramatic concession.

[2] ✗ The Book dates Sagatu’s invasion to 1278, whereas Yuan annals record peaceful diplomacy and royal investiture in that year [@yc, 3.5; @yuanshi1370, juan 210]. Actual military hostilities broke out four years later when Champa detained Yuan envoys.

[3] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule prints the king’s name Accambale inside square brackets solely from Ramusio’s 1559 text [@yc, 3.5]. The historical Cham ruler who resisted the Mongol invasion was Indravarman V.

[4] ~ The ageing sovereign’s plea offering elephants to buy off invading cavalry belongs to the Book’s diplomatic set-piece [@yc, 3.5]. In reality, the old king withdrew into the interior mountains and waged a prolonged guerrilla defence against the landing force.

[5] ✗ Imperial pity stopping the war for twenty elephants every year reverses both the military defeat and the tribute count in Yuan annals [@yc, 3.5; @yuanshi1370, juan 13, 210]. The Mongol expedition failed to hold the country and eventually had to withdraw.

[6] ✓ The *Yuan shi* places Sagatu’s actual invasion in late 1282, records his routed retreat in the third month of 1284, and notes his death in Annam in 1285 [@yuanshi1370, juan 13, 129, 210]. His campaign ended in disaster rather than the bloodless pacification Polo narrates.

[7] ✓ Chamba sent a recall request and a gift of three elephants after Sagatu retreated [@yuanshi1370, juan 13, 210]. A regular annual tribute of twenty tame elephants appears in the annals for Mien (Burma) in 1297 rather than for Champa.

[8] ~ The royal bride-inspection privilege and dowry payments appear in Polo’s Chamba chapter without outside contemporary confirmation [@yc, 3.5]. Similar tales of royal marriage prerogatives recur in his descriptions of distant courts.

[9] ~ Counting 326 children and 150 armed sons fits medieval court hyperbole [@yc, 3.5 n. 2]. Friar Odoric similarly credited the polygamous king of Champa with a good two hundred children [@odoric1913travels, ch. 23; @yc, 3.5 n. 2].

[10] ? Pauthier and Ramusio date Polo’s call to 1280, the Geographic Text prints 1285, and the Geographic Latin reads 1288—Yule’s preferred choice for a court sea mission [@yc, 3.5 n. 2]. Whether Marco landed at Champa on an earlier mission or during the homeward bridal voyage remains debated.

[11] ✓ Chamba was renowned across Asian and European merchant guides for aromatic lignaloes (eaglewood or aloeswood) [@yc, 3.5 n. 3]. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa and Arab geographers regularly singled out Cham aloeswood as the finest in the East.

[12] ~ Yule connects *bonus* to Persian *abnūs* (ebony) and notes a Persian craft word behind the Book’s term for ink- and pen-cases [@yc, 3.5 n. 3]. The vocabulary points again to the Persian commercial idiom spoken by foreign merchants in Yuan service.

[13] ~ Sailing fifteen hundred miles between south and south-east from Chamba overstates the sea passage to Java [@yc, 3.6 n. 1]. Polo did not visit Great Java himself and relies here on pilots' estimates.

[14] ✗ Three thousand miles in the Geographic Text (and five thousand in Pauthier) roughly doubles Java’s actual coast circuit [@yc, 3.6 n. 1]. Even early Arab and Chinese notices struggled to measure the island's southern ocean shore.

[15] ~ Javanese independence from Cambaluc and lively Fujian sea traffic fit conditions just prior to the 1292 war [@yc, 3.6]. Junk captains from Zayton and Fuju traded briskly at Javanese ports despite the lack of political submission.

[16] ~ Pepper, nutmeg, spikenard, galingale, cubebs, and cloves are presented by the Book as Java’s local produce [@yc, 3.6 n. 1]. The list reflects the assorted spice cargoes weighed and loaded by Chinese junks at Javanese entrepôts.

[17] ~ Yule reads Java as a regional spice mart rather than the grower of cloves and nutmeg, which came from the Moluccas and Banda Islands [@yc, 3.6 n. 1]. Hall documents Java’s active rice-for-spice exchange with the eastern islands without settling local cultivation claims for 1290 [@hall2011history, pp. 226–227].

[18] ~ Explaining Cublay’s restraint by the length and danger of the sea voyage overlooks or predates the actual 1292 naval campaign [@yc, 3.6 n. 1]. If Marco took his notes before leaving China, the fleet that sailed from Quanzhou late in 1292 may have departed after his own embarkation.

[19] ✓ Chinese annals confirm the 1292–93 expedition provoked when Java’s ruler Kertanagara branded the face of the Yuan envoy Meng Qi, named in Gao Xing’s biography [@yuanshi1370, juan 162, 210]. That diplomatic humiliation triggered Cublay's last great overseas war.

[20] ~ Yuan biographies list commanders Shi Bi, Ike Mese, and Gao Xing at the head of the expedition [@yuanshi1370, juan 17, 162, 210]. The records distinguish an initial twenty-thousand troop order from smaller numbers that actually sailed from Quanzhou.

[21] ✓ Yuan chronicles record the capture of Daha, Raden Wijaya’s subsequent ambush of his Mongol escort, and over three thousand dead before Shi Bi sailed back to Zayton [@yuanshi1370, juan 17, 162, 210]. See the campaign comparison table in this chapter.

[22] ~ Yule follows Pauthier in restarting the coastal bearing from Chamba rather than Java and identifies Sondur and Condur with the Pulo Condore islands (Côn Đảo) off southern Vietnam [@yc, 3.7 nn. 1–2]. Those twin islands served for centuries as a standard seamark for ships rounding into the Gulf of Siam or heading toward the Straits.

[23] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Locac’s secluded king stands in the base French text, while Ramusio supplies the short geographical cue placing it on the mainland [@yc, 3.7]. That mainland note helps separate Locac from the island chapters around it.

[24] ~ Gold, wild elephants, and red brazilwood (sappanwood) represent key mainland Southeast Asian exports noted across the Book [@yc, 3.7 n. 3]. Sappanwood from the Gulf of Siam littoral was a major bulk dyewood cargo in the China trade.

[25] ~ Small white porcelain cowries circulate as currency here just as in the Yunnan markets [@yc, 3.7]. See [Chapter II-7](../vol-2/ch-07-tebet-carajan.md) for Polo's earlier account of cowrie money imported from the southern seas.

[26] ~ Barring strangers to keep royal bullion hidden reflects Polo’s commercial explanation for why so few merchants visited the interior of Locac [@yc, 3.7]. A port off the main monsoon track easily acquired a reputation for jealously guarded gold.

[27] ? Pelliot connects Locac with Lopburi (Lavo) in lower Siam, whereas Yule favoured Ligor (Nakhon Si Thammarat) on the Malay Peninsula and cited a 1292–93 Sukhothai inscription whose authenticity scholars later challenged [@pelliot1959notes, “Locac”; @yc, 3.7 n. 3]. The exact polity remains contested.

[28] ~ Pelliot places Pentam at Bintan Island overlooking the eastern entrance to the Singapore Strait [@pelliot1959notes, entry 313; @yc, 3.8 n. 1]. See [Map A16, the return route](ch-09-cocachin-return.md#fig-a16-cocachin-return) for the approach to the Straits.

[29] ~ Lifting giant stern rudders through sixty miles of four-pace water accurately captures real junk handling across the shallow Riau and Singapore strait channels [@yc, 3.8 n. 2]. Deep-draught Chinese hulls had to unship or hoist their axial rudders to clear the shoals.

[30] ? A walled provisioning city with its own language and spice shops points toward Malayu in Jambi on Sumatra's east coast, though exact strait identifications vary between mainland and island entrepôts [@yc, 3.8 n. 3; @hall2011history]. From Malaiur the fleet crosses directly to the northern kingdoms of Sumatra.
