# Back Matter III-1
## Trivia, Disputes, and Afterlives — Volume III

> *“…the information given by one Marco Polo, a Florentine Physician!”*
> — Juan de Mariana, *Historia general de España* (1601), on Columbus’s sources, in Yule–Cordier, Introduction, § 74 n. 3

---

Volume III ends the travel and opens the echo. Trivia here covers the ocean book’s favourite marvels; disputes cover gold, brides, and belief; afterlives point toward Columbus, maps, and the modern Wood debate without replacing chapters III-11 and III-12. Pocket form first; long form in those chapters. [1]

### Trivia worth keeping

**Ships of Manzi were the other marvel.** Before Chipangu’s gold, Polo describes large Chinese ocean-going ships — multiple masts, bulkheads, crews — that startled Mediterranean sailors used to different naval habits. Needham and maritime historians still cite these passages as early Latin witness to East Asian ship technology (with the usual manuscript-number caution).

**Five months in Sumatra.** The monsoon wait in Java the Less is one of the more human logistical truths in Book III. Empires move by wind permission. Dull waits are evidence: forgers rarely invent them, and romancers rarely cherish them.

**Adam’s Peak wears two religions on the page.** Seilan’s holy mountain gathers Muslim, Hindu, and Christian footstep traditions in the book’s telling — a neat reminder that sacred geography travels with sailors.

**Pepper and pearls were arguments for believing him.** Maabar’s fisheries and Malabar’s pepper were products Europe already craved; concrete commodities made some readers trust the wilder neighbours on the page.

**The name Madagascar.** *Madeigascar* is among the names European letters largely owe to Polo’s tradition — even when the chapter’s content mixes island and continental African hearsay.

**Milione as nickname.** Back in Venice, tradition says Marco was teased as *Milione* — the man of millions — for his tall counts. Whether affectionate or mocking, the nickname stuck to house and book.

**Trebizond closes the wet book.** The Black Sea gate at Trebizond returns the traveller to a world Venice already understood — which is why the homecoming feels sudden after so many strange coasts.

**The book outran the man.** Catalan Atlas (1375), Fra Mauro, and later cartographers lifted Polo names into map skin. Columbus annotated his Latin Polo. Print multiplied the witness far beyond one Venetian household.

### Disputed claims and pointers

**Chipangu gold.** Japan exists; Mongol invasion attempts existed; roofs of gold are marvel speech. The dispute is not “did Polo invent Japan?” but “how far did hearsay and rhetorical gold reshape a real archipelago for European desire?” Columbus’s westward hope is the loudest consequence. [2]

**Cocachin / Kökechin embassy.** That a Yuan princess travelled by sea toward an Ilkhanid marriage in this generation is grounded in cross-tradition historical discussion. Exact onomastics, passenger lists, and how neatly Polo’s role matches Persian notices remain scholarly matching work. High escort mortality is book tradition. Treat the political frame as strong and the novelistic detail as tradition. [3]

**Personal landings vs periplus report.** Chamba and Sumatran wintering lean visited; Great Java interior, many Arabian and African coasts, dog-headed Andaman ethnography lean reported or legendary. Chapter tags and the gazetteer are the guide. [4]

**Columbus and the westward misreading.** Columbus used Polo (and Toscanelli’s geography) inside a smaller-earth theory that could make Chipangu seem reachable by sailing west. That is reception history: Polo did not tell him to find the Caribbean. Pointer: chapter III-11. [5]

**Frances Wood and the “never went to China” debate.** Wood’s sceptical popular argument leans on silences (Great Wall, tea, footbinding, Chinese script, etc.) and on the possibility of second-hand compilation. Responses by Haw, Larner, de Rachewiltz, Vogel, and others defend a core of autopsy mixed with report, manuscript complexity, and genre expectations. This series’ closing essay (III-12) is the full method statement; here only the pointer belongs. [6]

**Great Wall silence (harvest).** Seeded in Volume I’s back matter, harvested here: absence of a tourist Wall set-piece is real in Polo; whether that absence disproves travel is the leap. Yuan-period wall functions and where a guest’s routes ran matter to the counter-argument.

**Ramusio’s homecoming theatre.** Unrecognised kinsmen, shabby Tartar dress, successive robe reveals, jewels in the seams — beloved sixteenth-century colour for the final return, not early French Prologue fact. Volume I already warned you; Volume III’s Venice chapter must not quietly reabsorb it as affidavit. [7]

### Afterlives in one breath

Maps borrowed his names. Explorers borrowed his horizons. Printers borrowed his authority. Sceptics borrowed his silences. The retelling’s job has been to narrate what the book narrates — orally, clearly — and to interrupt when the stones wobble. Trivia keeps delight available; disputes keep honesty available; afterlives remind you the story did not stop at the lagoon.

If you want the long form, read III-11 and III-12. If you want the pocket form, you are holding it. Either way, leave Volume III knowing the sea-road was monsoon and marriage as much as marvel — and that Europe’s later dreams of gold islands were part of the Book’s fortune, not always part of its itinerary.
