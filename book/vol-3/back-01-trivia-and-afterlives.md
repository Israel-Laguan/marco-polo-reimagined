# Back Matter III-1
## Trivia, Disputes, and Afterlives — Volume III

> *“…the information given by one Marco Polo, a Florentine Physician!”*
> — Juan de Mariana, *Historia general de España* (1601), on Columbus’s sources, in Yule–Cordier, Introduction, § 74 n. 3

---

Volume III closes the sea road and opens the book’s long echo. This companion gathers short trivia the chapters do not tell and a shortlist of disputed claims flagged inline.

### Trivia worth keeping

**Ships of Manzi were the other marvel.** Before the glitter of Chipangu (Japan), Polo describes the great ocean-going junks of Manzi (South China) — multiple masts, hull bulkheads, and large crews — that startled Mediterranean sailors used to different naval habits. Joseph Needham and maritime historians still cite these passages as an early Western witness to East Asian ship technology [1].

**Adam’s Peak wears two religions on the page.** The holy mountain of Seilan (Sri Lanka) gathers Muslim (Adam) and Buddhist (Sagamoni Borcan) traditions in the Book’s telling, while outside writers add Hindu (Śiva) and Portuguese Christian (St Thomas or the eunuch of Candace) claims — a neat reminder that sacred geography travels with sailors [2].

**Pepper, pearls, and a hereditary charmer.** The pearl banks of Maabar (the Coromandel Coast) and the pepper of Melibar (the Malabar Coast) anchored wilder ocean reports in trade goods Europe already craved, and the hereditary shark-charmer office Polo describes at the fishery survived into nineteenth-century British Ceylon, where James Emerson Tennent found the family head a Roman Catholic whose rite divers still considered indispensable [3].

**How Madeigascar named a different island.** Polo’s Madeigascar (Mogadishu) mixes Somali-coast camels, four *shaykhs*, and ivory with island hearsay, yet textual scholars trace the name itself to a scribal corruption of Mogadishu that Renaissance cartographers later transferred south onto the great island of “Madagascar” [4].

**Trebizond closes the wet book.** The Black Sea gate at Trebizond (Trabzon) returns the travellers to waters Venice already knew — and to a sharp commercial loss recorded in Maffeo’s 1310 will from dealings involving the Comnenus ruler at the threshold of home [5].

**The pasta legend.** Popular folklore credits Marco with bringing pasta back from China, though pasta was already established in Italy before 1295; Igor de Rachewiltz traces the Book’s wheat-pasta notice to Ramusio’s 1559 text and distinguishes its reading of *lasagne* from later translation labels [6].

### Disputed claims — the Volume III shortlist {#disputed-claims-and-pointers}

**Chipangu gold.** Japan and the failed Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 are historical, whereas palace roofs and floors of solid gold are mainland port hearsay that later fed European westward expectations; see III-1, Notes 24–31, and III-11, Notes 14–17 [7].

**Cocachin (Kököchin) embassy.** Rashīd al-Dīn and a 1290 *Yongle dadian* notice matched by Yang Zhijiu corroborate the bride embassy from Cathay to Argon’s (Arghun’s) son Casan (Ghazan) without naming the Polos, while the sailing date remains debated; see III-9, Notes 13 and 21–22 [8].

For the reception history from fourteenth-century world maps to Columbus, print, and modern scholarship, see [Chapter III-11](ch-11-afterlives.md); for the full ledger of hits, misses, and silences across all three volumes, see [Chapter III-12](ch-12-reliability-essay.md).

## Notes

[1] ✓ Polo’s opening description of four-masted Fujian junks with private merchant cabins and divided holds is examined in Joseph Needham’s study of Chinese nautical technology [@yc, 3.1 nn. 1–3; @needham1971civil] and compared with the excavated late-thirteenth-century Quanzhou hull in [Chapter III-1, Notes 1–11](ch-01-ships-chipangu.md#notes).

[2] ✓ In the Book’s account of Seilan, only Muslims (claiming Adam’s grave) and Buddhists (claiming Sagamoni Borcan) contest the summit of Adam’s Peak [@yc, 3.15 nn. 1, 5]. Tennent, quoted by Yule, records the separate Hindu (Śiva) and Portuguese Christian (St Thomas or Candace’s eunuch) footprint traditions outside Polo’s text [@tennent1859ceylon]; see [Chapter III-4, Notes 18 and 28](ch-04-necuveran-seilan.md#notes).

[3] ✓ Polo’s Abraiaman charmers receiving a twentieth of the oyster haul match the long-lived *kadal-katti* (“sea-binders”) of the Gulf of Mannar [@yc, 3.16 n. 2]. Tennent [-@tennent1859ceylon, vol. 2, pp. 564–565] observed in 1859 that the hereditary chief charmer, though by then a Roman Catholic, remained an indispensable fixture of the colonial fishery; see [Chapter III-5, Notes 7–8](ch-05-maabar-st-thomas.md#notes).

[4] ? Pelliot [-@pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, pp. 779–781] and Andreose [-@andreose2024textual, pp. 76–77] argue that *Madeigascar* arose from a scribal corruption of Mogadishu—preserved in intermediate forms such as *Mogdaxo* (Z), *Madaschor* (V), and *Mogclasio* (F’s chapter index)—before late-fifteenth-century mapmakers attached Polo’s name to the island of Madagascar [@yc, 3.33 n. 1]; see [Chapter III-7, Notes 13–16](ch-07-male-abash.md#notes).

[5] ✓ Maffeo Polo’s will of 6 February 1310 (1309 Venetian style) records nearly 4,000 hyperpera lost in dealings involving the Comnenus ruler of Trebizond and his territory, alongside a 1,000-lire receipt to Marco covering joint affairs [@moule1938description, vol. 1; @orlandini1926marco; @yc, Intro. § 23]; see [Chapter III-9, Note 27](ch-09-cocachin-return.md#notes).

[6] ✗ The popular story that Marco Polo introduced pasta to Italy is contradicted by pre-1295 Italian evidence. De Rachewiltz [-@derachewiltz1997marco, pp. 61–62] locates the Book’s Fansur bread-flour and wheat-pasta passage in Ramusio’s 1559 redaction [@yc, 3.11 n. 3] and distinguishes its reading of *lasagne* from later English translation labels.

[7] ✗ Chipangu’s golden palace roofs and two-finger-thick gold floors are hearsay marvels gathered at southern Chinese ports, whereas the 1274 and 1281 Mongol invasions of Japan are historical [@yc, 3.2–3; @rossabi1988khubilai, chs. 4, 8]. Larner [-@larner1999marco, ch. 9] disputes whether Polo’s Book directly shaped Columbus’s westward project before 1492, as he and Gil [-@gil2006marcocristobal, pp. 25–27, 30–34] argue that the surviving annotated Latin copy in Seville [@polo1484consuetudinibus; @shawcross2023materiality] probably reached Columbus around 1497–98; see [Chapter III-1, Notes 24–31](ch-01-ships-chipangu.md#notes), and [Chapter III-11, Notes 14–17](ch-11-afterlives.md#notes).

[8] ✓ The Yuan–Ilkhanid bride embassy is corroborated by Rashīd al-Dīn [@thackston1998rashiduddin, p. 606] and the 1290 *Yongle dadian* provisioning entry matched by Yang Zhijiu [@ma2025chinese], though neither source names the Polos [@jackson1998travels, pp. 98–99]. Yang’s inferred late-1290 or early-1291 departure and Yule’s early-1292 date remain debated; see [Chapter III-9, Notes 13 and 16–22](ch-09-cocachin-return.md#notes).
