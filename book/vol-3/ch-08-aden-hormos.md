# Chapter III-8
## Aden, Esher, Dufar, Calatu, Hormos Again

> *“You must know that in the province of ADEN there is a Prince who is called the Soldan… it is by this way through Aden that the Saracens of Alexandria receive all their stores of pepper and other spicery… ESHER… white incense… cattle… live upon small fish… DUFAR… incense… CALATU… HORMOS… houses built with ventilators to catch the wind…”*
> — Book III.xxxvi–xl (Yule–Cordier)

---

Marco Polo starts by narrating that in the province of Aden (ʿAdan / Aden) there is a Prince who is called the Soldan. The people are all Saracens and adorers of Mahommet, and they bear a great hatred of Christians. There are many towns and villages in the country. [1]

He says that Aden is the port to which many of the ships of India come with their cargoes; and from this haven the merchants carry the goods a distance of seven days further in small vessels. At the end of those seven days they land the goods and load them on camels, and so carry them a land journey of thirty days. This brings them to the river of Alexandria — the Nile — and by it they descend to the latter city. It is by this way through Aden that the Saracens of Alexandria receive all their stores of pepper and other spicery; and there is no other route equally good and convenient by which these goods could reach that place. [2]

— A quick note on the stages: the overall corridor from Aden toward the Nile and Alexandria is firm; the exact seven-day and thirty-day legs, and which Red Sea landing (Suakin, ʿAidhāb, Kosseir) is meant, remain debated. Ramusio expands the Red Sea gulf and names *zerms* and the *Calizene* canal; Polo himself never says “Cairo.” The “Soldan of Aden” is the Rasulid Sultan of Yemen, whose chief seat was Taʿizz; Aden was the great port. [3] Anyway, next he mentions the Soldan’s revenues.

According to the book, the Soldan of Aden receives a large amount in duties from the ships that traffic between India and his country, and from the exports also; for there are despatched from the port of Aden to India a very large number of Arab chargers, and palfreys, and stout nags adapted for all work — a source of great profit. Horses fetch very high prices in India, there being none bred there, as the book has told before; insomuch that a charger will sell there for a hundred marks of silver and more. On these also the Soldan receives heavy payments in port charges, so that ’tis said he is one of the richest princes in the world. [4]

And it is a fact — so Polo narrates with living bitterness — that when the Soldan of Babylon (the Mamluk Sultan of Egypt) went against the city of Acre (Acre / ʿAkkā) and took it, this Soldan of Aden sent to his assistance thirty thousand horsemen and full forty thousand camels, to the great help of the Saracens and the grievous injury of the Christians. He did this a great deal more for the hate he bears the Christians than for any love he bears the Soldan of Babylon; for these two do hate one another heartily. [5]

— Acre fell in 1291; that date is an external chronological anchor from outside the Asia years, spoken as living memory at dictation. Arabia was summoned to the Mamluk war; the contingent numbers look inflated. Venetian bitterness at Acre’s fall is real enough without needing every camel counted. [6] Anyway, next he mentions a subject city on the incense shore.

Esher (Shihr / ash-Shiḥr), he says, is a great city lying in a north-westerly direction from Aden, some four hundred miles distant. It has a king who is subject to the Soldan of Aden; he administers his territory well and justly. The people are Saracens. The place has a very good haven, wherefore many ships from India come thither with various cargoes; and they export many good chargers thence to India. [7]

A great deal of white incense grows in this country, and brings in a great revenue to the Prince; for no one dares sell it to any one else. Whilst he takes it from the people at ten livres of gold for the hundredweight, he sells it to the merchants at sixty livres, so his profit is immense. Dates also grow very abundantly. The people have no corn but rice, and very little of that; but plenty is brought from abroad. They have fish in great profusion, notably tunny of large size — so plentiful that you may buy two big ones for a Venice groat of silver. They live on meat and rice and fish; they have no wine of the vine, but make good wine from sugar, from rice, and from dates. [8]

And he must tell another very strange thing. Their sheep have no ears, but where the ear ought to be they have a little horn — pretty little beasts. And all their cattle, including horses, oxen, and camels, live upon small fish and nought besides, for ’tis all they get to eat. In all this country there is no grass or forage of any kind; it is the driest country on the face of the earth. The fish given to the cattle are very small, and during March, April, and May are caught in such quantities as would astonish you. They are then dried and stored, and the beasts are fed on them from year’s end to year’s end. The cattle will also readily eat these fish all alive and just out of the water. The people likewise have many other kinds of large fish, exceedingly cheap; these they cut in pieces of about a pound each, dry in the sun, and store and eat all the year through, like so much biscuit. [9]

— The frankincense monopoly and the fish-fed cattle are among the stronger ethnographic hits of the Arabian chapters. Sheḥr–Dhafār hills are the true ancient source of white *lubān*, with Somali *peratic* supply beside; Ichthyophagi report runs continuous from Mekran to the Gulf in Edrisi, Sidi ʿAli, and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa at Dhafār. The “earless” sheep are likely a small-eared or multi-horn breed misread. [10] Soft rise now: five hundred miles further along the same coast, to Dufar, where the tree itself is shown.

Dufar (Dhafār / Dhofar), according to the book, is a great and noble and fine city, some five hundred miles to the north-west of Esher. The people are Saracens and have a Count for their chief, subject to the Soldan of Aden; for this city still belongs to the Province of Aden. It stands upon the sea and has a very good haven, so that there is a great traffic of shipping between this and India; and the merchants take hence great numbers of Arab horses to that market. [11]

Much white incense is produced here, and he will tell how it grows. The trees are like small fir-trees; these are notched with a knife in several places, and from these notches the incense is exuded. Sometimes also it flows from the tree without any notch, by reason of the great heat of the sun. [12]

— The trees “like small firs” are simile only; actual *Boswellia* is not fir. Classical λιβανωτοφόρος country; Marāṣid knew the mountains of Dhafār and Merbāṭ as port, with death for diversion of incense. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa found horse-trade, dirt, and flies, and a sultan kin to Yemen. [13] Anyway, next he mentions Calatu, six hundred miles further, at the Gulf’s mouth.

Calatu (Qalhāt / Kalhāt), Polo narrates, is a great city within a gulf which bears the name of the Gulf of Calatu. It lies some six hundred miles from Dufar towards the north-west, upon the sea-shore. The people are Saracens and are subject to Hormos (Hormuz / Old Hormuz). Whenever the Melic of Hormos is at war with some prince more potent than himself, he betakes himself to this city of Calatu, because it is very strong, both from its position and its fortifications. [14]

They grow no corn here, but get it from abroad; for every merchant-vessel that comes brings some. The haven is very large and good, and is frequented by numerous ships with goods from India, and from this city the spices and other merchandize are distributed among the cities and towns of the interior. They also export many good Arab horses from this to India — for, as the book has told before, the number of horses exported from this and the other cities to India yearly is something astonishing. One reason is that no horses are bred there, and another that they die as soon as they get there, through ignorant handling: the people feed their horses with cooked victuals and all sorts of trash, and they have no farriers. [15]

This City of Calatu stands at the mouth of the Gulf, so that no ship can enter or go forth without the will of the chief. And when the Melic of Hormos, who is Melic of Calatu also, and is vassal to the Soldan of Kerman (Kermān), fears anything at the hand of the latter, he gets on board his ships and comes from Hormos to Calatu. And then he prevents any ship from entering the Gulf. This causes great injury to the Soldan of Kerman; for he thus loses all the duties that he is wont to receive from merchants frequenting his territories from India or elsewhere. In this way he is constrained to give way to the demands of the Melic of Hormos. This Melic has also a castle which is still stronger than the city, and has a better command of the entry to the Gulf — Yule thought likely Muscat, though the book does not name it. The people live on dates and salt fish, which they have in great abundance; the nobles, however, have better fare. [16]

Then he brings the reader a second time to Hormos. When you leave the City of Calatu and go for some three hundred miles between north-west and north, you come to the city of Hormos — a great and noble city on the sea. It has a Melic, which is as much as to say a King, and he is under the Soldan of Kerman. There are a good many cities and towns belonging to Hormos, and the people are Saracens. The heat is tremendous, and on that account their houses are built with ventilators to catch the wind. These ventilators are placed on the side from which the wind comes, and they bring the wind down into the house to cool it. But for this the heat would be utterly unbearable. [17]

— The *bād-gīr* wind-catcher is a neat ethnographic hit: Oman, Kerman, Baghdad, Mekran, Sind, and a Cairo analogue all know the architecture. Della Valle, Figueroa, and Fryer later travellers confirm the same throat of masonry catching sea-wind. [18]

I shall say no more about these places, the book explains, because it formerly told in regular order all about this same city of Hormos, and about Kerman as well — of the stitched ships and the dates-and-fish diet, of the simoom and the turn inland. But as we took one way to go, and another to come back, it was proper that we should bring you a second time to this point. [19]

Now, however, we will quit this part of the world. First, however, there is a point omitted: when you leave the City of Calatu and go between west and north-west, a distance of some five hundred miles, you come to the city of Kis (Kish / Qais). Of that, however, we shall say no more now. [20]

— One last aside on bearings: Aden to Esher four hundred miles, Esher to Dufar five hundred, Dufar to Calatu six hundred, Calatu to Hormos three hundred — the north-west cascade and the mileages do not map cleanly on the real coast. Yule tried to draw Polo’s mental Arabia; only the Hormos bearing improves. The frankincense economy stands; the survey does not. [21]

Thus the Indian Ocean book closes upon itself: Aden’s spice-road to Alexandria; Esher’s monopoly and fish-cattle; Dufar’s notched incense; Calatu’s Gulf lock; Hormos again, with *bād-gīrs* catching the sea-wind into masonry throats; Kis named and set aside. The seas of India and Arabia have been told; the narrative is ready for the princess voyage — the fleet that will carry a bride westward and the Polos home. For this chapter it is enough that Aden, Esher, Dufar, Calatu, and Hormos have been joined in one coastal breath, and that the Book has returned, by the Ocean road, to the Gulf city from which, long before, the travellers turned their faces inland.
