# Chapter II-14
## Fuju, Zayton, and the Haven of the World

> *“And I assure you that for one shipload of pepper that goes to Alexandria or elsewhere, destined for Christendom, there come a hundred such, aye and more too, to this haven of Zayton; for it is one of the two greatest havens in the world for commerce.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 82 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

One day’s ride south out of Kinsay (Hangzhou), the road winds through unbroken gardens and suburban houses where shopkeepers still accept the Great Kaan’s black paper money without hesitation [1]. At dusk it brings a rider into Tanpiju (perhaps Shaoxing), a bustling walled city thriving under Kinsay’s rule. Silk looms clatter inside dim courtyards. Everyday white rice and coarse hemp cloth change hands cheaply beside crowded wharves, and households carry their dead out to hillside funeral pyres [2]. Tanpiju marks the first doorstep into south-eastern Manzi (southern China), where canal flats squeeze tightly between steep forested ridges. Yet the Book paces this entire southern highway like one continuous suburb of spinning looms, bamboo groves, arched stone bridges, and customs booths.

Three days south-east lies Vuju (Jinhua, in Zhejiang—not the northern Vuju we passed before Kinsay), governed under the same capital circuit [3]. Two days further up the winding valley, through market towns strung together like one green street, stands Ghiuju (Quzhou). Bright silk fills its plank-fronted shops, weavers and cabinet-makers crowd its alleys, and towering bamboo groves darken misty slopes outside [4]. Polo measures the thickest green canes at four palms around and fifteen paces tall. That bold number stretches real valley stalks along the roadside, though lashed Zhejiang bamboo rafts floated hundreds of yards long down toward coastal timber yards [5].

Four steep days climb past dense oak woods haunted by fierce striped beasts the Book names as lions. These are really striped woodland tigers prowling wet mountain ravines, until the stony track gains Changshan (the watershed hill, perhaps near Suichang) [6]. Stop a moment on that wet granite saddle and listen to cold water falling below your stirrups. Changshan straddles the mountain divide where springs split toward opposite coasts: one foaming brook hurries north-east back toward Kinsay’s river, while the other drops west toward inland valleys [7]. Between crowded canal lighters and sweating hill porters lifting timber panniers along steep stairways, this breezy crest marks the true geographical hinge of the southern road. Before tipping down the far slope, Polo plants one sweeping household rule across half a continent. All Manzi herds cattle, horned goats, and plump black pigs aplenty beside cottage pens, yet raises not a single wool-bearing sheep [8].

Three days beyond the watershed sits Cuju (perhaps Chuzhou, though its exact town stays disputed), the final prefectural wall paying tax under Kinsay’s eye [9]. Merchants stack linen bales beside stone toll gates while tired pack horses drink at cold brook troughs. Pass under Cuju’s southern gatehouse and the narrow paved track dives for six rugged days down timbered ravines, roaring mountain cataracts, and steep misty passes into the coastal realm of Fuju (Fuzhou) [10].

Stop beside open-fronted spice booths along those wet green valleys and count small silver coins onto a damp plank counter. Woven split-bamboo panniers brim with pale washed ginger roots lifted fresh from damp red earth. A single Venetian groat buys eighty full pounds of crisp fresh ginger. Heaped alongside the ginger sacks sit aromatic galingale roots, dried bark twigs, crisp pepper pods, and wide cedar trays brimming with a bright yellow gardenia fruit that dyes broth and flavours steamed rice like costly saffron [11]. Yet the warm comfort of busy spice baskets cools abruptly whenever the Book glances upward toward shadowed rocky spurs above the road. Hill fighters along those misty ridges shave clean every hair above their brows and paint broad blue bands across their foreheads like the curved steel blade of a glaive. Armed with ash-shafted spears, hide shields, and short swords, they march on foot down steep trails behind one mounted headman [12]. Worse still, Polo charges those highlanders with drinking warm blood and hunting executed felons to roast for dinner [13]. Leave that chilling tableau cleanly where it belongs: a hostile lowlands rumour bred out of fear of unconquered mountain clans, never an eyewitness campfire supper.

Halfway through the six-day descent—at the third march in the earliest accounts—rises Kelinfu (Jianning), a wealthy valley crossroads spanning the swirling upper Min beneath three extraordinary stone bridges [14]. Red-painted cedar roofs shelter walking merchants above tall cut stone piers shining over white river shoals. Polo stretches each bridge to a full mile long and nine paces across, lined down both parapets with carved white marble columns [15]. Busy looms weave raw silk while dyeworks wring bright crimson and green cotton cloth over stone troughs. Workers drape dripping bolts across tall cedar poles before packing dried bales for fairs across Manzi [16]. Beside those ringing dye alleys, Marco singles out a barnyard curiosity sure to stop any walker. Around shaded coops strut jet-black fowls dressed not in feathers, but in soft glossy fur like a tabby cat’s velvet coat. They lay ordinary white eggs and roast up tender and sweet at supper [17].

Three stages more—fifteen miles shy of the provincial port—lies Unken (perhaps Minhou, though fertile cane valleys farther south also press an equal claim) [18]. Heavy granite millstones creak slowly around Unken’s steaming boiling houses, crushing green-and-purple cane stalks to keep the Great Kaan’s royal tables stocked year round—a sticky harvest worth a prince’s ransom [19]. Before Cublay (Kublai) conquered the valley, local boilers knew only how to skim dark cane syrup with wooden ladles into coarse black paste. Then skilled craftsmen arrived from “Babylonia” (meaning Cairo) in Mongol court service and taught them how to leach clear syrup through hardwood ashes into gleaming white sugarloaves [20]. Fifteen miles past those smoky boiling houses rises Fuju itself, seat of the rich kingdom Polo labels Chonka [21].

Fuju serves as both an uneasy coastal garrison and an active trading hub. Heavy Yuan battalions patrol its high stone ramparts day and night because local townsmen stay dangerously quick to spark revolt [22]. A river a full mile wide cuts past the tall gatehouse in Polo’s telling, ringing with shipwrights’ oak mallets and sharp adzes hollowing scented camphor keels while incoming hulls tack upriver laden with Indian pearls, spices, and bright gemstones [23]. Watch how the southern harbour network clears its heavy freight. Deep-bellied ocean junks drop stone anchors down the coast at Zayton (Quanzhou) to heave spice chests, hardwood logs, and sealed pearl bags shoreside. Lighter coastal craft then slip through white breakers and ride the flood tide straight up the Min onto Fuju’s paved quays [24].

Leave the watchful garrison behind, ferry across the wide tidal current, and ride five bright days south-east through garden towns, sweet orange orchards, and dark glossy camphor forests until salt sea wind hits your face at Zayton [25]. Stand beside Marco on the granite seawall and count tall pine masts crowded across the wide bay. Nearly all the pepper fleets of India ride at anchor inside these outer headlands, encircled tightly by nimble river pinnaces, salt lighters, and sharp buyers streaming down from every province of Manzi [26]. For every single spice ship lifting anchor for Alexandria and Latin Christendom, Marco vows with proud certainty, a hundred or more empty their cargo nets upon Zayton’s crowded docks. To his eye, it stands tall as one of the two supreme commercial havens on earth [27].

![Junks and merchants at Zayton (Quanzhou); Polo's hundred-to-one pepper ratio is rhetoric, not a verified trade volume. *(Modern illustration in the manner of a Song–Yuan city-genre handscroll. Not a historical artifact. This edition.)*](../media/plates/II-14-zayton-harbour.png){#fig-ii-14-zayton-harbour}

A merchant’s son cannot gaze over busy timber berths without summing up every toll chalked onto the wharf ledger. Clerks flick smooth wooden counting-frames beside swinging brass steelyards while sweating porters stack fragrant sacks under shaded matting roofs. On every chest and bale carried ashore at Zayton, fine pearls and uncut rubies included, the Great Kaan claims ten per cent [28]. Then come the shipowners’ steep freight charges across southern waters: thirty per cent on light smallwares and folded silk bales, forty-four per cent upon loose black pepper, and forty per cent on bulky goods such as sandalwood and aloes. Customs duty and sea freight combined devour a clean good half of any venturer’s initial outlay [29]. Think about what those numbers mean to a captain standing beside his cargo hatches: half the spice in your hold goes straight to customs and ship hire before a single silver groat lands in your purse. Yet on the clear half left standing inside stone warehouses, merchants turn so handsome a profit that every man cheerfully counts out silver again to dispatch another vessel back across southern bays for fresh bales [30].

![Diagram C7. Duty plus freight at Zayton, by kind of goods, against the Book's "good half". Freight rates are from Ramusio's version. *(Diagram: this edition, from the Book via Yule–Cordier.)*](../media/diagrams/C7-zayton-cost.svg){#fig-c7-zayton-cost}

Step off those pepper wharves into a quieter alley beside the harbour and watch another trade altogether. Visitors from Upper India come to Zayton to have designs worked permanently into their skin with a needle. A traveller walking home with a bale of freight can set it down at night. A visitor who came for needlework carries his purchase home on his own skin for life [31]. Inside Chonka’s green hills, too, stands a kiln town Polo names Tyunju (perhaps Dehua), moulding pale hillside earth into the finest translucent porcelain bowls ever spun. Stacks emerge wrapped in dry rice-straw ropes so cheaply that one Venetian groat buys three delicate dishes [32]. Marco’s boast that fine porcelain is potted nowhere else breaks beside giant inland factories at Jingdezhen. Even so, pale glazed export bowls from southern Fujian genuinely packed wooden tubs sailing past Zayton’s headlands [33].

![Map A13. Zayton and the proposed porcelain locality of Tyunju. Cordier suggested Dehua, and Tingzhou or Changting is another reading. Kiln finds establish production in the region; the name remains disputed. *(Map: this edition, from Cordier and Lin and Zhang.)*](../media/maps/A13-tyunju-inset.svg){#fig-a13-tyunju-inset}

Step into the clay pits behind that kiln town—placed by some tellers along a river branch near Tingui, where a single groat buys eight bowls [34]—and ask a potter where the white earth comes from. He points to weathered mounds stacked in open air for thirty or forty years so rain, wind, and frost cure the clay. A grandfather digs the earth his grandchildren will spin into bowls [35]. Pause beside those damp heaps for a heartbeat. Few trades show a longer horizon than digging clay you will never live to shape. Even common speech fences off this salt-blown coast from northern plains. Where one version simply notes Zayton keeps a tongue all its own, another compares Manzi’s single shared written script to Italy’s splintered dialects across Genoa, Milan, Florence, and Naples [36]. It is a shrewd Renaissance analogy, even though Fujian’s coastal speech sounded far stranger to a northern clerk than Tuscan sounds in Milan.

Polo reckons the silver customs collected across Chonka match or overtake even Kinsay’s bursting treasury [37]. Out of Manzi’s nine wide provinces, he pauses cleanly to log just three—Yanju (Yangzhou), Kinsay, and Fuju—before promising to haul anchor at Zayton and steer across the Ocean Sea toward Chipangu (Japan) and the spiced archipelagos of India [38]. Before we board those ships, remember the vessel waiting below the tide. A merchant hull excavated near Zayton in 1973–74 proved to have twelve timber bulkheads dividing thirteen compartments, pierced with small limber holes along the keel for bilge water to pass [39]. Look down at those stout cedar walls inside the hold: one rock reef can flood a single chamber without dragging the pepper cargo to the bottom.

![Diagram C12. A cutaway of a seagoing junk, with bulkheads and limber holes drawn from the excavated Quanzhou hull. Volume III compares the find with the Book's account of the ships of Manzi. *(Diagram: this edition; the rig and upper works are schematic.)*](../media/diagrams/C12-seagoing-junk.svg){#fig-c12-zayton-preview}

Leave the customs clerk on Zayton’s wharf for one final chapter before we put out to sea. While these southern ports pour silver into Cublay’s chests, far out across the northern steppes his rebel cousin Caidu (Kaidu) is demanding his share of the empire at the point of a spear.

## Notes

[1] ✓ A single day’s ride south-east from Kinsay opens the Fujian corridor with the Book’s standard triad of paper currency, cremation, and busy handicrafts [@yc, 2.79]. See [Map A13, the Fujian road](ch-14-fuju-zayton.md#fig-a13-fujian-road).

[2] ? Tanpiju is plotted at Shaoxing (Shao-hing fu), following Richthofen and Yule’s later view, though Phillips and earlier editors argued for Fuyang across the Qiantang River [@yc, 2.79 n. 1]. Xiaoshan and Tonglu have also been proposed.

[3] ✓ Vuju here is Jinhua (Kin-hwa fu), known in Song and Yuan times as *Wuzhou* (Wu-chau), three stages from Shaoxing along a natural valley corridor [@yc, 2.79 n. 3]. It is entirely distinct from the Vuju (Huzhou) passed north of Kinsay in Chapter II-12.

[4] ✓ Ghiuju corresponds to Quzhou (K’iu-chau), two days south-west up the dense, garden-like Qiantang valley noted by nineteenth-century travellers [@yc, 2.79 n. 3]. Phillips alternatively read Ramusio’s *Gengiu* as Yanzhou (Yenchau).

[5] ~ Giant bamboos measuring four palms in girth and fifteen paces long exaggerate local stalks, though Zhejiang was famous for timber-sized bamboo [@yc, 2.79 n. 2]. Medhurst saw bamboo rafts a third of a mile long floating near Hangzhou.

[6] ✓ The “lions” along the Zhejiang–Fujian border are South China tigers, long attested in these wooded uplands [@yc, 2.80 n. 8]. Tigers remained common enough in Fujian that later travellers reported roads diverted around infested valleys.

[7] ? Changshan marks the watershed divide where travellers crossed from the Qiantang headwaters to west-flowing streams leading toward Lake Poyang [@yc, 2.79 n. 3]. Yule treats it as Changshan, Cordier as Suichang, and Ramusio’s *Zengian* points toward Lanxi, leaving the exact pass unresolved.

[8] ✗ The blanket claim that Manzi held no sheep exaggerates a regional scarcity [@yc, 2.79 n. 1]. Chinese sources cited by Cordier note mutton was less prized than pork in southern markets and often sold by Mongol and Muslim butchers, not that sheep were absent.

[9] ? Cuju is the final city under Kinsay’s jurisdiction, as *Kiang-Ché* province then reached into eastern Jiangxi [@yc, 2.79 n. 3]. Commentators divide between Chuzhou (Lishui), Chuju near Hokow, and Pucheng along the mountain approach into Fujian [@yc, 2.79 n. 3].

[10] ✓ Beyond the pass, the six-day descent enters Fuju (Fuzhou / Fujian) through steep valleys and mountain rivers [@yc, 2.80 n. 7]. Water travel down fast-flowing streams helps explain why Polo’s six-day count appears tight against overland mileage.

[11] ~ Eighty pounds of fresh ginger for a Venetian groat vividly conveys abundance alongside galingale (*Alpinia officinarum*) [@yc, 2.80 nn. 1, 5]. Dr. Flückiger and Bretschneider compare Polo’s saffron-like fruit with *Gardenia* pods (rich in yellow crocine) or safflower (*Carthamus tinctorius*).

[12] ? Shaved, blue-painted foreheads and foot soldiers armed with spears and swords reflect lowland impressions of unassimilated mountain communities in the Wuyi–Bohea borderlands [@yc, 2.80 n. 3]. Martini still found independent highland groups in seventeenth-century Fujian.

[13] ✗ Accusations of eating executed bodies and drinking blood belong to a familiar medieval frontier trope rather than corroborated ethnography [@yc, 2.80 n. 3]. Yule notes similar hearsay pinned onto distant mountain peoples across the Book.

[14] ✓ Kelinfu is Jianning (Kien-ning fu / Jian’ou) on the upper Min River, where local pronunciation often substituted *l* for *n* [@yc, 2.80 n. 4]. Three days from Cuju in the Franco-Italian text becomes six days in Ramusio [@yc, 2.80 n. 7].

[15] ✗ Three bridges each a mile long and adorned with marble columns dramatically inflate the local crossing [@yc, 2.80 n. 4]. Ramusio reduces them to “more than a hundred paces long and eight paces wide,” while Martini saw a single covered stone-pier bridge lined with shops.

[16] ✓ Ramusio-only in part. Ramusio’s 1559 text adds woven silks and dyed cotton cloth exported throughout Manzi [@yc, 2.80 n. 4]. Phillips confirmed that Kelinfu later remained famed for its patterned red-and-green dyed cottons (*Hung Lu Kin*).

[17] ✓ Black-skinned fowls with hair-like plumage rather than flat feathers are Silkie chickens (*Gallus lanatus*), also noted at Fuzhou by Friar Odoric [@yc, 2.80 n. 6; @odoric1913travels, ch. 31]. Odoric and later observers found white-plumaged silkies common in Fujian, while Polo notes black ones.

[18] ? Unken sits fifteen miles from Fuju in the Franco-Italian text, pointing toward Minhou (*Houguan* / *Minqing*) [@yc, 2.80 n. 8]. Phillips argued instead for Yongchun (*Ung-chun*), a major southern Fujian sugar district along the road to Quanzhou.

[19] ✓ Unken’s cane sugar supplied the Yuan court [@yc, 2.80 n. 8]. Bretschneider notes that sugarcane had already been cultivated in southern China since the second century BC, long before the refining techniques of the Mongol period [@yc, 2.80 n. 8].

[20] ~ Ramusio-only. The story that men of “Babylonia” (meaning Cairo, whose *Misri* sugar was prized across Asia) taught Fujian boilers to refine white sugar with tree ash appears only in Ramusio [@yc, 2.80 n. 8]. Yule, citing a note from C. Phillips (and dropping a derivative Turkish Geography notice from Hajji Khalfa), reports Fujian local histories crediting men “from the West” in Mongol times with teaching fine-sugar making; those local histories have not been inspected directly [@yc, 2.80 n. 8].

[21] ✓ Fuju is Fuzhou (Fu-chau), visited by Friar Odoric as *Fuzo* [@yc, 2.81 n. 1; @odoric1913travels, ch. 31]. Polo’s regional name *Chonka* remains unexplained despite proposals linking it to *Quanzhou*, *Jian’guo*, or *Zhongguo* [@yc, 2.81 n. 1].

[22] ✓ A large Yuan garrison fits Fujian’s turbulent early decades after Song loyalist resistance collapsed [@yc, 2.81 n. 1]. Rashīd al-Dīn notes that the provincial *Sing* shifted back and forth between Fuzhou and Quanzhou during 1281–1283.

[23] ~ The Min River is a mile wide near its mouth, though its main channel flows south of the walled city and only creeks entered the suburbs, rather than bisecting the city as Polo phrases it [@yc, 2.81 n. 3]. Shipbuilding and river transport between the coast and Fuzhou are well attested.

[24] ✓ Indian Ocean wares arrived at Zayton and moved north up the coastal estuaries and Min River to Fuzhou [@yc, 2.81 n. 2]. See [Chapter III-1](../vol-3/ch-01-ships-chipangu.md) for the hull design of ocean-going Chinese junks.

[25] ✓ Camphor trees (*Cinnamomum camphora*) flourished across Fujian, feeding a substantial trade in sublimated camphor [@yc, 2.82 n. 1]. Five days south-east through busy settlements matches the coastal road from Fuzhou to Quanzhou.

[26] ✓ Zayton (Arabic *Zaitūn*) is firmly identified with Quanzhou (T’swan-chau fu) through Yuan maritime-trade ordinances (*shibo si*, established 1277), Islamic tombstones, and harbour archaeology [@yc, 2.82 n. 2; @schottenhammer2001emporium; @chen1984quanzhou]. Andrew of Perugia (1326) and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa likewise describe its global sea trade.

[27] ~ Calling Zayton one of the world’s two greatest ports and claiming a hundred pepper ships arrive for every one sent to Alexandria is merchant hyperbole capturing real Asian volume [@yc, 2.82 nn. 2–3]. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa went further and called Zaitūn the single greatest harbour in the world.

[28] ✓ A ten per cent imperial customs tithe accords closely with Yuan *shibo* maritime tariff practice, though official rates varied by commodity [@yc, 2.82; @schottenhammer2001emporium]. See [Diagram C7, Zayton cost stack](ch-14-fuju-zayton.md#fig-c7-zayton-cost).

[29] ~ Yule prints the three rates and the estimate of a good half in the main account. The Book’s calculation measures how much of a merchant’s investment goes on freight and duties [@yc, 2.82].

[30] ~ Ramusio-only in Yule. The assurance that merchants could still make a good profit and return with more merchandise appears in the bracketed addition [@yc, 2.82].

[31] ? Ramusio-only. The description of Zayton as an easygoing, quiet town patronised by visitors from Upper India seeking needle tattoos comes solely from Ramusio [@yc, 2.82 n. 4]. Yule compares tattooing habits among Southeast Asian, Arab, and Japanese sailors frequenting the port.

[32] ? Tyunju is plausibly identified with Dehua (*Tek-hwa*) in the Fujian interior behind Quanzhou, though Lin and Zhang [-@lin2017chinese] show Tingzhou/Changting and other kiln centres remain open candidates [@yc, 2.82 n. 5]. Cheap export bowls—three for a Venetian groat—fit Dehua’s massive export volume.

[33] ✗ Polo’s claim that porcelain was made nowhere else in the world is false [@yc, 2.82 n. 5]. Fujian produced enormous quantities of *qingbai* and white export wares, yet Jingdezhen in Jiangxi already dwarfed it as China’s premier porcelain centre.

[34] ? Ramusio-only in Yule. As Yule quotes him, the river location is at a branch and the town reads Tingui. This version prices the bowls at eight to the groat [@yc, 2.82 n. 5].

[35] ~ Ramusio-only in Yule. The clay aged for thirty or forty years and the explanation of its being dug for later generations are in his version [@yc, 2.82 n. 5].

[36] ✓ Ramusio-only in part. The Franco-Italian text notes only that Zayton keeps a language of its own, whereas Ramusio widens the remark to China’s shared written script and regional dialects compared with Genoa, Milan, Florence, and Naples [@yc, 2.82 n. 6]. In fact, Fujian’s spoken Min dialects differed far more sharply from northern speech than Italian dialects from each other.

[37] ~ Claiming Chonka’s customs equalled or surpassed Kinsay’s reflects Zayton’s maritime prominence while rounding off Book II’s fiscal panorama [@yc, 2.82]. Even if the comparison exaggerates a single province's receipts, the harbour taxes on pepper, spices, and pearls made Quanzhou one of the Yuan treasury's richest ports.

[38] ✓ Naming three of Manzi’s nine divisions and turning toward Chipangu and India prepares the maritime crossing of Volume III [@yc, 2.82]. Before setting sail, Chapter II-15 detours to Caidu’s Central Asian steppe wars as planned for this volume’s northern horizon.

[39] ✓ The wreck was discovered in 1973 and excavated in 1974. Pearson, Li and Li describe the bulkheads and compartments, and Green and colleagues report the limber holes. The coins give a date after 1271 for the sinking [@pearson2002quanzhou; @green1998quanzhou].
