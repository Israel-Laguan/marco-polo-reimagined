# Chapter II-7
## Tebet, Caindu, Carajan, Zardandan

> *“After those five days’ march that I spoke of, you enter a province which has been sorely ravaged; and this was done in the wars of Mongu Kaan.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 45 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

Leave the covered silk booths and toll-clerks on the bridge at Sindafu (Chengdu) behind. Five days’ ride into the hills, a merchant has far more use for a good length of bamboo than for anything sold on that painted timber deck. At the threshold of Tebet (eastern Tibet), the Book drops us among villages shattered in the wars of Mongou (Möngke) Kaan [1]. Steep ridges close round the narrow track. Inhabited settlements vanish, and bears alongside the striped beasts Polo calls lions have had years to reclaim the ruined valleys.

At sundown the muleteers hack down green canes and heap them onto the campfire. Once heat reaches the hollow joints, trapped air and sap blow the bamboo apart with cracks loud enough to send prowling beasts running. Ten miles off through the dark hills you could still hear the volley, Polo reckons [2]. Take that ten-mile echo as a measure of his pride in campcraft. Just here on the trail, though, there is a sharper question to settle before dark. How do you keep your own horses from bolting into the woods?

Cotton stuffed in the riders’ ears settles the men. Cloth hoods go over the horses’ eyes, and thick ropes hobble all four hooves at the pegs [3]. The Book warns of careless travellers whose mounts snapped their halters at the first bang. After a few nights of cannon-fire, the beasts learn to doze through the din. Meanwhile every rider is betting his whole pack-train on wooden tent-pegs driven into wet ground. Twenty foodless marches stretch ahead before the road meets another village market [4].

Stay with the hobbled horses by that fire for a moment, dear reader. Out in the scrub, a bear hears a sudden crack that sends it crashing uphill. Right here inside camp, you have unleashed the exact same explosion six feet from the animals you need to carry your rice tomorrow morning. Picture the mules plunging against their heel-ropes in the dark, eyes rolling under blindfolds, while drivers shout over snapping cane. Keep your pegs driven deep—because out here, losing your beasts means walking twenty marches on foot.

At the first inhabited stretch beyond the forest, a traveller’s ring buys a custom nobody back at Sindafu would have predicted. Older women bring unmarried daughters to wayside camps, expecting departing guests to leave a brass ring or token behind. When a girl has collected at least twenty such tokens, she shows them as proof of desirability and marries. After the wedding, the Book expects strict fidelity [5]. It reads like a story told for young men on the road, far easier to place around a tavern bench than in a mountain village.

For wares a merchant can weigh on a balance, turn to Tebet’s musk. Trained dogs hunt the small deer the Book names *Gudderi*. At market, salt serves where the Kaan’s paper notes pass elsewhere [6]. Polo credits Tebet with eight kingdoms, rivers yielding gold dust, fine woollens, and cinnamon. Coral fetches a handsome price from buyers who hang it around women’s necks and sacred images [7]. A merchant riding west from the plains who packed coral beads in his saddlebag finds eager buyers long before he sees another silver mint. Even a handful of red Mediterranean coral buys mountain provisions faster than coin.

Mastiffs the size of donkeys prowl through the tale, bold enough to hunt a fierce wild ox called *Beyamini* alongside mountain sakers and lanner falcons [8]. Astrologers and enchanters work feats Polo refuses to describe on the grounds that no good can come of telling them. At the same time, he asserts that Tebet and every province ahead obey the Great Kaan [9]. A distant court that claims authority over hills like these needs something valuable inside them to justify sending tax collectors up the pass.

---

Down in Caindu (Xichang and its valley), begin beside the clear lake. Its pearls run white and uneven. Diving for them without imperial leave carries the penalty of death [10], just as the Kaan guards the region’s turquoise mines under strict monopoly [11]. Both decrees show a ruler protecting luxury prices by shutting everyone else out of the water and the rock.

Hang a hat on a doorway in Caindu, and you read an unexpected rule of hospitality. A host offers a passing traveller the company of the women in his household, then walks out to the fields. He stays away so long as the visitor’s hat hangs over the lintel, expecting divine favour and good harvests in return [12]. Earlier along the caravan road at Camul (Hami), we met a similar tale of hospitable husbands. Here it is the hat hanging above the door that sticks in memory.

Small change in Caindu goes straight into the soup pot. Watch brine poured into moulds until half-pound salt cakes emerge, flat beneath and rounded on top. The prince’s officers stamp each loaf and value eighty cakes to a *saggio* of fine gold [13], [14]. Out in the lonely mountain tribes where gold and musk abound without city markets, that same *saggio* of gold buys only sixty, fifty, or forty salt cakes [15]. Pause and watch a mountain customer chip a corner off his currency to season a boiling pot of broth. Down in town, whole stamped cakes stay intact like silver coins. Up in the hills, you can literally eat your small change when dinner calls for salt. Every cake carries the prince’s stamp so nobody trims half a loaf in town and passes it off at full weight, yet out beyond the market gates the pot matters more than the seal.

At table, Caindu serves lake fish alongside wine brewed from wheat, rice, and spices. Above the road stand trees with narrow laurel-like leaves and pale blossoms that Polo calls cloves—more likely cassia [16]. Even a sharp merchant who knows every spice price on the Rialto has to grope for a word when he sees unfamiliar bark and blossoms growing on a mountain tree.

Ten days’ riding past scattered villages brings the road to the Brius (Jinsha River crossing). Gold dust glitters in the sand, and cinnamon spices the riverbanks [17]. Across that water stretches Carajan (Yunnan), divided into seven kingdoms under Sentemur (Esen-Temür). Introduced here as Cublay (Kublai)’s son, Sentemur appears later—more accurately, at Vochan (Baoshan)—as his grandson [18]. Watching those princes shift across the pages helps date the reports to the late 1270s or early 1280s. The information reads like knowledge gathered on an imperial errand or picked up from officials serving in the south-west.

---

Five days past the crossing, farms give way to the bustling markets of Yachi (Kunming). The city holds Muslims, a handful of Church of the East Christians, and local idolaters [19]. Wheat stands in the stalls, yet locals prefer boiled rice, clear rice drink, and a butcher’s dish of raw minced liver seasoned with garlic and spices [20].

Salt wells feed boiling pans and fill the governor’s purse [21]. In the market square, eighty white cowrie shells count out one weight of silver, worth two Venetian groats, and eight weights of silver buy one of gold [22]. A European listener who has never seen a cowrie shell might recognize the shiny white shell sewn onto a hound’s collar. Marco gets him from a dog-collar ornament to a working exchange rate in a single breath.

Ten days farther west stands Talifu (Dali), a second capital sharing Carajan’s name and governed by Cogachin (Hügechi), a true son of the Kaan [23]. Gold washes down in river grains and comes in heavier nuggets from the hills. Here six weights of silver buy one of gold, and the cowries arrive up trade paths from India [24]. Watch those bullion scales tilt as we ride west: eight silver for one gold at Yachi, six for one at Talifu—and an even sharper bargain waiting five days ahead.

Before you spur onward, look at a heavy track dragging out of the river mud. Polo sets a huge two-legged serpent here with eyes wider than bread loaves and a tail leaving a furrow like a dragged wine-cask. A native crocodilian is the usual reading, grown considerably in the telling [25]. Hunters bury iron-spiked logs along its nightly slide to the water, wait till circling crows call over the carcass, and slice out the gall [26]. Buyers pay a prince’s ransom for that gall as a sovereign cure for everything from difficult childbirth to rabid dog-bites [27].

Spare a glance too for the stout hill ponies bound for India, docked in the tail and ridden long in the stirrups like Frenchmen. Warriors wear boiled leather and carry spears, shields, and poisoned crossbow bolts [28]. Bold captives keep a phial of that poison concealed to swallow before torture, while captors race to force emetics down their throats [29]. Darker still, the Book claims old-time hosts once murdered noble guests in their beds so the dead man’s wisdom and good fortune might stay inside the house, until Mongol rule stamped the practice out [30].

---

Five days farther on lies Zardandan (western Yunnan), the country of the Gold-Teeth. At Vochan, men fit thin gold cases over both upper and lower rows of teeth [31] and prick dark bands into their arms and legs with five needles bound together [32]. Men ride to war and hunt while their wives and enslaved war-captives manage all trade and household work [33].

Step inside a house where a child has just been born, and every European expectation flips upside down. Once the baby is washed and swaddled, the mother rises from bed to run the household and wait upon guests. Meanwhile the father climbs under the covers for forty days, cradling the newborn and receiving neighbours who come to congratulate him on his share of the labour [34]. Picture bringing a birth gift to the front door and finding the father propped on pillows receiving visitors while his wife pours the rice wine.

Business deals need no parchment clerks. Two traders take a wooden stick, cut matching notches across the grain, and split it cleanly down the middle. Each party keeps one half until repayment reunites the two halves of the tally [35]. Without churches or temples, a house facing illness sends for spirit-healers instead. They beat drums and dance until one collapses possessed by the offending spirit, demanding black-headed sheep, spiced liquor, lights, and fragrant wood. Once the spirit relents, even the fallen dancer rises to join the feast [36].

Check the bullion scales one last time in Zardandan: five weights of silver buy one full weight of gold [37]. Pack enough silver mules from Yachi to Vochan, and the profit on the gold exchange alone pays for the entire mountain road. Watch how the trade works from stop to stop: silver leaves the lowlands heavy in saddlebags, climbs through salt-cake hills and cowrie markets, and turns into compact bars of yellow gold that slip easily into a belt. Yet the Kaan’s regiments are not massing on the plain of Vochan solely to weigh bullion. Out through the western timber, an approaching king is marching onto the field—and his soldiers have strapped wooden battle-towers onto the backs of their elephants.

## Notes

[1] ~ Yule locates the approach on the routes west and south-west from Sindafu. Polo’s Tebet here is the eastern frontier, around Kham, and the damage he attributes to Mongou’s wars belongs to the wider Mongol campaigns in the region [@yc, 2.45 n. 1].

[2] ~ Yule quotes Hooker on the noise of heated bamboo bursting and on its use to keep animals away from a camp. The sizes of the canes and the distance at which Polo says they can be heard are more doubtful [@hooker1854himalayan; @yc, 2.45 n. 2].

[3] ~ The precautions with horses and cotton in the ears come from the Book. They make the difference between a camp alarm and a device the travellers can use on successive nights [@yc, 2.45].

[4] ~ Twenty days without provisions for sale is Polo’s reckoning. The comparable journey reported by Gabriel Durand in Yule’s notes took a different number of days [@yc, 2.45 n. 3].

[5] ? Yule compares reports about marriage and gifts in several places, while Polo’s twenty tokens remain a local allegation. Bolognari compares Jacopo d’Acqui’s account with the F, Z and VA versions of Polo and treats the parallels as evidence of related texts [@yc, 2.45 n. 4; @bolognari2024chronica, pp. 55–57].

[6] ~ The Book calls the musk animals by a Mongol name and places them alongside salt currency in its account of Tebet. Vogel surveys the circulation of salt money as part of his assessment of Polo’s account [@yc, 2.45 n. 5; @vogel2013marco, ch. 4].

[7] ✓ Yule describes the value attached to coral in Tebet and cites other travellers on its use as an ornament. The Book’s eight kingdoms and its list of commodities describe a wider region than the opening road [@yc, 2.46 nn. 1–3].

[8] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Yule brackets the particular wild ox and the addition of sakers, while the mastiffs and lanner falcons are already in the base account [@yc, 2.46 n. 5].

[9] ~ The claim of power over all the realms to come belongs to Polo’s presentation of the Kaan. The same chapter declares a refusal to elaborate on the enchanters’ feats, which it attributes to diabolic arts [@yc, 2.46 n. 4].

[10] ? Ramusio-only in part. The lake and restriction form the base account; Yule brackets the colour and shape of the pearls as an addition from Ramusio. Polo presents the restriction as a way to protect the value of pearls [@yc, 2.47].

[11] ~ Yule cites Chinese reports of a deposit of turquoise near the chief town of the district. The Book’s restriction requires imperial permission to extract it [@yc, 2.47 n. 2].

[12] ? Yule compares the Caindu story to the episode at Camul and cites Garnier and others for local analogues. Polo’s account gives the men’s reasons and makes a general claim about a province [@yc, 1.41; @yc, 2.47 n. 3].

[13] ~ Polo distinguishes gold reckoned by weight from the moulded salt used for small payments. The half-pound cakes and the rate of eighty to a *saggio* are in the base account [@yc, 2.47 nn. 4–5].

[14] ~ Ramusio-only. The shape and the ruler’s stamp are features of Ramusio’s version, along with the restriction of manufacture to royal officers [@yc, 2.47 n. 4].

[15] ~ Ramusio-only. The sliding exchange rate in the hills and the use of chipped salt for food are in Ramusio’s account. Vogel judges the close detail of the weights, rates and stamping highly likely to rest on observation. The practice itself leaves the traveller’s route open [@yc, 2.47 nn. 4–5; @vogel2013marco, ch. 4].

[16] ~ Yule’s notes discuss the local spiced wine and compare the tree in the Book with cassia. “Clove” is Polo’s identification [@yc, 2.47 nn. 6–7; @yc, 2.46 n. 3].

[17] ✓ The Brius is generally identified with the Jinsha. The account of washing gold and finding cinnamon along the river gives the boundary a commercial character [@yc, 2.47; @yc, 2.48 n. 1]. See [Map A11, the southwest circuit](#map-of-the-southwest-circuit).

[18] ✓ Sentemur was Cublay’s grandson through Hügechi, as the Book’s account of the frontier campaign has it. The rulers’ assignments help place the account broadly around the period of Sentemur’s appointment, while the exact date and extent of a personal journey remain uncertain [@yc, 2.48 n. 1; @yc, 2.51].

[19] ~ The Book’s list gives Yachi more than one religious community. Yule discusses later evidence for Christians in the region, while Polo’s description leaves the size of the earlier community unclear [@yc, 2.48 nn. 1–2].

[20] ~ Yule compares the use of wheat and rice across the region. The Book describes rice drink and the sale of seasoned raw meat, including liver for customers of modest means [@yc, 2.48 nn. 3, 5–6].

[21] ✓ The salt wells and the ruler’s revenue belong to a wider system of salt production and taxation. The lake in the account is generally identified as Dianchi [@yc, 2.48; @vogel2013marco, ch. 4].

[22] ~ The cowries are the “porcelain” shells of the Book. The conversion to Venetian groats and the ratio of eight weights of silver to one of gold are the rates it gives for Yachi [@yc, 2.48 n. 4]. See [Diagram C9, gold and silver exchange rates](ch-08-mien-bangala.md#fig-silver-exchange).

[23] ✓ Yule identifies the second capital as Talifu, the old seat of kings in Carajan, and its Mongol prince as Hügechi. The conquest of the kingdom dates to the 1250s [@yc, 2.48 n. 1; @yc, 2.49 n. 1].

[24] ~ The six-to-one exchange and the Indian origin of the cowries are what the Book reports at Talifu. Gill, cited by Yule, reports the later use of cowries in the region [@yc, 2.49 n. 2].

[25] ~ The beast is usually identified as a crocodilian, with the species open. No record from Carajan appears in the twenty dated records tabulated by Iijima and colleagues from Wen and colleagues’ 1980 study, whose primary Chinese distribution table was not inspected directly [@yc, 2.49 n. 3; @iijima2022hanyusuchus, supplementary table S1; @wen1980malai]. The great eyes, two feet and daytime burrow are features of Polo’s telling.

[26] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Yule brackets the crows alerting the hunters; the account of the blades already appears in the base text [@yc, 2.49].

[27] ~ The price Polo assigns the gall depends on the cures credited to it in the account. Yule records comparisons for the medicinal use of gall and for eating the animal’s flesh [@yc, 2.49 n. 3].

[28] ~ Yule discusses local ponies, tail treatment and crossbows in comparison with the equipment in the Book. The French comparison describes a longer seat in the saddle [@yc, 2.49 n. 4].

[29] ? Ramusio-only. The story about prisoners swallowing poison and their captors trying to make them vomit is bracketed in Yule. The base account already has poisoned bolts [@yc, 2.49].

[30] ? Yule finds tales with similar elements but no precise parallel for lodging and killing a guest to acquire his qualities. Polo’s thirty-five years since the conquest are approximate [@yc, 2.49 n. 5].

[31] ~ The Persian name *Zardandan* means “Gold-Teeth” and corresponds to Chinese *Jinchi*. Explanations of the name range from fitted gold to the colour produced by chewing betel, and the ethnonym covers a wider region than the town of Vochan [@yc, 2.50 nn. 1–2].

[32] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule brackets the tattooed bands and the five needles used to apply them. He compares descriptions of related practices in the region [@yc, 2.50 n. 2].

[33] ~ The Book divides the work this way, and Yule quotes Garnier’s report of a similarly sharp division. The parallel gives a point for comparison and leaves the scope of Polo’s description open [@yc, 2.50 n. 3].

[34] ~ The arrangement is called couvade. Yule gives examples from several regions, while the forty days in bed and the family celebration belong to Polo’s telling [@yc, 2.50 n. 4].

[35] ~ Dividing a notched tally gives each person a record to match against the other’s. The Book’s broad claim about the absence of writing goes further than the existence of a stick for recording a debt [@yc, 2.50 nn. 6–7].

[36] ~ Yule cites descriptions of spirit-medium healing for comparison with the music, possession and offerings Polo describes. The account puts the family’s expenses and preparations on the page as clearly as the performance itself [@yc, 2.50 nn. 8–12].

[37] ~ The Book gives the same five-to-one rate here and at the market on the descent. The claim about a distant supply of silver is Polo’s explanation for the opportunity he sees in the exchange [@yc, 2.50 n. 5; @yc, 2.53]. See [Diagram C9, gold and silver exchange rates](ch-08-mien-bangala.md#fig-silver-exchange).
