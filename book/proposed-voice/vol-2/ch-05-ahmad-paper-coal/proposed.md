# Chapter II-5
## Ahmad the Bailo, Paper Money, Posts, Coal, and Charity

> *“Now when all this happened Messer Marco was upon the spot.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 23 (Ramusio; Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**

---

A midnight messenger has just knocked in the Old City, summoning finance minister Achmath (Ahmad Fanakati) to a palace where the crown prince is still miles away. Achmath steps out into the dark streets. At the city gate stands Cogatai, captain of the garrison, asking sharply where the minister is riding at such an hour. Cogatai has heard no word of any princely return, so he falls in behind the minister with a party of armed men [1].

While their horses clatter toward the inner gate, we have time to weigh the man riding into the trap. Achmath rose from the household of Empress Chabi to become chief financial hand to Cublay (Kublai) Kaan [2]. The Book styles him *bailo*, giving a Venetian audience the picture of a viceroy with full power to act for his sovereign [3]. He collects revenues and hands out provincial offices, and in the story of his fall those offices are openly for sale—or traded for the daughters of ambitious petitioners who have little choice in the bargain [4]. The Kaan trusts him completely, and the people who might complain have to live under his clerks.

Waiting inside the palace are two conspirators, Chenchu and Vanchu, commanders of a thousand and ten thousand [5]. They have picked their night well: Cublay is far north at Chandu (Shangdu, in Duolun County), and his crown prince Chinkin (Chingkim) is away from the capital. In their plan, killing the minister inside the palace will ignite a wider rising against the Kaan’s foreign officials, with signal fires carrying the news to other cities [6]. First they have to get Achmath under the lights.

Inside the great hall, lamps blaze round a figure seated high on the dais, dressed to look like the returned prince. Achmath enters, spots Vanchu standing ready, and drops to his knees in greeting. Chenchu swings a sword and takes off his head on the spot. From the doorway, Cogatai looses an arrow that cuts Vanchu down, seizes Chenchu, and stops the signal fires before a single beacon can flare across the plain [7]. Chinese histories tell the same midnight ruse of a false princely summons, though there the assassin Wang Zhu swings a heavy copper mace instead of a sword [8]. The chronicles agree closely on the trap, the exhumation of Achmath’s corpse to be thrown to the dogs, and the confiscation of his Old City treasure [9]—while the Book adds punishments for his sons and tight restrictions on Muslim marriages and animal slaughter [10]. A court turns swiftly from judging one minister to policing an entire community.

Before leaving that bloodstained floor, look closely at one famous claim hanging over the scene. Marco puts himself right inside Cambaluc (Beijing) when the conspiracy breaks [11]. For generations readers pointed to a Chinese record of an imperial assessor named *Po-lo* questioned in the aftermath as proof of Polo’s presence. That official, however, was the Mongol minister Bolad, who held high honours at court years before the Venetians ever rode in from the west [12]. The murder plot is real history; the Venetian’s eyewitness signature on the doorpost remains his own claim.

---

Step out of the bloody hall into daylight and watch how the same city pays out a fortune without unlocking a single chest of gold. At the Kaan’s mint, clerks strip the pale inner bast from between the wood and rough bark of the mulberry tree—the very tree whose leaves feed silkworms [13]—and beat it into sheets of dark, stiff paper cut to graded sizes. Worth ranges from half a silver groat to ten gold bezants depending on the mark pressed across the sheet.

Pull up a stool beside the head clerk’s table. Lesser officers have already signed and stamped each blank sheet in turn. Now the chief master dips the Great Kaan’s seal in bright vermilion ink and presses it squarely onto the dark fibre [14]. That crimson square is all the backing a merchant gets. It turns a strip of tree bark into legal gold, and counterfeiting it carries the penalty of death.

Refusing a stamped sheet in any market across the realm is likewise treason, yet Marco does not dwell solely on the threat. A merchant’s eye lights up at the sheer convenience of paper alchemy: you can tuck the value of ten gold coins into your sleeve without feeling the weight of one [15]. Foreign caravans bringing pearls, gems, or bullion sell straight to the Kaan’s twelve valuers, collect their price in crisp paper, and buy return cargoes of silk and spice the very same afternoon [16]. Need real metal for a silver belt or a gilt goblet? Walk back to the mint and buy bullion with those same notes. And if rain or rough pockets fray a sheet, the mint exchanges it for a fresh note at a steady fee of three per cent [17]—a detail only a trader who has carried damp paper through summer downpours thinks to tally.

Old notes even circulated alongside newer issues. In 1287, the court set one *guan* of the new Zhiyuan paper equal to five *guan* of the older Zhongtong issue [18]. Marco is at his most dazzled watching the pearl merchant walk away with lighter saddlebags; he leaves it to later accountants to ask how long paper keeps its buying power when presses keep rolling.

---

Inside a palace wing in Cambaluc sit twelve high barons of the *Shieng*, overseeing thirty-four subject provinces [19] through regional judges, tax clerks, and governors confirmed by the throne. Yet decrees drafted on mulberry paper mean nothing unless an empire has legs to carry them.

Follow any stone trunk road out of Cambaluc and every twenty-five or thirty miles—or thirty-five to forty-five across emptier country—rises a walled station of the *Yamb*, the imperial horse post. Inside wait fresh saddled mounts and beds hung with silk for resting envoys [20]. Polo’s inventory counts over three hundred thousand horses and ten thousand station houses across the empire [21]. Leave the grand capital ledger aside for a moment and step into a wayside stable yard: what matters to a courier spurring up lathered in dust is whether a fresh horse is standing bridled in the stall.

Between the horse-posts, listen into the night for brass bells. Foot-runners wearing wide bell-girdles sprint three-mile stages between small relay booths. Long before a runner rounds the bend, the next man hears the jingle, ties on his belt, takes the wrapped packet from a clerk who marks the hour on a tally, and vanishes down the track [22]. Fresh orchard fruit picked in Cambaluc one morning reaches the Kaan’s table at Chandu by the following evening—ten days’ ordinary travel compressed into a single relay sprint [23]. An emperor’s dinner table makes a wonderfully vivid yardstick for an expensive state communications machine.

When dispatches carry life-and-death urgency, express riders wrap tightly in broad bandages, seize a horn, and blow a blast half a mile out from each post-house so a fresh mount stands waiting before their boots hit the cobbles. They cover two hundred miles or more in a single day, slowing only when torch-bearers must trot beside them through moonless dark, and a rider flashing a gerfalcon tablet can even pull a fresh horse straight from beneath a private traveller’s saddle [24]. Half a station’s horses rest in nearby village meadows while the other half stand ready in harness, backed by ferry boats waiting at every river crossing [25].

Those same roads carry news back toward the throne. When hailstorms or locusts ruin a harvest, imperial inspectors ride out to remit taxes, supply seed grain, and replace lost cattle [26]. If a stray arrow from the Kaan’s own bow happens to strike a passing flock or boatload of wares, the lucky owner pays no duty on the cargo for years after—turning even a royal miss into a tax break [27]. Shade trees line the great highways so couriers never lose their way in snow, with tall cairns and stone pillars marking treeless steppe; Cublay plants them willingly, trusting an astrologer’s promise that tree-planters earn long life [28]. At journey’s end, a tired rider pours clear, potent rice wine spiced to taste—harmless-looking in the cup, yet quick to go straight to the head [29].

---

Walk through winter evening alleys in Cambaluc and another everyday marvel glows behind courtyard grates. Ordinary households dig black stones out of the mountains and lay them on their hearths. Light a lump at dusk and it burns steadily until dawn. It throws off fierce heat and costs far less than carting firewood into town [30]. Picture Europe’s shrinking oak forests for a moment, then watch the whole population of Cambaluc streaming into public bathhouses at least three times every week [31]. Without black burning stones stacked beside the boiler doors, a city that size would strip every timber grove for fifty miles around before Christmas.

State foresight reaches the pantry as well. In fat harvest years, imperial agents buy up surplus grain and store it in dry granaries. When famine strikes, clerks throw open the doors and sell corn back to citizens at a quarter of the market price [32]. Poor families receive yearly grain doles, while thirty thousand needy petitioners line up daily at a palace gate for hot loaves [33]. At the same time, weavers contribute a weekly share of wool, silk, and hemp cloth to clothe the destitute [34]. In Cambaluc’s markets, a single Venetian groat buys a little printed *Tacuin* weather-almanac compiled by five thousand court astrologers [35]. Every citizen tracks his fortune by the twelve-year animal cycle stamped from the day of his birth [36].

Look inside an ordinary household doorway before leaving the city. Incense curls upward before an inscribed tablet set high on the wall, while down on the floor stands a felt figure of Natigai with his wife and children, petitioned for mild weather and good crops [37]. Beside those household rites sits a belief that souls pass upward or downward into new lives after death [38]. Even inside the palace itself, strict discipline rules every step: barons kick off street footwear at the hall door and pull on clean white leather boots so no dust touches the woven carpets, tucking small covered spittoons into their sleeves [39]. Out on the street, decrees ban gambling, punish filial neglect, and brand released prisoners on the cheek [40]. Keep both pairs of boots straight in mind, because court life leaves little margin for a careless step.

For five chapters we have lived inside the walls of Cublay’s capital—walking his banquet carpets, watching his tiger-carts, auditing his paper mint, and listening to his night watch-bells. Now the Great Kaan is sending his messenger outward along the first great spoke of the imperial wheel. Leave the clean white court boots at the palace door and buckle on riding spurs: Marco has imperial business down the western highway, where carved stone lions wait above muddy water ten miles outside the gate, and four months of mountain roads stretch beyond them.

[14] ✓ Ramusio-only in part. The sequence of signatures, seals and the final red impression is bracketed in Yule. The issue of paper notes is already in the base account [@yc, 2.24]. See [Diagram C1, paper money](#fig-c1-paper-money).

[16] ~ Polo describes the state as the purchaser of imported bullion and gems and assigns the valuation to twelve experts. Yule compares other accounts of exchange between the government and merchants [@yc, 2.24 n. 1].

[17] ✓ The Book puts the fee for replacing a damaged note at three per cent. The charge belongs to an official exchange system, just as wear belongs to the experience of the person carrying the note [@yc, 2.24 n. 1; @vogel2013marco, ch. 1].

[18] ✓ The Zhongtong notes date from 1260 and continued to circulate with the new Zhiyuan issue. The rate of five old units to one new was the official conversion of 1287 [@yc, 2.24 n. 1; @guan2024yuan].

[23] ~ The fruit is Polo’s example of the speed of a relay between the two capitals. Yule compares another royal household’s transport of perishable fruit [@yc, 2.26 n. 5].

[24] ~ The urgent tablet fits the system of travelling credentials. The figures are Polo’s, and his text itself makes the pace fall after dark when torches have to keep up [@yc, 2.26 n. 7].

[25] ~ The Book identifies a local obligation to provide horses and, where needed, boats. It assigns horses to imperial expense in uninhabited stretches [@yc, 2.26].
