# Chapter I-10
## Pamier, Cascar, Samarcand, Yarcan, Cotan: Roof of the World to Jade Country

> *“The plain is called Pamier, and you ride across it for twelve days together… The region is so lofty and cold that you do not even see any birds flying.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 32 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Follow the mountain river upstream out of Badashan (Badakhshan). For twelve days the trail climbs north-east along narrowing banks through lands ruled by a brother of the Badashan prince [1]. Villages and scattered farms still dot the valley floor, so a caravan can buy bread and fodder without opening its emergency packs. Yet every day’s ascent narrows the gorge and warns the muleteers how soon the last inhabited hamlet will drop behind.

See [the map of the highlands and Tarim, I-9](ch-09-balc-badashan.md#map-of-the-highlands-and-tarim).

At the top of that climb sits Vocan (Wakhan), a pocket kingdom only three days wide in any direction. Its chief bears the title *None*—count, in the Book’s gloss—and holds the valley under Badashan [2]. The people are Muslims with a speech of their own and a reputation as stout fighters. A traveller can ask whose banner flies over the valley and get a clear answer, even if he cannot follow the local speech in the bazaar. For a caravan leading laden mules, though, a valley’s politics matter far less than its hay: how many days remain to buy forage before the grass runs out? Game still roams these slopes, and three days higher into the peaks brings you to what the Book takes for the highest ground on earth, where even the bushes vanish.

Look out between the ridges at what greets a drover with a worn-out horse. The stream pours out of a high mountain lake and winds across a plain whose grass, the Book swears, will fatten a lean beast in ten days [3], [4]. Behind that wonder sits a hard caravan calculation: can your pack-animals put on enough weight here to survive the barren crossing ahead? Every ounce of flesh on a mule’s ribs is insurance once the grass disappears, and every day spent grazing in these meadows buys another safe stage across the waste.

Giant wild sheep roam these heights with horns *"good six palms"* long. Shepherds hollow them into bowls and stack spare horns into night pens to shelter their flocks from the wind [5]. Wolves leave scattered bones and horns across the slopes, and riders pile those bleached horns into tall wayside cairns to mark the track when snow buries the road [6]. A traveller spotting one of those horn-cairns above the drift needs far less help from local guides than he would on an unmarked pass. On a treeless plateau where blizzards wipe out every hoofprint in an hour, a cairn of wild sheep horns is the only architecture keeping a column from riding off a cliff.

Then comes the Pamier (Pamir Plateau) itself: twelve days with every ounce of food lashed to your saddles, where no houses stand, no green blade sprouts, and not even a bird crosses the sky [7]. Stranger still, a camp-fire misbehaves here—burning dull and weak so that a supper pot takes forever to boil [8]. The Book blames the freezing air; modern climbers know thin high-altitude air starves both lungs and flames, and local tongues named these heights the "Roof of the World" [9].

Pause beside that stubborn campfire for a second. Even today, anyone who has tried to boil pasta or brew coffee high in the Rockies or the Alps knows how water simmers at a lower temperature and leaves rice crunchy. In the thirteenth century, with no barometer and no thermometer in your saddlebag, a fire that burns pale and refuses to cook your mutton is the sharpest physical proof you have climbed onto the roof of the earth. Every cold camp feels twice as far from home when supper stays half-raw in the pot.

Beyond lies Bolor (the Gilgit–Chitral mountains), forty days of peaks, gorges, and wilderness where skin-clad hunters scrape a living and win little praise [10]. Any reader trying to trace a straight caravan road across those forty days soon sees the snag: the narrative pauses its march to sketch wild mountain folk off the main track, leaving gaps between the last market and the next oasis. That pause reminds us the Book’s map is built from travellers’ reports as well as a single line of march.

Watch how the scale changes the moment the caravan drops off those frozen ridges. For weeks, every conversation has been about hay, sheep-horn cairns, and whether a fire will boil water. Ahead in the Tarim Basin, the desert is ringed by green oasis towns fed by snowmelt rivers, where merchants weigh cotton, wine, and river-jade instead of counting emergency rations. Every caravan emerging from the gorges breathes easier at the sight of irrigation ditches and walled gardens.

---

Down off the heights at last, Cascar (Kashgar) greets the caravan with vineyards, cotton fields, walled gardens, and merchants who range far abroad. Several towns dot the oasis under the city’s name [11]. Nestorian Christians keep their churches here beside Muslim neighbours, and townsfolk speak a language of their own [12]. After twelve days of gnawing cold rations on the plateau, a rider has finally dropped into a market where traders pack goods out rather than hoarding them, and where fresh fruit hangs over garden walls. The contrast between frozen, birdless rock and shaded orchard lanes could hardly be sharper.

The Book assigns Cascar to the Great Kaan, though rival Mongol princes made allegiance on the ground far messier [13]. In these oases, you can stand in a bazaar credited to one khan and still ask whose tax-collectors actually hold the gate. To illustrate that rivalry, the narrative swings north-west for a wonder set in Samarcan (Samarkand), ruled by Caidu (Kaidu)—Cublay (Kublai)’s kinsman and bitter enemy—alongside the house of Sigatay (Chagatai), though Marco himself likely never set foot there [14].

The story turns on a single block of stone. Sigatay, the tale runs, converted to Christianity, and his fellow believers built a church to St. John the Baptist—wedging a stone taken from the Muslims beneath the central pillar holding up the roof [15]. Once the prince died, the stone’s owners demanded it back. The Christians offered silver instead; the new ruler gave them three days to strike a bargain or surrender the block [16]. Picture standing under those heavy timber rafters as the three-day sandglass runs out, knowing every builder in town expects the roof to crash down the instant the keystone is dragged clear.

Silver failed, and the congregation turned to prayer. No mason could shore up those massive beams in time; only a miracle could keep the timber from crashing down when the block was hauled out. Picture the entire Christian quarter crowded beneath the nave at daybreak, shoulders braced, watching the Muslim workmen wedge iron levers beneath the foundation stone while the roof-timbers creak overhead.

On the appointed morning, a gap three palms wide opened cleanly beneath the pillar’s foot—yet the column hung fast in mid-air, holding up the whole roof as if nothing had moved. The Muslims carried their stone away, and the hanging pillar supposedly stands there still [17]. Generations later, a church inscription far east in China founded by Mar Sarghis (Mar Sargis)—whose family hailed from Samarcan—told the very same miracle of a floating column [18]. Ask who carried the story across the continent, and you no longer have to stake everything on the physics of the roof: a family of Samarcan Christians carried their hometown’s proudest legend all the way to the Yangtze.

---

Back on the southern oasis road at Yarcan (Yarkand), Caidu holds sway over Nestorian, Jacobite, and Muslim townsfolk [19]. Many passers-by carry swollen throats—goitres blamed locally on the drinking water [20]—a bodily mark any visitor remembers long after he forgets the governor’s name. Walk down a Yarcan street and you notice it before you ever ask a price: a physical signature of the mountain snowmelt that tells a traveller immediately he has crossed out of the Pamier passes into a different valley. And look at how quickly the oasis economy shifts from mountain wool and wild sheep to irrigated fields and busy looms.

Cotton gives a merchant an even sharper reason to remember this stretch. Yarcan weaves it in abundance [21], and eight days east at Cotan (Hotan) the oasis pairs cotton with orchards, vineyards, busy workshops [22], flax, hemp, wheat, and wine [23]. Enough grows here to feed a caravan and fill its return packs at once. A trader who only knew Cotan from its cotton bales still needed to know whether his men could buy bread, wine, and fodder while waiting for a buyer, and Cotan answers both questions at the same gate. These are towns where a caravan rests its beasts, mends harness, and trades cloth for fresh provisions without losing a week on the road. Every oasis along this desert rim acts like a port of call on a dry ocean, where merchants refit before steering out into the next stretch of sand.

Next comes Pein (probably Uzun-tatïr, near Keriya), another cotton oasis with a quiet fortune hidden in its riverbeds. Waders pull smooth stones from the streams—jasper and chalcedony in the Book’s words, the prized jade of the Tarim rivers—even if the exact town site remains disputed today [24]. Picture men standing knee-deep in snowmelt water at dawn, feeling with bare toes for smooth, heavy cobbles that look dull wet grey until a dealer’s thumb rubs them dry. A broker on the bank sorts white pebbles from green by colour and weight before loading a single mule, while a mapmaker centuries later still struggles to pin that river-bank to one coordinate.

Here too the Book records a brisk local rule on absence: if a husband stays away more than twenty days, his wife may take another spouse, and he may marry again wherever his road has carried him [25]. Two people watch that twenty-day clock very differently. A merchant delayed by snow or broken axles knows how fast three weeks vanish without a courier; a wife watching the city gate knows the exact morning her waiting ends and settles her household accordingly. If you have ever waited at a crossroads for a late caravan, twenty days sounds like an eternity—or no time at all, depending on whether you hold the reins or the hearth. Every oasis town along this rim of the desert lives by calendars like that, where marriage, credit, and departure are all measured in weeks of sand.

To this whole chain of oases the Book gives the name Great Turkey, stretching all the way to Lop (Charklik) [26]. At Pein, a caravan can still pack saddlebags with cotton cloth and river-jade, mend its harness, and eat fresh fruit beneath orchard walls. Beyond those pebbles, on the road to Charchan (Cherchen) and Lop, every rider must count his water-skins again before the dunes swallow the trail, and every guide watches the shifting sand for sweet wells along the desert rim before thirty waterless days begin in earnest.

## Notes

[1] ~ The river, stages and government of the land are given in the Book. Yule and Cordier discuss the approach to the upper Oxus and compare lines of travel in the mountains [@yc, 1.32 n. 1].

[2] ✓ The place is the Wakhan country. The size and dependency are Polo’s, and Yule discusses the title and possible readings of the word the Book gives [@yc, 1.32 n. 1].

[3] ~ The French has the plain, river and good grass. The ten days are the Book’s estimate of the effect on a lean animal [@eusebi2018devisement, ch. 49 § 9; @yc, 1.32].

[4] ~ Shared by TA and Ramusio. Yule brackets the lake, which Moule–Pelliot also mark in the Tuscan witness. The Venetian V describes two rivers [@moule1938description, ch. 50, pp. 141–142; @simion2019devisement, 26.16–17; @yc, 1.32].

[5] ~ V also describes the horns made into bowls. Blyth uses *Ovis Polii* in 1841 and refers to Polo. The species name gives no separate evidence that Polo measured an individual animal [@simion2019devisement, 26.16–17; @yc, 1.32 n. 2; @blyth1841ovis, p. 195].

[6] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The wolves occur in Zelada and Ramusio, while the wayside heaps of horns and bones that serve as markers in the snow are in Ramusio’s text [@moule1938description, ch. 50, pp. 141–142; @yc, 1.32].

[7] ~ The duration, lack of settlements and absence of birds belong to Polo’s account. Yule and Cordier consider different lines for the passage [@yc, 1.32 nn. 1–2].

[8] ? The Book puts the weak fire and difficulty cooking down to cold. Yule weighs several explanations, and the cause of the particular difficulty described stays open [@yc, 1.32 n. 2].

[9] ✓ Yule quotes Wood’s account of 19 February 1838, which gives *Bám-i-Duniah* as a local expression. The phrase comes into Yule’s commentary through that quotation [@yc, 1.32 n. 2].

[10] ~ Pelliot prefers a form equivalent to Balūr and discusses proposed limits for the mountain country. The duration, subsistence claim and judgement of the inhabitants belong to the Book [@pelliot1959notes, vol. 1, pp. 91–92; @yc, 1.32 n. 3].

[11] ✓ The Book gives its chief city a share in textile production and trade. Yule discusses the road by which a party might have reached this oasis before Yarcan [@yc, 1.33 n. 1].

[12] ~ The religious communities, churches and local speech are particulars in the Book. Its comments on the habits of the people are its own [@yc, 1.33].

[13] ? Yule describes competing political powers and a geography on which a simple assignment of allegiance sits uneasily. The Book has Cascar and Cotan subject to the Great Kaan and assigns Yarcan to Caidu [@yc, 1.33 n. 1; @yc, 1.35–36].

[14] ~ Polo presents Caidu as a rival of the emperor. Yule discusses the sharing of power in Samarcan and treats the passage as an account of a place Marco did not visit [@yc, 1.34 n. 1].

[15] ✗ Sigatay was the uncle of Cublay. The conversion and its connection to the church are part of the story in the Book; a historical conversion has yet to be established [@yc, 1.34 n. 2; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Marsarchis”].

[16] ~ In the Book the Christians offer to pay and must take the time set by the ruler to resolve the dispute. The property of the stone and the damage its removal might do give the story its stakes [@yc, 1.34].

[17] ~ The three palms and the standing column are the Book’s report. Its claim of a visible survival belongs to the miracle account [@yc, 1.34 n. 3].

[18] ~ An inscription by Liang Hsiang for the Mar Sargis church at Chên-chiang also has a hanging pillar. Pelliot proposes Mar Sargis as Polo’s informant. This leaves a possible shared source behind the two accounts [@pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Marsarchis”].

[19] ~ The territory’s allegiance and Christian communities are given by Polo. The chapter treats Caidu as the ruler it has already named [@yc, 1.35].

[20] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The swollen throat has support in V and L. The water explanation is in the bracketed Ramusio material, while reports of goitre in later visitors do not establish that cause [@simion2019devisement, 28.3; @moule1938description, ch. 53, p. 146; @yc, 1.35 n. 1].

[21] ~ Ramusio-only. He adds cotton and the skill of the craftsmen to the remarks about the resources of Yarcan [@yc, 1.35].

[22] ✓ The French describes cotton, vineyards, gardens and trades at the oasis. Yule compares later reports about the agriculture [@eusebi2018devisement, ch. 53 §§ 4–8; @yc, 1.36 n. 3].

[23] ~ Moule–Pelliot mark flax, hemp, oil and wheat in L and VL. Wine is present in V as well as Ramusio [@moule1938description, ch. 54, p. 146; @simion2019devisement, 28.8; @yc, 1.36].

[24] ? Yule links the description of the stones to jade gathered in the wider region. Pelliot preferred Uzun-tatïr, which Bernardini considers possible, and Cordier discusses an alternative near Keriya [@yc, 1.37 nn. 1–2; @ycadd, on 1.37; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, pp. 800–802; @bernardini2008polo].

[25] ~ The twenty-day allowance is Polo’s account. Yule compares arrangements for temporary marriage elsewhere without establishing the precise rule in Pein [@yc, 1.37 n. 3].

[26] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The name Great Turkey is in the base account; the bracketed Ramusio clause expressly extends it to the city of Lop [@yc, 1.37].
