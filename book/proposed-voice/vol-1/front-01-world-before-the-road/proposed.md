### How to listen to a seven-hundred-year-old book

Seven centuries ago, this book was never meant to be read in silence. It began in 1298 inside a stone prison in Genoa, where a Venetian merchant named Marco Polo poured out twenty-four years of Eastern memories to a fellow captive—Rustichello of Pisa, a professional writer of King Arthur romances. Their collaboration was built to be read aloud to rooms full of people who wanted two things at once: hard merchant truth (what pepper costs, how mulberry-bark paper money works, where the desert water holes lie) and grand chivalric spectacle (twelve thousand horsemen dressed in robes of beaten gold).

Read a literal medieval translation today, however, and you trip over repetitive scribe formulas on every page (*Now let us quit this province and tell you of the next…*). Read the monumental 1903 Yule–Cordier scholarly edition, and every sentence is ambushed by fifty lines of tiny Victorian footnotes arguing over vowel shifts and extinct bishoprics.

This retelling strikes a different bargain. Onstage, in the main text, one narrator walks the road with you in living modern English—keeping Marco’s sharp eye for cargoes and prices, Rustichello’s flair for court theatre, and the vivid sixteenth-century additions of Giovan Battista Ramusio, while stepping forward whenever a thirteenth-century marvel needs a quick physical translation for modern eyes. Backstage, every small number `[n]` leads to the Notes at the end of the chapter, where the same voice puts on the historian’s spectacles to weigh Chinese dynastic annals, Persian chronicles, excavated shipwrecks, and seven hundred years of detective work (`✓ ~ ✗ ?`) without ever stopping the show. The closing essay explains the [principles of the retelling](../vol-3/ch-12-reliability-essay.md#principles-of-this-retelling) in full.

Before the curtain rises on that Genoese prison cell, look at the world of 1260 that sent two Venetian brothers east with a pouch of jewels—a world already joined by Mediterranean galleys and Mongol post-roads, and split by fierce wars over who controlled the best ports and passes [1].

### The Mediterranean as a counting-house

Venetian and Genoese merchants traded in spices, silk, alum, grain, and enslaved people. Their galleys called at Acre (Akko), a Crusader foothold and trading port until its fall in 1291 [2].

Harbours were open to both cities, yet customs ledgers tipped the scales. At Constantinople (Istanbul), a Venetian merchant sold cargo duty-free thanks to Venice’s part in the 1204 conquest, while a Genoese trader paid. When Michael Palaeologus retook the city in 1261, Genoa collected the reward instead [3]. A merchant’s bargaining began well before his ship dropped anchor.

Across the Black Sea, Venetians kept a trading post at Soldaia (Sudak) in Crimea. Marco the Elder owned a house there and left it to the Franciscans in his will, reserving life residence for his children [4]. His brothers Niccolò and Maffeo belonged to a family with an address on either side of the crossing.

### Pax Mongolica

Within two generations, Mongol conquests had toppled Khwarezm, the Abbasid caliphate at Baudas (Baghdad), and the Jin in northern China. Under the relative security historians call *Pax Mongolica*, envoys and caravans crossed Eurasia [5].

Picture what a fresh horse meant on the steppe. At a relay station, a rider carrying a khan’s *paiza*—an inscribed tablet of authority—traded a spent mount for a fresh one and claimed supplies [6]. When the Franciscan friar William of Rubruck rode east in the 1250s, his escort changed horses two or three times on good days, or rode days without seeing a soul; when mounts failed, the friars shifted baggage onto pack animals, doubled up on one horse, and thanked heaven for morning millet gruel or evening mutton broth [7].

Four Mongol realms anchor our story: Cublay (Kublai) in China and Mongolia, later called the Yuan; the Ilkhanate in Persia and Iraq; the Golden Horde on the Volga steppe; and the heirs of Sigatay (Chagatai) in Central Asia [8]. A road open under one khan could snap shut at the next frontier—as when Barca (Berke) fought Alaü (Hulagu), or Caidu (Kaidu) defied Cublay [9].

### Venice and Genoa

In Acre, the church of St Sabba straddled the boundary between the Venetian and Genoese quarters. A quarrel over it broke out in 1255 and grew into a multi-year war [10]. Venice drove Genoa out of Acre, yet neither city could cross the sea safely without armed convoys—a feud that returns in [Chapter I-1](ch-01-genoa-prison.md), which weighs how Marco ended up a prisoner in Genoa in 1298 [11].

![Timeline B1. Marco’s life and the family documents. His birth is inferred, two accounts date his capture differently, and two years have been reported for his uncle Maffeo’s will. *(Timeline: this edition.)*](../media/timelines/B1-polo-family.svg){#fig-b1-polo-family}

### Why Latins went east around 1260

Mongol courts drew merchants carrying rare jewels and skilled artisans—like the Parisian goldsmith William Buchier, whom Rubruck found busy on an imperial commission in Caracoron (Karakorum) [12]. Curiosity travelled alongside commerce: friars John of Plano Carpini and Rubruck brought word of Cathay (northern China) back to Europe [13], giving returning Venetians sharper questions to carry east in [Chapter I-2](ch-02-house-of-polo.md).

### Names, dates, and the outbound volume

The book’s own place-names come first, with modern equivalents in brackets on first mention. Approximate dates mark scholarly reconstructions—such as 1260 in the epigraph, Yule’s working date for the Polo brothers’ Black Sea crossing [14].

[Maps for the Outbound Road](front-02-maps-outbound.md) traces Volume I from Venice to Chandu (Shangdu) and the threshold of Cambaluc (Beijing).

Listen first for the clash of Venetian and Genoese oars—because our story opens not on the open road, but behind iron bars in Genoa in 1298, where a captured merchant is telling a romance-writer to pick up his pen and call the room to order.

[1] ✓ Historical context is drawn from the Yule–Cordier introductions, Larner’s [-@larner1999marco, chs. 9–10] reception history, Mongol-empire surveys by Morgan [-@morgan1986mongols] and Rossabi [-@rossabi1988khubilai, chs. 1–2], and Allsen [-@allsen2001culture] on Eurasian cultural exchange. This opening companion frames the political and commercial stage rather than retelling a chapter of *Il Milione*.

[2] ✓ The Venetian and Genoese quarters at Acre were bases for trade through Syria. Yule describes the fall of the port in 1291 as a blow to a southern route for goods from Asia [@yc, Intro. § 31].

[3] ✓ Under the Latin Empire, Venice enjoyed exemptions and trading rights the Genoese lacked. Yule describes Genoa’s alliance with Michael Palaeologus, who displaced Baldwin II in 1261 and rewarded his allies [@yc, Intro. § 31; @yc, Prol. 1 n. 1].

[4] ✓ Yule dates the Venetian trading post at Soldaia to about the middle of the century. The elder Marco’s 1280 will names his house there and spells out the provision for his son and daughter [@yc, Prol. 1 nn. 1–2].

[5] ~ *Pax Mongolica* is a modern term for the relative security Mongol rule could provide. Routes and conditions varied with local politics [@morgan1986mongols; @larner1999marco, ch. 1].

[6] ✓ The Prologue gives the Polos a gold tablet authorising supplies and assistance for their embassy [@yc, Prol. 8]. Yule compares that practice with other uses of Mongol tablets of authority [@yc, 2.7 n. 2].

[7] ✓ Rubruck’s description of travel across the country he calls Cangle gives both the frequency of the horse changes and the difficulties his party faced between stops [@rockhill1900journey, pp. 131–132].

[8] ✓ The four realms took shape over decades, and the claim to supreme Mongol authority outlasted effective control over all four. Morgan [-@morgan1986mongols] surveys the successor realms, and Rossabi [-@rossabi1988khubilai, chs. 1–2] gives the background to Cublay’s rise.

[9] ✓ The war between Barca and Alaü broke out in 1262. Caidu’s resistance to Cublay was a later conflict, centred on Central Asia [@yc, Prol. 2 n. 3; @morgan1986mongols].

[10] ✓ Yule dates the dispute at Acre to 1255 and describes the war that followed as lasting years. His account traces the fighting from the trading quarters to attacks on shipping [@yc, Intro. § 31].

[11] ? The French Prologue dates the writing of the Book in Genoa to 1298 [@yc, Prol. preamble]. Yule favoured Ramusio’s tradition of capture at Curzola (Korčula). Jacopo d’Acqui put the capture at Ayas [@yc, Intro. § 37]. See [Chapter I-1](ch-01-genoa-prison.md), Notes 7–8.

[12] ✓ Allsen [-@allsen2001culture, ch. 1] documents the movement of specialists across Mongol Eurasia. Rubruck names the goldsmith as William Buchier and records writing to ask for the services of a young man in Buchier’s household [@rockhill1900journey, pp. 177–178].

[13] ✓ Yule credits Carpini and Rubruck with bringing back accounts of Cathay. Rubruck identified its people with the Seres of classical geography [@yc, Intro. § 16].

[14] ~ The principal manuscripts give 1250. Yule used the chronology of the brothers’ venture and their return to Venice in 1269 to propose 1260 as the likelier year of the crossing [@yc, Prol. 1 n. 1].
