# Chapter I-11
## Charchan, Lop, Tangut, Camul: Desert Voices and the Edge of Cathay

> *“Even in the day-time one hears those spirits talking. And sometimes you shall hear the sound of a variety of musical instruments, and still more commonly the sound of drums.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 39 (Yule–Cordier)

---

A river near Pein (probably Uzun-tatïr, near Keriya) yields pebbles worth hauling half a continent to sell. At Charchan (Cherchen), streams still wash down jasper and chalcedony fetching handsome prices in Cathay (North China)—yet from here onward, clean water becomes the only cargo that keeps you alive [1]. Families who know where hidden desert springs lie can vanish ahead of an invading army: pack kin and livestock two or three days into the dunes, let the wind erase every hoofprint, and leave pursuers guessing where to dig [2]. In a waste where sand swallows every track by nightfall, knowing which hollow holds sweet water is as good as owning a stone fortress. Even a pursuing force cannot follow where it cannot drink.

Five days across sand bring the caravan to Lop (Charklik), where sweet water flows again. Every party bound across the Great Desert halts here for a full week to rest riders and beasts, mend girths, and lay in a month’s provisions [3]. Out in the dunes there are no shops for anything forgotten—no salt, no flour, no spare horseshoe, and no well-bucket if a rope snaps. At its narrowest waist, the crossing takes thirty days, and the very first spring—reached after a day and a night—barely waters fifty or a hundred riders and their mounts. Across the whole stretch, the Book counts twenty-eight sweet springs and four brackish ones [4]. When you are riding thirsty, it matters enormously how far ahead the vanguard has pushed, and whether the men in front left anything in the basin for the stragglers behind. On a desert track, the order of march is a matter of water as much as speed.

Picture it at night. A rider lags behind the caravan to tighten a girth and suddenly hears someone call him by his own name. It is the exact sound he has been straining to hear. Ahead of his comrades, a straggler might halt and wait; behind them in the pitch dark, every instinct tells him to spur toward the voice. By the time he realises there is no lantern ahead, he has wandered too far from the column to find his way back. Spirits haunt these dunes, the Book warns—filling even broad daylight with *"the sound of a variety of musical instruments, and still more commonly the sound of drums"* [5]. One vivid telling even has a lost rider hear an entire ghostly cavalcade thunder past, follow it till dawn, and wake alone in the sand [6]. Centuries earlier, Buddhist pilgrims crossing these same dunes also dreaded phantom voices and goblins in the dark [7]. When a man loses his bearings on shifting sand, desert folklore fastens straight onto whatever he strains hardest to hear.

Experienced guides carry practical insurance before stepping onto the dunes: hang brass bells on every pack-animal’s neck, keep tight formation, and plant a wooden marker at each night’s camp pointing the morning’s course [8]. A bell answers a rider’s ear; a signpost answers his eye. Both are worth remembering in a chapter that opens with a man led astray by his own name. When wind rises over the dunes, a brass bell and a wooden stake are worth all the courage in the caravan.

---

Out of the dunes, the road reaches Sachiu (Dunhuang) in Tangut, where Buddhists, Nestorian Christians, and Muslims share the oasis [9]. Close by stand the painted caves of Mogao, preserving centuries of Buddhist devotion, though the Book leaves no record of Marco stepping inside a single shrine [10]. After thirty days of sand, the sight of irrigated fields and monastery roofs tells a rider he has crossed into another world.

When a family here seeks a blessing for its children, it brings a fattened sheep to the temple feast. Cooked and laid before the image while prayers go up, the meat is carried home to be shared among kinsfolk [11]—while the priests receive the head, feet, entrails, skin, and a generous share of the meat [12]. Someone has fed that lamb for months; the children watch it pass from the family pen to the altar and back to the supper table.

Though the Book lumps all “idolaters” together as folk who burn their dead, its eye for a Tangut funeral is razor-sharp. Silk and cloth drape a wooden pavilion along the procession route, spread with food and wine. At the pyre, paper cut-outs of servants, horses, camels, and coin go into the flames so the dead arrive well furnished in the next world [13]. The mourners know exactly where both the body and its paper retinue are bound, and they pack the afterlife as carefully as a merchant packs a caravan for the road.

Some households keep a corpse for months before cremation, hiring astrologers to cast the dead person’s birth date for an auspicious day while craftsmen seal every joint of the thick painted coffin [14]. Packed with camphor and spices—another portable luxury a merchant can sell by the pound—the coffin receives daily meals, and if a soothsayer declares the front threshold unlucky, mourners knock a hole straight through the house wall to carry the dead out another way [15]. It is a strikingly specific custom, showing how closely Marco watched what a household actually spent on its dead. A merchant selling camphor and spice remembers the mourners who buy them by the pound.

Pause inside that Tangut courtyard for a moment to feel the sheer practical drama of it. A family keeps a painted cedar box in the main room for months, setting hot rice and wine before it every morning while waiting for the stars to align. When the auspicious dawn finally arrives, masons take hammers to the mud-brick wall rather than risk carrying the dead over the living family’s doorstep. Every detail—from the camphor resin sealing the seams to the paper camels waiting by the pyre—turns grief into a visible, costly ceremony.

---

Camul (Hami) sits like a green island between two wastes, stocked with provisions and famous for singing and dancing [16]. Hospitality here, the Book claims, goes far beyond music: a host cheerfully yields his wife to a passing guest and moves out until the stranger departs, counting the visit an honour. When Mongou (Möngke) forbade the custom [17], he ordered public inns built for travellers instead [18]. Three lean harvests then struck the oasis. The townsfolk blamed the ban and sent a costly embassy until the Kaan relented and restored their old ways [19]. The tale tells us a great deal about what European listeners loved to hear of distant manners; whether Marco ever walked Camul’s streets remains open.

Ask about a stranger marvel along this frontier. In a mountain of Chingintalas—a province whose exact site remains unfixed [20]—veins of steel run beside a mineral called salamander. Marco’s acquaintance Zurficar spent three years mining it for the Great Kaan: asbestos, as we call the fibre today. European listeners knew “salamander” as a mythical lizard that lived in fire, so it took someone holding an actual woven cloth to shift the story. Once the rocky fibres are separated, beaten, and spun, the resulting napkin comes out of a blazing hearth whiter and cleaner than it went in [21]. A table-napkin pulled spotless from the coals gives a listener something far more tangible than a fire-lizard—something you can fold, weigh in your hand, and toss into the embers to prove it. Watch how a merchant dismantles a legend without losing the wonder: he keeps the miracle of fireproof cloth, swaps the mythical lizard for a quarryman’s pickaxe, and lets the Great Kaan’s own miner explain how stone turns into thread. The Book adds that the Kaan even sent one to Rome to wrap the Lord’s Sudarium, a gift that remains unverified [22].

---

Back on the main road east lies Sukchur (Jiuquan), where fine mountain rhubarb draws traders from afar, packing roots for apothecaries across Asia [23]. Yet a herdsman must watch his beasts’ mouths as closely as his purse: a poisonous plant on these slopes makes foreign horses and cattle shed their hooves, while native beasts know enough to leave it untouched—a warning to hire only local pack-animals here [24]. A merchant eyeing roots in a basket and a drover watching a mare crop grass are looking at two halves of the same mountain landscape, one worth carrying home and the other worth steering clear of.

Not far along this corridor stands the pass at Jiayuguan. The surviving Ming stone fortress dates to 1372, a full century after the Polos’ journey, leaving uncertain what stood on that exact spot in the 1270s [25]. Famously, the Book never mentions a Great Wall. Modern debate has pressed that silence hard, yet scattered thirteenth-century tamped-earth ramparts bore little resemblance to the continuous brick battlements later visitors expected a traveller to name [26]. For a reader standing at the pass today, the silence is a reminder not to project later stone battlements back onto a thirteenth-century caravan road.

In Campichu (Zhangye), step inside the temples the Book separates from the city’s three Christian churches [27]. Wood, clay, and stone statues gleam under lacquer and gold leaf, chief among them huge reclining figures ringed by smaller images—and a colossal reclining Buddha still survives in the city today [28]. Even a hurried visitor remembers scale like that, when a single gilt figure fills half a hall and dwarfs every worshipper at its feet.

Townsfolk here keep five fast days each month without meat or slaughter [29], and men take as many wives as their purses allow, with the first wife holding chief rank [30]. Understanding rules like those takes time—and the Book keeps Marco and Maffeo in Campichu for an entire year, though the manuscripts disagree on who stayed there and on what errand [31]. A whole year in one garrison town gives a traveller time to watch the fast days come round, learn who sits at the head of a household, and see caravans arrive from both ends of the road with news of the steppe and the court. After months of singing dunes, ghost-voices, and mountain rhubarb, Campichu feels like a city where a merchant can finally unpack his trunks and listen.

If you have come all the way from Lop’s singing dunes to Campichu’s gilt halls, notice how the Book measures distance not only in days of water and sand, but in the customs a visitor only learns by staying put for a season. You can ride past a temple in an afternoon; you need a winter and a summer in one town to understand how five monthly fast days shape the butcher’s stall.

When the caravan finally packs up after that year-long halt, turn twelve days north toward Etzina (Khara-Khoto), where the next reckoning will be how many days of flour, dried meat, and water you can lash to your camels before the desert closes round the road again.
