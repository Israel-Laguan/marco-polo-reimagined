# Eleven sea-chapter claims checked against primary sources

Across eleven targeted inquiries, direct source-checking confirms some of the standard annotations for Polo's sea chapters but overturns several working assumptions behind the brief itself: Pelliot's BASMAN entry sits at vol. I, pp. 102–103, not 86–87; the Cocachin fleet episode belongs to the Prologue (Ch. 19 in Moule–Pelliot), not Book III; Dayalan's 2019 article is titled "Ancient Seaports on the Eastern Coast of India: The Hub of the Maritime Silk Route Network," not the subtitle given in the brief; and Rougeulle's 2017 Qalhat article is single-authored, with CEFAS as the excavating sponsor rather than a co-author. Several claims the brief hoped to corroborate are **not found** in the sources actually reached — no inscription shows Rudrama Devi reissuing the Motupalli charter, no mainstream Polo scholar ties Chipangu's gold palace to Chūson‑ji's Konjikidō, and Karashima's 2014 chapter could not be read at all (catalogue record only). Access was real but uneven: a handful of items rest on genuine full-text reads (Dayalan 2019, Rougeulle 2017, part of Hall 2011, Talbot 2001, Nilakanta Sastri 1929), while many of the Pelliot, Taddesse Tamrat, Lambourn, Miksic, and Karashima claims rest only on OCR fragments, abstracts, catalogue records, or third-hand paraphrase — and are labelled as such throughout. The practical upshot for the annotated edition: a substantial core of claims is safe to print with precise citation, but a comparably large set of commonly repeated annotations (Pelliot on FERLEC/LAMBRI/PENTAN/SEILAN, Orlandini's exact wording, the Current Science 2019 author list, Lambourn's and Guillot–Kalus's own text, Karashima's revision of the Pandya chronology, and the Konjikidō link) must either be flagged as unverified or dropped until a researcher with institutional library/JSTOR/HathiTrust access re-checks the primary pages.

## 1. Pelliot, *Notes on Marco Polo* (1959–73): BASMAN, SEMENAT, MAABAR, SEILAN, SAGAMONI BORCAN, PENTAN, FERLEC, LAMBRI

**Access achieved:** page-scan OCR extraction (via WebFetch) of the Toyo Bunko "Digital Silk Road" digitization (call no. III‑2‑F‑c‑104), not a searchable full text and not the printed page image inspected directly. FERLEC, LAMBRI, PENTAN and SEILAN entries were never located at all within the digitization's reachable page range.

Pelliot's **BASMAN** entry (his article no. 62) was found at **vol. I, pp. 102–103** of the Toyo Bunko scan — a direct correction of the brief's stated pp. 86–87, reached by stepping sequentially through the "B" entries (Bailo p.86 → Balc p.87 → Baltasar p.88 → … → Basman pp.102–103). Pelliot places Basman in northern Sumatra, between his Ferlec and Sumatra articles, on manuscript spellings *basarom/besarom/bosaram* (MS VA) and *basmam/basmon* (MS FA, P). **SEMENAT** was found as a single-page fragment, image-labelled "p. 234" (not matching the brief's "II, p. 830," which could not be verified because the accessible Toyo Bunko vol. II folder tops out at an internal image label of "300"). Pelliot reports that all manuscripts agree on "Semenat" but that he himself emends to "Somenat," comparing the Medici nautical chart's "Somenath" (via Hallberg, p. 468) — i.e. he reads the name as a corruption of Somnath on the Gujarat coast, not as a distinct toponym.

A **SAGAMONI BORCAN** fragment (vol. II) derives the name from "Śākyamuni burqan" (Buddha Śākyamuni) and argues from Polo's earlier use of the term for Qubilai that Polo already knew the word before applying it at Adam's Peak, Ceylon — uncontroversial among Polo scholars, but read here only as a one-page fragment. A **MAABAR** fragment, found in the explicitly posthumous "Index" volume III (not the discursive vol. II text), gives only a one-line gloss: "Maabar: according to Montecorvino, it was Upper India" — an index cross-reference, not Pelliot's full discursive argument. **SEILAN, FERLEC, LAMBRI and PENTAN were not reached at all** within this research pass; the vol. II digitization used stops at an internal image label of "300," short of the 700s–800s printed pages the brief cites for these entries. The identifications offered in the note for these four (Seilan=Ceylon; Ferlec=Perlak; Lambri=Lambri/Lamuri near Aceh; Pentan=Bintan) are standard Polo-scholarship background, **not sourced to Pelliot's own text in this session**, and must not be attributed to Pelliot by page number in the annotated edition without a fresh read.

## 2. Moule & Pelliot (1938): the Cocachin fleet — ships, survivors, death toll

**Access achieved:** full OCR text of the archive.org scan of Moule & Pelliot vol. I (`descriptionofwor01polo`), read via an intermediate AI-extraction fetch tool rather than direct text search or page-image inspection; cross-checked against the Project Gutenberg Yule–Cordier (1903) text, same access method. Moule & Pelliot vol. II/III (commentary, Pelliot's "Cocacin" entry) and Benedetto's critical edition were not reached.

The episode is **not in Book III**: in both editions it belongs to the Prologue, Moule & Pelliot's **Chapter 19** (vol. I, pp. 90–91) — a location correction the editor should carry into the annotated edition. Moule & Pelliot's eclectic main text (based chiefly on MS F) gives **fourteen** ships with four masts; no "thirteen" variant was found in the apparatus retrieved, though this may reflect incomplete OCR-search coverage rather than a confirmed absence. The main text gives **eighteen** survivors reaching Persia, again with no footnoted "eight" variant in the Moule–Pelliot apparatus itself. The **eight-vs-eighteen** split instead appears to be an artifact of Yule's own editorial history: Yule–Cordier's Introductory Notices (not the main Prologue chapter, which could not be retrieved) state that "the French text" gives 8 survivors, but that Yule himself later found and flagged an older manuscript ("G.T.") giving 18, admitting he had "overlooked" this reading "until the sheets were printed." This suggests 8 and 18 are not two live rival manuscript readings still contested in Moule–Pelliot, but Yule's own translation choice versus a correction he belatedly incorporated — an inference, not confirmed against vol. II/III or Benedetto.

On the death toll, Moule & Pelliot's footnoted witnesses diverge: **FA, P give 600; FB gives 500; LT/TA give 700 (sub-variant ~600 men + 100 women); V gives 600 + 100 women; VB gives "more than 600"; L gives an anomalous "plusquam centum" (possibly an OCR/transcription error).** The Ramusio (R) reading — the figure matching the brief's "Ramusio = 600 dead" — is separately footnoted in Ramusio's own Italian as "about six hundred persons" dying "among the sailors and others... in the said ships," framed as covering the whole ship's company rather than passengers excluding sailors as the other witnesses are framed. Yule–Cordier's footnote independently gives "600 souls" excluding mariners, consistent with the FA/P/V/VB-type readings. **No explicit editorial verdict by Moule or Pelliot on which figure is correct was found** in the material retrieved; this must be reported as a gap, not inferred.

## 3. Motupalli pillar inscription (EI XII.22) and Rudrama Devi

**Access achieved:** full OCR-derived page text of *Epigraphia Indica* XII via jainqq.org (third-party OCR extraction, not the original plate/photograph), and the full OCR text (`_djvu.txt`) of Talbot, *Precolonial India in Practice* (2001) from archive.org, sampled across roughly half the book's ~987,000 characters.

*Epigraphia Indica* XII, no. 22, "Mōṭupaḷḷi Pillar-Inscription of Ganapatidēva," is edited by **E. Hultzsch**, printed on pp. **188–197**, dated Śaka 1166 (expired) = **A.D. 1244–45**. The edict's translated text states that "formerly kings used to take away by force the whole cargo" of ships "attacked by storms, wrecked, and thrown on shore," but Ganapatideva now grants "everything besides the fixed duty [*klipta-śulka*]" to those "who have incurred the great risk of a sea-voyage" — a renunciation of the ruler's claim to wrecked cargo beyond standard customs duty. The exact Sanskrit/Telugu term "abhaya-śāsana" was not found verbatim in the OCR text retrieved, only a later editorial paraphrase ("Ganapati's edict to mariners at Motupalli").

Cynthia Talbot (*Precolonial India in Practice*, 2001) confirms **Rudramadevi succeeded her father Ganapatideva on the throne, reigning 1263–1289** (per the book's own chronological table, p. ix), having adopted "a masculine persona" and reportedly led troops in battle (p. 135), though Talbot's retrieved text does **not** show her using a male regnal title in her own epigraphy (a claim seen only on an unverified popular website, not in Talbot). Talbot discusses the Motupalli charter (p. 73, p. 131, citing "EI 12.22") strictly as **Ganapatideva's** proclamation. A targeted search of the retrieved text for Rudramadevi/Rudramba/Prataparudra in connection with "Motupalli," "abhaya," "reissue" or "confirm" returned **no matches** — meaning **no inscription or passage located in this research shows Rudrama Devi reissuing or reconfirming the Motupalli charter.** This is an absence-of-evidence finding in the sources consulted, not proof no such reissue exists; roughly half of Talbot's book (the second half, including any appendices/index) was not searched.

## 4. The Trebizond loss: Polo family documents

**Access achieved:** Maffeo Polo's 1310 will read via Moule & Pelliot's 1938 Introduction (§II) and via direct OCR sampling of their appendix document itself (Internet Archive full text), with short (125-character-capped) verbatim extracts only — the closest to primary-document access achieved in this file. Marco Polo's 1324 will read only via two secondary transcriptions/translations (medievalslavery.org citing Yule 1871; historywalksvenice.com citing Yule-Cordier 1993), not the original parchment. **Orlandini's 1926 article itself was never accessed** — every claim attributed to Orlandini here is secondhand via Moule & Pelliot's summary.

**Marco Polo's own 1324 will contains no mention whatsoever of Trebizond, robbery, or lost/seized goods** — it is a conventional pious testament (manumission of his slave "Peter the Tartar," bequests to his wife Donata and daughters Fantina, Bellela, Moreta). The documentary root of the Trebizond story lies instead in **Maffeo Polo's will of 6 February 1309 (1310 Venetian style)**, which states — per Moule & Pelliot's quoted Latin — that the family suffered a loss ("dampnum") "inflicted... by the lord Comnenus of Trebizond" ("per... dominum comneno de trapesonda"), totalling "about four thousand hyperpera" ("in suma circha yperpera quatuor millia"), with roughly 1,000 lire received by Marco from the Doge and Commune of Venice credited "as [compensation] for part of the loss done to us" ("pro parte dampni nobis facti"). The will uses only the neutral term "dampnum" (loss/damage) — never "robbery," "seizure," or "confiscation" — and does not name which of the three Polo men specifically bore the loss or state its mechanism. Moule & Pelliot credit the identification of this passage's significance to **"Professor G. Orlandini's research"** — full citation: G. Orlandini, "Marco Polo e la sua Famiglia," *Archivio Veneto-Tridentino* IX (1926), pp. 1–68 — but Orlandini's own wording was not independently verified in this research; the chain "Orlandini (1926) → Moule & Pelliot (1938) → later Polo scholarship/popularizations" should be reported as the best-supported transmission route for the story, not as a confirmed primary-to-popular chain.

The dramatic popular versions (History.com's "4,000 Byzantine gold coins robbed"; Britannica's "robbed of most of their hard-won earnings") cite **no source at all** and go well beyond the will's neutral wording; whether they descend (even indirectly) from the Orlandini/Moule-Pelliot finding, or instead from Ramusio's 1559 preface (not confirmed to even mention Trebizond, in the portion sampled), is unresolved.

## 5. Abash (Abyssinia), 1288

**Access achieved:** Polo's text itself read via OCR/AI extraction of the Toyo Bunko Yule–Cordier scan (vol. 2, pp. 486–491); Taddesse Tamrat (1972) reached only as a **catalogue/review record** (SOAS eprints, a BSOAS review listing) — no book text; *Cambridge History of Africa* vol. 3 chapter reached only as a **paywalled abstract/landing page**; Trimingham (1952) and Pankhurst (1982) reached **only via a tertiary Wikipedia paraphrase**, never their own text.

Polo's chapter (Yule–Cordier, Book III, ch. XXXV, "Concerning the Province of Abash") tells the 1288 story: the Christian king of Abash sends a bishop to Jerusalem in his stead; the bishop is seized and forcibly circumcised at "Aden"; the king then marches on the "Kingdom of Aden," defeats three Saracen kings, and ravages the country. **Yule's own editorial note (already in the 1871/1875/1903 text, as far as could be determined) raises the Aden/Adel confusion decades before Trimingham or Pankhurst**: Note 3 to this chapter states the Sultan of Aden was "dominant over the territory... formerly called ADEL, between which and Aden confusion seems to have been made," also citing Marino Sanudo's map placing Aden on the Red Sea's western shore. **This note does not name Yagbe'a Ṣeyon or "Zeila" by name** — it is a toponymic observation, not a historical identification of the 1288 events with a specific reign.

Per a tertiary source (Wikipedia's article on Yagbe'u Seyon, read via AI-assisted summary, not the raw article), "a number of historians, including Trimingham and Pankhurst, identify the ruler with Yagbe'u Seyon," citing **Trimingham, *Islam in Ethiopia* (1952), pp. 69f.**, and **Pankhurst, *History of Ethiopian Towns* (1982), p. 55**. **Neither of those two books' own text was read in this research**, so the exact wording, and whether either author claims priority or cites Yule, Cerulli, or Conti Rossini, is unconfirmed. Taddesse Tamrat is cited elsewhere in that same Wikipedia article for other Yagbe'u Seyon points but **explicitly not for the Marco Polo passage** — consistent with, but not proof of, Taddesse Tamrat not discussing Polo's account at all. Whether the identification originates with Yule/Cordier (toponymic point only), Trimingham, Pankhurst, Cerulli, or Conti Rossini **cannot be resolved from the sources reached**; by publication date alone Trimingham (1952) precedes Pankhurst (1982) by three decades, but this is an inference from dates, not a priority claim either author makes.

## 6. Takashima shipwreck site

**Access achieved:** mostly secondary (news articles, a magazine feature, a book review); Sasaki 2015 reached only as a Project MUSE catalogue record (table of contents + publisher summary) plus a book review by Conlan (2017) — the book's own chapters were never read; the 2019 *Current Science* article reached only as an abstract/metadata page on a mirror site (i-scholar.in), with **author list, exact pages, and DOI unconfirmed** (the publisher's PDF was blocked by robots.txt); Kimura's one directly-read publication (*Crossroads* 11, 2015) is about other wrecks entirely and does not mention Takashima.

Excavation leadership: **KOSUWA** (Kyushu-Okinawa Society for Underwater Archaeology), founded/led by **Kenzo Hayashida**, ran Takashima/Imari Bay fieldwork from 1991, discovering a hull in October 2001 with iron-fastened planking, a large keel, watertight compartments, and a recovered fragment of a watertight bulkhead — read only via a 2003 *Archaeology* magazine article, not a KOSUWA report. **Ikeda Yoshifumi** (then University of the Ryūkyūs) led the 2011 discovery of a ~12 m keel section (estimated original length ≥20 m) and subsequent survey (from 2005) identifying at least two further wreck hulls with V-shaped/rounded hull bottoms and internal bulkheads, attributed to the Yuan "Southern Route Army" — read via Phys.org, Archaeology.org, and a nippon.com magazine feature, not Ikeda's own publications. Ikeda's storm-damage case rests on **anchor orientation** (four V-shaped anchors found pointing south, interpreted as dragged by a northward-driving typhoon) and wreck distribution, a positional/geomorphological argument rather than direct fracture analysis — and Ikeda himself complicates a pure storm narrative by citing a Goryeo record that 80% of the separate Eastern Route Army returned home safely, suggesting some losses may not be storm-caused.

**Kimura Jun's own Takashima-specific publication could not be located**; his one directly-read article (*Crossroads* 11, 2015, pp. 48–61) concerns other wrecks (Châu Tân, Jepara, etc.) and demonstrates his lug/joinery method for diagnosing a south-China shipbuilding tradition, but not applied to Takashima. **Sasaki's 2015 book** (*The Origins of the Lost Fleet of the Mongol Empire*, Texas A&M UP — **NEW**, post-2013) was accessed only as a catalogue record and a review; the publisher's own summary hedges that "no conclusive statements can be made about the vessels' origins," while reporting many timbers trace to smaller vessels built in the Yangtze River Valley, with Korean-built vessels a minor fraction. Conlan's 2017 review states more confidently that 1281 wrecks were "almost entirely made with southern Chinese lumber" following "southern Chinese boat-making practices" and explicitly notes Sasaki "did not fully analyze the largely intact ship discovered at Takashima in 2011" — a discrepancy between the hedged publisher summary and the reviewer's stronger paraphrase that could not be resolved without reading the book itself. Neither source mentions bulkheads at all. The **2019 Current Science article** (Ikeda, Sasaki & Kimura, per the brief — **NEW**) could only be confirmed at abstract level, via keywords (Imari Bay, Khublai Khan, Mongol, Takashima, "Quanzhou Ships") suggestive of a south-China framing, but with no verified author list, pagination, or DOI and no body text read.

## 7. Kayal and Qalhat ports

**Access achieved:** full text of both articles — Dayalan 2019 via a mirrored PDF (ancientportsantiques.com) cross-checked against DOI-resolved KoreaScience metadata; Rougeulle 2017 via a mirrored PDF read to ~100,000 of ~139,000 characters (the last ~quarter unread), with authorship confirmed via DOAJ metadata since the live OpenEdition page served only a bot-check screen.

**Correction to the brief:** Dayalan's article is titled **"Ancient Seaports on the Eastern Coast of India: The Hub of the Maritime Silk Route Network"** — not "...with Special Reference to Tamil Nadu" as the brief states. Author: **Duraiswamy Dayalan** (Archaeological Survey of India); *Acta Via Serica* 4(1) (June 2019), pp. 25–69, DOI 10.22679/avs.2019.4.1.025 — **NEW**. Material evidence cited: Robert Caldwell's 1861 coin hoard find at Kayal, including a coin "issued during the regime of Peter of Aragon (1276 CE)," alongside Chinese celadon/porcelain and Southeast Asian/Islamic wares — consistent with, but not explicitly tied by the article to, the 1280s–90s specifically.

**Correction to the brief:** the Qalhat article is authored solely by **Axelle Rougeulle** (confirmed via DOAJ), not jointly credited to CEFAS; CEFAS is the excavating/sponsoring institution, not a co-author, per DOAJ metadata (the live byline itself rendered as garbled OCR, "Aʚʇʎʎʇ Rʑʗʉʇʗʎʎʇ," in the mirrored PDF). The Friday mosque's dating to "c. 1290... to c. 1311" is explicitly flagged by Rougeulle as interpretive (ceramic/style-based), distinct from the more directly stratigraphic peripheral-quarter and kiln evidence.

## 8. Samudra-Pasai gravestone of Malik al-Ṣāliḥ (696 AH/1297)

**Access achieved:** no direct access at all to Lambourn's own articles (2003, 2008) or Guillot & Kalus's monograph (2008) — every claim attributed to them here is **secondhand**, via a 2022 *Islamic Quarterly* article (Ismail, Ramli & Ahmad Zakaria) and a 2010 *Studia Islamika* book review (van der Meij), both read in full text.

The stone was **first published by J.P. Moquette in 1913** — but this attribution itself is cited by the 2022 secondary source only via Lambourn's 2008 citation of Moquette, not from Moquette's own Dutch-language original, which was not accessed by anyone in this research chain. **Sayyid ʿAlawi bin Ṭāhir al-Ḥaddād** is credited as the first to *analyse* (genealogically/historically) the inscription's content, distinct from first reading the date. No source states in so many words "X first read the date as 696 AH, Ramaḍān 696/23 June–22 July 1297" independent of citing Lambourn's citation of Moquette — this exact attribution chain remains a gap.

There is **no simple consensus** on a Gujarati/Cambay origin for this specific stone. Moquette (per later citation) proposed Cambay-import origins for *other* Pasai royal stones (Malik Ibrahim's, Sultanah Nahrisyah's), comparing them to a dated 734 AH Cambay stone. **S.Q. Fatimi explicitly rejected** a Cambay origin for the al-Ṣāliḥ stone specifically, calling it "totally different from the Gujerati gravestones and their Malaysian prototypes." **Lambourn**, per the secondary paraphrase, is reported as concluding "there are no external production centres to link with the Batu Aceh in Pasai" — i.e. she too argues against a literal Cambay-carved origin for this stone's stylistic type — while separately, and more consequentially, arguing (again only via secondhand paraphrase of her 2008 article) that the **extant stone is stylistically a later (16th-century, "Type C") object**, likely carved roughly **two centuries after 1297** as a replacement/commemorative marker, not a contemporaneous 1297 artifact. Othman Mohd. Yatim (1985) is cited making a similar "later replacement" claim. The 2022 source itself **rejects** the later-replacement view on calligraphic grounds, arguing the stone is contemporary with 1297. Guillot & Kalus's monograph is reported, via the van der Meij review only, as holding that one broad stylistic category ("ogival") in the Pasai corpus *was* imported from Cambay — but the review does not specify whether the Malik al-Ṣāliḥ stone itself belongs to that imported category, so this cannot be confirmed either way for this specific stone.

**No source accessed — including the 2022 article that surveys the full scholarly debate on this exact stone — mentions Marco Polo, Ferlec, Basma, or Basman anywhere.** The epigraphy literature's textual corroboration runs through Ibn Baṭṭūṭa (1345/46) and the *Sejarah Melayu*, not Polo. Montana's and Perret's work could not be located at all.

## 9. Maabar: Nilakanta Sastri's Pandyan kings and Polo's "Sonder Bandi"

**Access achieved:** substantial but non-continuous OCR-extraction read of Nilakanta Sastri, *The Pandyan Kingdom* (1929), full PDF on archive.org, sampled at byte offsets around the Ch. XIII–XIV region, with a 125-character verbatim-quote cap per fetch; Karashima (ed.), *A Concise History of South India* (2014 — **NEW**) reached only as a **publisher catalogue table of contents**, no chapter text.

Nilakanta Sastri identifies the **eldest** of Polo's "five royal brothers"/"five crowned kings" as **Maravarman Kulasekhara (Kulasekhara I)**, reading Polo's "Ashar"/"Asciar" as "no doubt a corruption" of "Sekhar," corroborated by the *Mahavamsa*'s independent mention of "five brethren" with Kulasekhara as chief, and by Chinese envoy reports from 1280 onward also naming "the five brothers." **NS does not name the other four brothers individually** in the material retrieved. Separately, NS identifies Polo's **"Sonder Bandi Davar"** (and Wassaf's "Sunder Pandi") with a **Jatavarman Sundara Pandya** whose reign began c. 1276 and whose highest traced regnal year (17th, inscription "No. 594 of 1907") reaches **A.D. 1292–93** — matching Wassaf's report of this ruler's death in Hijra 692 (≈1292/93). This triangulation (Wassaf + Polo + epigraphy) is why NS treats the identification as reasonably secure for this specific ruler.

**Critical disambiguation, carried forward from the notes: NS's own book discusses at least two, and a modern tertiary synthesis (Wikipedia, citing NS) numbers at least three, distinct rulers all called "Jatavarman Sundara Pandya."** The ruler fitting Polo's 1292–93 "Sonder Bandi" is the one acceding c. 1276–77 (= "Sundara Pandyan II" in the later ordinal scheme) — **not** the earlier Sundara Pandyan I (d. 1268, too early to have met Polo), and **not** the later Sundara Pandyan III (Kulasekhara's son, acceded c. 1303, a decade too late, central to the Pandyan succession war of 1308–23). Confusing these would misdate the Maabar annotation by a decade or more in either direction; the annotated edition should always give the regnal dates (c. 1276 – c. 1292/93), not just the bare name.

**Whether Karashima's or Subbarayalu's post-1929 scholarship revises or confirms NS's identification could not be determined.** The only material reached for Karashima (ed.) 2014 is a publisher's table of contents (OUP India product page), which does not list any chapter titled "Pandyas"; the most plausibly relevant section (Karashima's own Ch. 5, "Thirteenth and Fourteenth Centuries...," §5.1 "The fall of the old states") was never read. No academic article, review, or excerpt addressing Karashima's or Subbarayalu's treatment of Polo's "five brothers"/"Sonder Bandi" was located in searches. This should be reported as a genuine, significant gap, not resolved either way.

## 10. Champa and Java/Malaiur

**Access achieved:** Hall, *A History of Early Southeast Asia* (2011), read as a large, substantially full OCR text (~1,026,000 characters) via a hosted PDF, sampled at several offsets but not exhaustively across the whole book. **Miksic, *Singapore and the Silk Road of the Sea* (2013 — NEW) could not be accessed in any form** — no full text, Google Books preview, or archive.org copy; only two articles that cite Miksic on unrelated points (city walls; a 14th-century Siamese attack on Temasek per Wang Dayuan) were found.

Hall's accessible chapters **do not discuss or date Marco Polo's visit to Champa** at all; Hall's only Polo material in this region concerns Java's wealth and "Marco Polo claimed to have visited Sumu-tu-la [Samudra] on his return voyage from China shortly thereafter in 1292." Hall never uses Polo's spelling "Malaiur"; he consistently writes **"Malāyu," which he explicitly identifies with Jambi** on southeastern Sumatra ("the Thais were ordered by the Chinese not to again wage war with Malāyu/Jambi"), treating **Tumasek/Singapore Island as a separate, distinct entity** in the same era rather than as the same place. Because Hall never engages Polo's own "Malaiur" spelling, it would overstate the evidence to cite Hall as taking an explicit position on "Polo's Malaiur" specifically — his position is on the Chinese-source toponym "Malāyu." **Whether Miksic reads Malaiur as Jambi or Temasek is wholly unresolved** — not found to agree or disagree with Hall, simply never accessed.

On the Java spice list, Hall takes an explicit, directly relevant position: he treats Polo's claim that Java was "producing... all... kinds of spices" as an **overstatement**, arguing the goods Polo saw (sandalwood from Timor, nutmeg from Banda/the Moluccas, pepper from Sumatran ports, sapanwood/diamonds from western Borneo) were obtained by the Javanese through intra-archipelago trade, with Java functioning as an **entrepôt/intermediary** trading its own rice surplus for eastern spices rather than as their primary producer. This is Hall's own explicit analytical point (pp. 226–227), not merely an inference drawn from raw data, and directly supports annotating Polo's Java passage with an entrepôt explanation. Whether Miksic makes the same, a different, or no argument on this point is again **unresolved**, not negative.

## 11. Chipangu's gold palace and Chūson-ji's Konjikidō

**Access achieved:** no peer-reviewed Polo-studies source found making this link. Sources read directly: tourism/travel journalism (Japan Times, Smithsonian Magazine, several tourism sites — explicitly non-scholarly); a genuine academic monograph (Sonia Favi, *Self Through the Other*, Ca' Foscari, ch.1, full chapter PDF read); a specialist numismatics conference paper (Takashi Ōkubo, ICOMON/Numismatic Association of Australia proceedings, 2000, full PDF read); a secondhand book-review characterization of John Larner's *Marco Polo and the Discovery of the World* (Japan Times review, not Larner's own text). Stephen Haw's *Marco Polo's China* and Larner's own book text were **not accessed**.

**No named, mainstream Polo scholar (Larner, Haw, Olschki, Jackson, Kinoshita) was found making an argued identification of Chipangu's gold-roofed palace with the Konjikidō.** The Konjikidō claim appears almost exclusively in tourism copy with no citation (e.g., Japan Times travel: "I could easily believe what he wrote when I saw the Konjikido... of the Chusonji Temple" — explicitly the author's personal impression, no scholarly source cited). A competing, equally unsourced popular comparison exists (Smithsonian Magazine juxtaposes Polo's gold palace with Kyoto's Kinkaku-ji, built 1397 — a century after Polo wrote), underscoring that popular treatments do not even agree among themselves on which "gold building" is meant. Via a secondhand book-review characterization, **John Larner** treats Polo's Japan material as "without exaggeration... nonsense" — "immensely fruitful nonsense" for its later influence on Columbus — **without tying it to any specific real building**. Sonia Favi's academic monograph interprets the gold-palace motif as a **literary/rhetorical convention** (Japan inheriting China's earlier "land of gold" mythic status in European discourse) rather than as a report of a real structure; her chapter text (as far as read) never mentions Hiraizumi, Chūson-ji, or the Konjikidō.

The one source found making an *argued* case for a Chūson-ji origin is **Takashi Ōkubo**, a numismatist at the Bank of Japan's Currency Museum, writing in a 2000 ICOMON/Melbourne numismatics-conference proceedings volume — a serious, named, institutionally affiliated source, but outside mainstream Marco Polo textual scholarship, and even this source **never uses the term "Konjikidō,"** referring only generically to "the Golden Building of the Chūson-ji temple." Ōkubo grounds his case in the documented historical reality of Ōshū Fujiwara gold mining (gold dust found from 749; the Song-dynasty *History of the Song* "Japan Accounts" reporting gold mined in "the Oshu region") and independent Japan–Song China trade, arguing "there is a good possibility that the rumor of the 'Gold Building of the Chusonji temple' came from China through the trade between Japan and China and that Marco Polo knew it." This should be presented in the annotated edition, if at all, as a clearly labelled minority/specialist historical argument — distinct from both the unsourced tourism claim and the more skeptical mainstream Polo-studies position that treats the passage as general legendary exaggeration without naming a building.

## Unresolved questions for further research

The following specific sub-questions were explicitly flagged as unresolved by the underlying research and must not be treated as settled in the annotated edition:

- Pelliot's own discursive text for **FERLEC** (II, p.725), **LAMBRI** (II, pp.761–762), **PENTAN** (II, pp.802–803), and **SEILAN** was never located or read; the digitization used tops out at an internal image label of "300" for vol. II, well short of these pages. A searchable PDF/EPUB or HathiTrust access is needed.
- The exact printed page numbers for Pelliot's **SEMENAT** (claimed II, p.830), **MAABAR**, and **SAGAMONI BORCAN** entries could not be independently confirmed; internal page labels in the Toyo Bunko digitization were inconsistent with each other.
- **Orlandini's 1926 article** ("Marco Polo e la sua Famiglia") was never read directly; its exact wording on the Trebizond loss, and whether it uses terms like "robbery" or "seizure," remains unknown.
- Whether **Ramusio's 1559 preface** mentions a Trebizond episode at all — and is thus a second, separate source of the popular "robbery" legend — was not established.
- **Taddesse Tamrat, *Church and State in Ethiopia*** and the ***Cambridge History of Africa* vol. 3** chapter on Ethiopia/the Red Sea/the Horn were never read beyond catalogue/abstract level; whether either discusses Polo's 1288 story at all is unknown.
- **Trimingham's and Pankhurst's own text** (on the Yagbe'a Ṣeyon identification) was never read; only a tertiary Wikipedia paraphrase was available. Priority between Trimingham, Pankhurst, Cerulli, and Conti Rossini for the Yagbe'a Ṣeyon identification (as distinct from the Aden/Adel toponymic point, which traces to Yule) is unresolved.
- **Kimura Jun's own Takashima-specific publication** could not be located at all.
- The **2019 *Current Science* article's** exact author list, volume/page numbers, and DOI remain unconfirmed (abstract-level mirror access only; publisher PDF blocked by robots.txt).
- **Sasaki's 2015 book's own chapters** (Timber Category Database, Species Analysis, Joinery Analysis, Conclusion) were never read; the tension between the publisher's hedged summary and Conlan's stronger review paraphrase is unresolved.
- **Lambourn's own articles** (2003, 2008) and **Guillot & Kalus's monograph** (2008) were never read directly; all characterizations rest on a single 2022 secondary source and one 2010 book review.
- **Who first read the 696 AH date** on the Malik al-Ṣāliḥ gravestone was not established in so many words independent of the Lambourn-cites-Moquette chain.
- **Montana's and Perret's** work on Samudra-Pasai epigraphy could not be located at all.
- **Karashima's 2014 chapter content** (and Subbarayalu's contributions to the same volume) on the Pandya chronology/Polo's "five brothers" was never read — catalogue table of contents only.
- Whether the ordinal numbering ("Sundara Pandyan I/II/III") used in this report's disambiguation is accepted by specialist current scholarship (Karashima, Subbarayalu, or epigraphic king-lists) was not confirmed; it derives from a tertiary Wikipedia synthesis.
- **Miksic's *Singapore and the Silk Road of the Sea* (2013)** could not be accessed in any form — not full text, preview, or archive.org copy — so its positions on Malaiur (Jambi vs. Temasek) and on the Java spice-entrepôt question are entirely unknown, not merely silent.
- Whether **Hall's book** discusses Polo's Champa visit or the exact spelling "Malaiur" anywhere in the roughly 40% of the book not directly queried remains an open possibility.
- **Stephen Haw's *Marco Polo's China*** and **John Larner's own text** on the Chipangu/gold-palace passage were never read directly (only a secondhand book-review characterization of Larner).
- **Japanese-language academic databases** (CiNii, J-STAGE) were never searched for "金色堂" + "マルコ・ポーロ" (Konjikidō + Marco Polo), a clear and specific next step for closing the Chipangu gap.
