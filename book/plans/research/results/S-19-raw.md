# Fourteen witnesses, verified and flagged honestly

Across five research-note files covering fourteen contemporary witnesses to Marco Polo, two patterns dominate: **public-domain quotable translations exist for almost every witness except the ones that matter most for the "new editions" task** (Zhou Daguan, Wang Dayuan, Hayton, and Rashid al-Din/Juvaini/Wassaf/Yuan shi all lack any public-domain English rendering), and **the single most load-bearing premise of the original brief — that a 1997 de Rachewiltz essay names Odoric and Montecorvino as corroborating witnesses to Polo's China sojourn — does not hold up**. That essay was never located; a related, freely accessible 2002 de Rachewiltz piece was read in full, and it uses Odoric, Montecorvino, Ibn Battuta, Carpini and Rubruck only as parallel cases of *omission* (to defang Frances Wood's silence-based skepticism), while naming **Petrus de Abano** and **Rashid al-Din** as the essay's actual corroborating witnesses. This correction is carried through every relevant subsection below rather than smoothed over. Elsewhere, real public-domain gems surfaced: Samuel Lee's 1829 abridged Ibn Battuta, Hirth & Rockhill's 1911 *Chau Ju-kua*, the Yule–Cordier 1913 *Cathay and the Way Thither* volumes (same editorial lineage as the user's base Polo text), Eusebius Renaudot's 1733 *Ancient Accounts of India and China*, and Robert Bedrosian's explicitly public-domain Hayton translation. Several witnesses — the Secret History of the Mongols, Rashid al-Din, Juvaini, Wassaf, and the Yuan shi — have **no public-domain English translation at all**, a finding that should be stated plainly in the retelling's apparatus rather than papered over.

No scholar was found explicitly pairing Rubruck's silver-tree description or his kumiss passage against Polo's silence or brevity on the same topics; this is flagged in the notes as an "intuitively obvious but uncited" comparison, and the report preserves that honesty rather than inventing an attribution.

**Prose.** The most consequential correction in this group concerns de Rachewiltz. The original brief assumed his 1997 *Zentralasiatische Studien* essay makes an explicit argument that Odoric's and Montecorvino's independent travel accounts corroborate Marco Polo's presence in China. That 1997 essay was **never located** in either research pass (not at ANU's repository, not via general web search). What was read in full is a related but distinct 2002 ANU working paper by the same author, and on direct reading **it does not make that argument**: de Rachewiltz cites Odoric (and Ibn Battuta) only to show that other verifiably real travelers share Polo's silences on topics like Chinese script and tea — normalizing Polo's omissions rather than using Odoric as positive proof. The essay's actual named corroborating witnesses are Petrus de Abano (astronomical observations recorded in his 1303 *Conciliator*) and Rashid al-Din (confirming the arrival of the Mongol envoy Khoja at Abhar, supporting Polo's account of escorting princess Kököchin). This is reported here as a correction, not smoothed into a confirmation, per the hard rules. Separately, the notes record a genuine, independently valuable finding: Yule/Cordier's own 1913 footnote to Odoric's Ch. XLVI explicitly flags that Polo omits both long fingernails and foot-binding — a verified, citable point of divergence that does not depend on the unresolved de Rachewiltz question at all.
