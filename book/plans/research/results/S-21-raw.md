# Marco Polo's natural history, checked against modern scholarship

Of the 59 animal, plant, mineral, and marvel claims Marco Polo makes (Yule-Cordier 1903/1920 text), a clear majority hold up as genuine eyewitness or merchant-market reporting once translated into modern taxonomy and trade history — the Marco Polo sheep (*Ovis ammon polii*, literally named for him), the yak, musk deer, coal ("black stones"), Baku's petroleum seeps, Fansur camphor, Gulf of Mannar pearls, ambergris, and couvade all check out against independent modern sources. A second, nearly as large group are cases of **misidentification or entrepôt confusion**: Polo correctly names a real commodity or animal but gets its species, point of origin, or category wrong — his "unicorn" is a rhinoceros, his striped "lions" are tigers, and his Java spice list (nutmeg, cloves) describes goods sold at a market rather than grown there. A third group are **ancient legends** layered onto real geography — the rukh, the dog-headed men of the Andamans, the diamond-retrieving eagles, and the Male and Female Islands all have pre-Polo pedigrees in classical or Arabic marvel literature, though the research notes could not always pin down a single earliest textual source and say so explicitly. Finally, a meaningful number of items — roughly a fifth — are genuine **research gaps**: no peer-reviewed or critical-edition source specifically engaging Polo's passage was located within the research budget, and these are flagged "not found" rather than papered over. Four scholarly disagreements recur across the notes and are substantial enough to call out in prose: the chinar/cypress/plane-tree/Ferula identity of the "Dry Tree," the Kerman-versus-Nishapur turquoise-source question, the Epiphanius-versus-Sindbad genealogy of the diamond-valley legend, and the coastal-China-versus-Indian-Ocean origin of Yunnan's cowrie currency. Several items rest on sources published after 2013 and are marked **NEW** below, per the brief's instruction; none of these NEW sources were confirmed to engage with Polo's text specifically — they establish the underlying zoological/historical fact, not a Polo-specific reassessment.

Method note carried through from the research notes: nothing below invents a page number, quotation, DOI, or URL. Where the notes say a citation's full bibliographic detail (author, year, DOI) could not be independently confirmed, that is stated as "DOI/URL not found" or "not found" rather than guessed. Where a source's full text was not fetched and only a summary, review, or secondary account was consulted, that is stated plainly in the finding or sources cell.

## How to read the table

Each row gives: a running number (duplicated across sections for claims tagged to more than one book section, e.g. musk deer at I-11 and II-07); the claim; a finding that folds in the descriptive verdict (accurate / partly accurate / misunderstanding / hearsay / ancient legend, naming the legend's earliest attested source where the notes identify one, or saying the notes could not confirm one); the required confirmed/corrected/disputed/not found verdict; sources; confidence; and the affected section IDs. "Confirmed" = the notes report the claim as accurate against modern identification. "Corrected" = Polo's label or framing needed modern correction (misidentification, entrepôt confusion, mixed accuracy) but the underlying referent is real and now identified. "Disputed" = genuine, named scholarly disagreement about identification, genealogy, or earliest source. "Not found" = the notes could not locate a source sufficient to confirm, deny, or meaningfully assess the claim.
