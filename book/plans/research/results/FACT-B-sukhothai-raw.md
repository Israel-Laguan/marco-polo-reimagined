**Figure 1:** Synthesis of epigraphic, historical, and geographical findings concerning Inscription No. 1 and Locac.

Sukhothai Inscription No. 1 was discovered in 1833 by Prince Mongkut (later King Rama IV) while on pilgrimage in Sukhothai and moved to Bangkok (Bauer, 2020). The text is inscribed across four faces and traditionally associated with 1292 CE (Bauer, 2020; Wyatt, 2001). Skeptics such as Michael Vickery and Piriya Krairiksh suggested the inscription was an erudite 19th-century creation by Rama IV or his circle (Wright, 1995; Aranyanak, 1990). Conversely, defenders ("Angels" in William J. Gedney's phrasing) including Prasert na Nagara, David Wyatt, and Barend Jan Terwiel argued that its cultural content, lexicon, and stone weathering patterns support a pre-modern, late 13th- or 14th-century origin (Wright, 1995; Aranyanak, 1990; Jay, 1993; Yoshimura, 2020). Intermediate arguments suggest the stone may represent a later copy of a late 13th-century document rather than a modern forgery (Jay, 1993).

Polo's kingdom of Locac is consistently identified by modern geographical scholarship with Lopburi/Lavo (rendered as Luohu or Lohuk in Yuan annals and Lộ Hạc in Vietnamese chronicles), rather than the inland northern polity of Sukhothai (King, 2018). While the inscription has been examined alongside Chinese tribute missions to illustrate autonomous Tai polities seeking Yuan recognition (Jay, 1993), conflating Locac with Sukhothai is obsolete (King, 2018; Jack-Hinton, 1964). Calling the inscription "almost coeval" is chronologically defendable if the traditional 1292 date is accepted (Bauer, 2020; Jay, 1993), but the endnote should specify that Locac designates southern/central Lopburi (Luohu) rather than Sukhothai.

**Explanatory notes on disagreements**

On authenticity (row 2): Vickery and Piriya Krairiksh (late 1980s–1991) mounted the main challenge on epigraphic, linguistic, and (for Piriya) art-historical and motivational grounds, arguing the stele cannot be a genuine 13th-century product and (in Piriya’s strongest form) is a Mongkut-era composition. Replies in the same 1991 volume and later work (Terwiel, defenders of Griswold–Prasert readings, physical analyses) emphasized consistency with other early Thai evidence, the absence of decisive proof of forgery, and the 1990 microscopy results supporting antiquity. The debate cooled after the early 1990s; most specialists now treat the core text as usable 13th-century evidence while remaining alert to possible later additions or idealization. The inscription’s UNESCO Memory of the World status reflects its cultural importance more than settled technical consensus.

On Locac identifications (row 4): Yule–Cordier’s note is the place where the inscription enters the Locac discussion (chronological coincidence + Sukhothai’s claimed southern reach). Later scholarship has largely preferred Chinese Luohu = Lavo/Lopburi (phonetically and documentarily stronger for the Yuan period) or kept the question open; the inscription is not routinely invoked as decisive.

On Xian vs. Sukhothai (row 5): Older scholarship often equated Xian with Sukhothai. Some modern readings (Yamamoto and others using Da-de Nanhai zhi) treat Xian as a distinct coastal or lower-basin polity that controlled or interacted with upstream Sukhothai (Sugutai). Both Xian and Luohu appear in Yuan records of the 1280s–1290s, supplying firm coeval Chinese documentation regardless of exact mapping.
