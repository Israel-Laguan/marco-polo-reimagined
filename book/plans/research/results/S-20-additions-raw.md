## Columbus and Cartographic Evidence

Shawcross identifies the surviving volume as **Biblioteca Capitular y Colombina 10-3-2**, formerly **V-117-13**, and reports Hernando’s earlier registration numbers **1069** and **2741** (Shawcross, 2023). Her abstract attributes the marginalia to **Columbus and members of his immediate circle**, which rules out casually calling every annotation autograph (Shawcross, 2023).

Her most useful bibliographic warning is that the English volume titled *The Book of Marco Polo: Copy with Annotations by Christopher Columbus* **does not contain the postils**. She instead directs readers to the facsimile and Gil’s edition (Shawcross, 2023). The supplied passages do not expose individual postil transcriptions, counts, hand assignments, or the fol. 66v/1499/1501 argument. Those details remain unsuitable for printing.

Hellinga’s abstract links Day’s shipment with the **only Latin Polo edition printed before 1497**, supplying an edition-level argument worth following (Hellinga, 2014). It does not supply the full argument identifying the particular surviving exemplar. Coneys states that this copy entered Columbus’s possession only in **1497**, but the supplied note does not explain its evidentiary basis (Coneys, 2018).

Costa acknowledges scholarly denial of the Toscanelli correspondence’s authenticity and discusses the letter dated **25 June 1474** (De Sousa Costa, 1990). His narrower documentary contribution is that Fernando Martins had returned to Portugal before 1474 and remained in Lisbon until 1483; this supports contextual plausibility, not automatic authentication of every transmitted letter (De Sousa Costa, 1990).

For the Catalan Atlas, **Jung interprets Taprobana as combining information about Ceylon, Sumatra, Japan, and Korea**. That does not establish either a separately identifiable Japan island or the absence of a Chipangu label (Jung & Jung, 2015). Jung also names multiple travel sources, preventing exclusive attribution of the atlas’s Asian content to Polo (Jung & Jung, 2015).

No supplied passage provides a Fra Mauro legend locator, resolves Z/Ghisi map-source alternatives, or gives Ravenstein’s Cipangu transcription page. Davies’s abstract establishes that she analyzes Fra Mauro’s responses to eastern accounts, but cannot supply the missing legend (Davies, 2012).
