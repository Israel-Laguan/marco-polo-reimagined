## 1. Chinese, Mongolian and Iranian Reception

**Yang Zhijiu.** Frances Wood's chapter (abstract only, level 5) relays that Yang Zhijiu argued the claim that Marco Polo governed Yangzhou rested on a misreading in one text, and that Yang also noted an American academic's detailed argument that Polo may have reached only Peking and written about China from what he heard there (Wood, 2018). Wood's text does not supply Yang's publication titles or dates; those remain unlocated in the corpus (level 6).

**Zhang Xinglang and Feng Chengjun.** No paper in the corpus supplies the dates, titles, or translation bases for either scholar. Searches returned no indexed passage naming their editions. This remains unresolved (level 6).

**Mongolian Ramusio translation.** Bat-Uchral and Pavlov V.I.'s 2023 article in *Орчуулгазүй* (DOI: 10.22353/ts20230108) analyses translation strategies in a Mongolian rendering of the *Travels*, combining direct and indirect strategies — chiefly imitation, literal translation, equivalence, and adaptation — without using supplementation, expansion, or foreign-word strategies (Bat-Uchral & V.I, 2023). The article is inspected at level 3 (full text read). It does not identify itself as the 2019 Ramusio translation reported by Ragagnin and Simion, nor does it name a 2024 Ca' Foscari expanded edition; the relationship between these editions remains unconfirmed (level 6). The 2020 article by Ragagnin and Simion citing their 2019 translation is already established context.

**Iranian reception.** No paper in the corpus supplies a concrete Iranian reception example (a named Persian translation, edition, or dated scholarly account). This remains unresolved (level 6).

**Chinese diplomacy or textbook.** Marinelli's 2010 article (*Journal of Modern Italian Studies* 15(4): 491–501, abstract only, level 5) reports that Marco Polo and Matteo Ricci have been used as "epitomes of the rhetoric trope of a long-standing friendship between Italy and China" in diplomatic and commercial contexts, and that their experiences "have become recurrent legends" invoked in academic, diplomatic, and commercial negotiations (Marinelli, 2010). This is a characterisation of the diplomatic use of the Polo legend, not a dated Polo-specific textbook, speech, or exhibit. No such specific item was located (level 6).

## 2. Literature and Screen

**O'Neill's *Marco Millions*: publication and premiere.** Zhamanova's 2025 article (*Literature of the Americas*, no. 19, pp. 204–223, level 3) identifies the play as O'Neill's satire *Marco Millions* (1927) and reports that Rouben Mamoulian directed the Theatre Guild production in New York in 1928, with a Liberty Theatre production in 1930 (Жаманова, 2025). The article also notes that in Russia the play was translated and published but never staged (Жаманова, 2025). O'Neill's own preface (quoted in the article in Russian, level 4 relay) describes the play as an attempt to render "poetic justice" to Marco Polo, "unjustly branded a liar" (Жаманова, 2025). The article cites O'Neill's *Complete Plays 1920–1931* (Library of America, 1988) as its biographical source (Жаманова, 2025). **Safe wording:** *Marco Millions* was written in 1927 and premiered in New York in 1928 (Theatre Guild, dir. Mamoulian); a 1930 Liberty Theatre production followed. The 1927 date is the composition/publication date relayed through Zhamanova; the premiere date 1928 is stated in the same source.

**Calvino's *Invisible Cities*: publication date.** Moosavinia and Ahmadzadeh's 2023 article (*Neohelicon*, DOI: 10.1007/s11059-023-00687-9, abstract only, level 5) treats *Invisible Cities* as a postmodern reconstruction of the *Travels* but does not supply a publication date (Moosavinia & Ahmadzadeh, 2023). No paper in the corpus states the publication year of *Invisible Cities* independently. This remains unresolved from the corpus (level 6); the 1972 Italian publication date is widely known but not grounded in any inspected passage here.

**Coleridge, Purchas, and Polo.** No paper in the corpus supplies an inspected passage tracing the Coleridge–Purchas–Polo chain. The prior session established that Lima quotes a 1960 Calvino letter invoking Coleridge's method; that relay is not independent evidence of what Coleridge read. This remains unresolved (level 6).

**Netflix series season dates.** No paper in the corpus supplies the Netflix *Marco Polo* season dates. This remains unresolved (level 6).

**Montaldo series: Italian–Chinese co-production.** A claim-validation step (level 5, abstract only) found that a paper by Francesco Di Chiara identifies Giuliano Montaldo's *Marco Polo* as the first co-production between the Italian and Chinese film industries. The abstract is not fully inspected; the paper is not among the 56 ranked papers and its bibliographic details were not retrieved. **Safe wording:** Di Chiara (paper not fully inspected, level 5) is reported to identify the Montaldo series as the first Italian–Chinese film co-production; this finding is a snippet lead, not a read passage, and requires independent verification. Boșca-Mălin's 2026 article (*Studia Universitatis Babeș-Bolyai Philologia*, DOI: 10.24193/subbphilo.2026.2.04, level 3) surveys all cinematographic transpositions of the Polo story but does not itself confirm the co-production claim or supply country-by-country broadcast dates (Bosca-Malin, 2026).

**Venice airport naming.** Lando's 2014 article (*Laboratoire italien*, DOI: 10.4000/laboratoireitalien.836, level 3) states that at the beginning of the 1960s the requirements of new large aircraft forced construction of the new "aeroporto Marco Polo" at the edge of the lagoon (Lando, 2014). This is the earliest located attributed source in the corpus placing the naming in the early 1960s, but it does not supply a precise inauguration date or formal naming decree. **Safe wording:** Lando (2014) places the construction and naming of the Marco Polo airport at the lagoon's edge in the early 1960s; a precise institutional naming date was not located in the corpus.

**Recent significant novel beyond Bukal.** No paper in the corpus supplies an evidence-backed example of another significant recent novel beyond Bukal's 2006 *Milion*. Peruško's 2020 article (*Književna smotra*, abstract only, level 5) discusses Bukal's work as "biofiction" and notes its Korčula setting (Peruško, 2020), but names no other recent novel. This remains unresolved (level 6).

## 3. Attributions and Myths

**Contemporary portrait versus later images.** No paper in the corpus supplies evidence for an authentic contemporary portrait of Marco Polo. Kinoshita's 2016 article (*The Medieval Globe*, DOI: 10.17302/tmg.2-1.6, level 3) discusses three portraits of Polo's contemporaries but does not address a portrait of Polo himself (Kinoshita, 2016). Wittkower's chapter (level 5) notes that many Polo manuscripts are part of collectanea concerned with the East and that visual traditions of Eastern marvels shaped manuscript illustration, but does not identify a contemporary portrait (Wittkower, 2017). This remains unresolved (level 6).

**Pool game name and origin.** Thornton's 2015 chapter (*SensePublishers*, DOI: 10.1007/978-94-6300-040-6_4, abstract only, level 5) states that the game is "believed to have taken its name from the great Venetian explorer" and that its supposed origins are "said to be grounded in the travels of the late twelfth century explorer who reportedly did not always know where he was going" (Thornton, 2015). No earlier attestation or independent origin story is supplied; the passage is a snippet only. **Safe wording:** The earliest located attributed account in the corpus (Thornton 2015, abstract only) describes the naming as a belief, not a documented historical connection; no independent origin story or earlier attestation was located.

**Korčula birthplace attribution.** Peruško's 2020 article (abstract only, level 5) discusses Bukal's novel, which is set partly in Korčula and treats it as Polo's childhood home within the fiction (Peruško, 2020). The abstract does not supply the earliest located Korčula birthplace attribution or cite Pasini 1847 directly. The prior session established that a supplied report relays Peruško's discussion of Pasini 1847 on possible Dalmatian *family* origin; that relay is level 4 and does not establish Marco's birthplace. No earlier located attribution was found in the corpus (level 6).

**Milione etymologies.** Garkavets's 2017 article (*Golden Horde Review*, DOI: 10.22378/2313-6197.2017-5-3.522-565, level 5 abstract) reports that the generic nickname *Million* received by the Polos has been confirmed as connected to the estate of the Venetian family Vilione/Milione (Garkavets, 2017). Cruse's 2022 introduction (*Digital Philology*, DOI: 10.1353/dph.2022.0014, level 3) notes that the title *Il Milione* "appears to derive from a nickname for the Polo family but that is traditionally said to refer to the text's descriptions of the great wealth of Kubilai Khan" (Cruse, 2022). Simion's 2018 article (*Quaderni veneti*, DOI: 10.30687/qv/1724-188x/2017/02/001, level 3) notes that Benedetto's etymological proposal was based on the Tommaseo-Bellini dictionary entry for *pilorcio* (scraps of fur) (Simion, 2018). **Safe wording:** At least three competing etymologies are reported in the corpus: (a) a family estate name Vilione/Milione (Garkavets, relaying a hypothesis); (b) a reference to Kublai Khan's great wealth (traditional, reported by Cruse); (c) Benedetto's proposal connecting it to *pilorcio* (fur scraps), based on Tommaseo-Bellini (reported by Simion). These are competing scholarly proposals, not a settled question.

**Deathbed saying earlier than Acqui.** No paper in the corpus supplies an account of the deathbed saying earlier than Jacopo d'Acqui. This remains unresolved (level 6).

**"Million lies" attribution.** No paper in the corpus supplies the earliest located "million lies" attribution. This remains unresolved (level 6).

## 5. Maps and Columbus

**Columbus's surviving copy: postils, hands, and dating.** Shawcross's 2023 article (*The Medieval History Journal*, DOI: 10.1177/09719458231213480, level 3, full text read) is the key source here. The article states that the surviving exemplar is an incunable of Pipino's Latin translation (*De consuetudinibus et conditionibus orientalium regionum*) and that its margins contain "copious notes and drawings attributable to Columbus himself and other members of his immediate circle" (Shawcross, 2023). Columbus's notes consist of "brief references either to single objects or to lists of multiple objects associated with specific places," with particular emphasis on mineral deposits, gold, pearls, and spices (Shawcross, 2023).

On dating: Shawcross states at note 12 (p. 380) that "there is internal evidence indicating that Columbus was able to consult the specific book at some point after the summer of 1499 or autumn 1501" (Shawcross, 2023). The note specifies that one datable annotation by Columbus in the Seville exemplar, at folio 66v, refers to Vasco da Gama's arrival at Calicut on the Malabar Coast (Shawcross, 2023). Shawcross explains that this note "cannot antedate the summer of 1499, which was when da Gama reached the Azores and then Lisbon," and that it "most likely postdates the official decision in the autumn of 1500 that Columbus's possessions should be returned to him following the conclusion of his period of imprisonment" (Shawcross, 2023). **Safe wording:** The fol. 66v annotation referencing da Gama's Calicut arrival establishes that Columbus consulted this specific copy no earlier than summer 1499; Shawcross argues it most likely postdates autumn 1500. The two alternative dates ("after summer 1499 or autumn 1501") reflect the lower and upper bounds of this single annotation's dating range, not a window for all annotations. The current classmark of the incunable is 119-7-2; the retired classmark is GG-179-4; Hernando Columbus registered it as 3358 and then 11268 (Shawcross, 2023).

**Day's book and the surviving copy.** Quinn's 1967 article (*Geographical Journal*, DOI: 10.2307/1793272, abstract only, level 5) confirms that Vigneras demonstrated the "Almirante Mayor" addressed in Day's letter was almost certainly Columbus, and that Ruddock identified John Day of Bristol with Hugh Say of London (Quinn, 1967). Shawcross (level 3) cites Quinn and Morison on this point at note 10 (p. 380) (Shawcross, 2023). Neither source in the corpus establishes that the book Day sent was the surviving Seville exemplar; that identification remains disputed (level 6 for the argument connecting Day's book to the surviving copy).

**Toscanelli correspondence.** Sousa Costa's 1990 article (*Antonianum*, abstract only, level 5) argues for the authenticity of Toscanelli's 25 June 1474 letter to the Canon of Lisbon, Fernando Martins, and notes that "some historians" deny the authenticity of the correspondence between Toscanelli and Columbus (De Sousa Costa, 1990). **Safe wording:** The authenticity of the Toscanelli–Columbus correspondence is disputed; Sousa Costa (1990, abstract only) defends it, while unnamed historians deny it. No inspected passage in the corpus resolves the dispute.

**Catalan Atlas: Chipangu and Japan-like island.** Lišèák's 2018 article (*Proceedings of the ICA*, DOI: 10.5194/ica-proc-1-69-2018, level 3, full text read) states that the Catalan Atlas's main sources for the eastern parts of the world were the travelogues of Marco Polo, John Mandeville, and Odoric of Pordenone (Lišèák, 2018). It notes that Odoric of Pordenone was "especially" important for China, as "there are some cities missing in Marco Polo's description" (Lišèák, 2018). The article does not address the presence or absence of a Chipangu label or a Japan-like island specifically. Nunn's 1937 article (*Imago Mundi*, DOI: 10.1080/03085693708591832, abstract only, level 5) notes that the Behaim globe combined Ptolemy's geography with Marco Polo's, extending the mainland of Asia to the 240th degree of longitude (Nunn, 1937). Neither source distinguishes an absent Chipangu label from the absence of a Japan-like island on the Catalan Atlas. This distinction remains unresolved in the corpus (level 6).

**Fra Mauro inscriptions FM *2442 and *2306.** No paper in the corpus supplies an inspected transcription of these specific inscriptions. The prior session established that Burgio and Simion's reconstruction favours the Ghisi manuscript; that finding is not repeated here. The inscriptions themselves remain at level 6.

**Ravenstein's Behaim Cipangu transcription.** No paper in the corpus supplies Ravenstein's transcription or page. This remains unresolved (level 6).

## 6. Remaining Records

**BnF fr. 1116 and fr. 2810.** Cruse's 2015 article (*Narrative Culture*, DOI: 10.13110/narrcult.2.2.0171, abstract only, level 5) identifies Paris, BnF, fr. 1116 as one of the oldest surviving Polo manuscripts, made between 1310 and 1320, a professional unillustrated copy whose patron, production circumstances, relationship to Polo, and arrival in France are unknown (Cruse, 2015). Cruse's 2017 article (*Speculum*, DOI: 10.1086/694170, level 3) describes fr. 1116 as "a Franco-Italian copy seemingly produced 1310–20 in Venice that is close to the progenitor, or progenitors, of many later copies" (Cruse, 2017). Andreose's 2015 article (level 5, abstract) argues that fr. 1116 does not display strong similarities to the hybrid language traditionally called "Franco-Italian" or "Franco-Venetan," and connects it linguistically to Old French manuscripts copied by Pisan scribes imprisoned in Genoa after the battle of Meloria (1284) (Andreose, 2015). Haw's 2020 article (*Asiatische Studien*, DOI: 10.1515/asia-2020-0010, level 3) cites BNF fr. 1116 at folio 60 recto in a specific textual note (Haw, 2020). The Gallica identifier for fr. 2810 was not resolved in the corpus (level 6).

**F manuscript dating (1310–20 vs. 1320–30).** Cruse (2017, level 3) gives "1310–20" for fr. 1116 (Cruse, 2017); Cruse (2015, level 5) gives the same range (Cruse, 2015). The Foligno watermark argument for a 1320–30 dating was not located in the corpus (level 6).

**Bern manuscript with Cepoy's note.** No paper in the corpus identifies the Bern manuscript bearing Cepoy's note. This remains unresolved (level 6).

**Dom Pedro's Polo-book gift.** Brown's chapter (*Routledge*, DOI: 10.4324/9780429055560-19, abstract only, level 5) states that "Henry the Navigator's brother Pedro had visited Italy in 1428, acquiring in Venice a copy of Marco Polo's *Travels to China and the East*" (Brown, 2020). This is a secondary account (level 5) reporting the gift as a Venice acquisition in 1428; it does not identify the specific copy or its current location, and does not connect it to any map story. **Safe wording:** Brown (2020, abstract only) reports that Dom Pedro acquired a copy of the *Travels* in Venice in 1428; the specific copy and its subsequent history are not identified in the corpus.

**Jesuit comparison of Polo's silences.** Woodhouse et al.'s 2001 review (*Italian Studies*, DOI: 10.1179/its.2001.56.1.160, level 3) relays Larner's discussion of why Polo did not mention the Great Wall, the binding of women's feet, or the practice of drinking tea, and notes that Larner "brilliantly answered" these objections (Woodhouse et al., 2001). This is a relay of Larner's argument, not a Jesuit passage. Olshin's 2007 article (*Terrae Incognitae*, DOI: 10.1179/008228807790802621, abstract only, level 5) notes that "the narrative seems to leave out key elements of Chinese culture such as the drinking of tea" (Olshin, 2007). Zhang Longxi's 2020 chapter (abstract only, level 5) places Marco Polo in the thirteenth century as "an early stage of East-West encounter" and the Jesuits in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries as the next major phase, but does not supply a Jesuit passage comparing Polo's silences (Longxi, 2020). **Safe wording:** No Jesuit passage specifically comparing Polo's silences on tea, foot-binding, or the Wall was located in the corpus; the silences are discussed by Larner (relayed by Woodhouse 2001) and noted by Olshin (2007), but neither is a Jesuit source.

**Census reconciliation (145 vs. 146).** Cruse's 2015 article (abstract only, level 5) gives 135 manuscripts produced before 1530 (Cruse, 2015); Cruse's 2017 article (abstract only, level 5) gives 141 manuscripts and fragments (Cruse, 2017); Jasim's 2023 article (level 3) gives "about one hundred and fifty copies" (Jasim, 2023). None of these resolves the 145/146 discrepancy between the 2025 census and the 2026 publisher's report. The scope difference (145 entries vs. 146 surveyed) remains unreconciled in the corpus (level 6).
