## **What the Corpus Can and Cannot Support**

The single Polo-specific paper is a review of three new editions of Polo's travels, including the 1903 third edition of Yule's translation by Henri Cordier, which the author had not seen (Houtum-Schindler, 1909). It establishes a methodological point directly relevant to the project: the French Geographical Society text (Paris, 1824, from BnF MS. 1116) is treated as an original text and is "much superior to any form of the work previously published" as regards proper names, and is classified following Yule as the Geographic Text (GT) (Houtum-Schindler, 1909). The GT reproduces the old manuscript faithfully, without correction of style or orthography (Houtum-Schindler, 1909), and Polo's Oriental spellings in it are "in the main as correct as Italian lips and orthography will admit" (Houtum-Schindler, 1909). This matters for any name-based identification (Pem, Tenduc, Tonocain): the version consulted determines the spelling being identified.

On the Arbre Sol / Arbre Sec, the paper gives the actual GT and GL readings, which the project can use to separate what Polo's text says from later interpretation. The GT, chap. 33 reads "Tunocan qui est pres a l'abre [sic] seul," and chap. 40 has "une grandisime plaigne en lequel est l'arbre seul que les cristiens appellent l'arbre seche" (Houtum-Schindler, 1909). The Latin GL, cap. xxvii, has "Ibi est arbor sola quam christiani vocant arborem siccam" (Houtum-Schindler, 1909). The paper's title asserts the tree is "not 'Sun-Tree', but Cypress of Zoroaster," but the supplied excerpts do not state the botanical argument or the cypress identification itself; that claim cannot be cited from this corpus beyond the title.

## **Baghdad: What the Corpus Actually Establishes**

The death of al-Mustaʿṣim is the one topic with real coverage, and it is a case study in the distinction the project asks for between text, proposal, and fact. Neggaz frames the problem as a set of contradictory medieval accounts—starvation, molten gold poured down the throat, execution by Hülegü's own hands or by a Georgian prince, rolling in a carpet, hanging, strangling—and argues these are "replete with symbolism" targeting specific audiences rather than records of fact (Neggaz, 2020). Strange, working from the Vatican MS. of Ibn-al-Furāt, states plainly that "concerning the manner of his death the accounts differ" and that no eye-witness narrative survives (Strange, 1900). The sack itself is described as a forty-day reign of terror in which the population was massacred except the Christians, with the caliph and his sons put to death (De Somogyi, 1933). Somogyi dates the destruction to mid-Muharram 656/January 1258 (De Somogyi, 1933); Shay gives 10 February 1258 for the Mongol arrival and adds the destruction of the canal and irrigation system as the lasting economic blow (Shay, 2014). The Twilight chapter frames the conquest as the acceleration of Abbasid collapse under al-Musta'sim, with a shadow caliphate surviving in Mamluk Egypt until 1517 (, 2021). Hassan's study, as reviewed, treats 1258 as the start of a three-and-a-half-year caliphal vacancy and reads the lamentation literature as evidence of symbolic loss (Young, 2019).

For the project's purposes, the corpus supports a note that Polo's Baghdad material belongs to a tradition of *conflicting* death accounts, not a settled narrative, and that the treasury/starvation motif is one strand among several whose historicity Neggaz explicitly declines to adjudicate (Neggaz, 2020). The corpus does **not** contain the Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī attribution, Gregory of Akner, Hayton, or Polo's own Baghdad text, so the requested trace across those witnesses cannot be built from these papers.

## **Substantive Disagreements and Unresolved Questions**

The only scholarly disagreement the corpus documents in detail is over the caliph's death: Neggaz's literary-critical reading treats the variant accounts as audience-directed commentary on ʿAbbāsid rule (Neggaz, 2020), while Strange's edition presents the variants as a straightforward problem of missing eye-witness testimony (Strange, 1900). Hassan's work, as summarized, pushes back on Lewis and Crone for reading the sources as indifferent to the caliphate's fall (Young, 2019). On Polo's geography, the corpus records only that the 1903 Cordier edition went unseen by one contemporary reviewer (Houtum-Schindler, 1909) and that earlier popular editions perpetuated errors long corrected in Yule's second edition (Houtum-Schindler, 1909)—a caution against relying on derivative retellings for names.

Unresolved and requiring sources outside this corpus: K-103 coordinates and datum; the outward-vs-return Hormuz reconstruction; Tun/Qāyen; Alam and Piemontese; Keriya/Uzun-tatïr; Tenduc and Sichintingiu; the Muqaṭṭam parallel; the Nizari sources and Rokn al-Din chronology; Bām-i Duniyā attestation; and the Faxian/Xuanzang desert-spirit comparison. No map coordinates should be added on this evidence. Enrichment candidates: for the Arbre Sec chapter, quote the GT and GL readings side by side to show the "Christians call it" framing (Houtum-Schindler, 1909); for Baghdad, a caption noting that no eye-witness account of the caliph's death survives (Strange, 1900).
