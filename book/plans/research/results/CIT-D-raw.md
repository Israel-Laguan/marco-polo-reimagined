**1. THE DEATHBED LINE (I-B1)**

**(1) Original text (Latin)**  
From Jacopo d’Acqui, *Chronica sive Imago mundi* (written before 1334):  
«Non scripsi mediantem de hiis que vidi»  
(with the surrounding context in the standard printed extract: friends asked him on his deathbed to correct the book by removing what went beyond the facts; he replied that he had not written half of what he had seen).

**(2) English translation**  
Yule’s rendering (Introduction to *The Book of Ser Marco Polo*): “he had not told one-half of what he had really seen!”  
A more literal modern rendering: “I have not written the half of those things which I saw.”

**(3) Edition and URL**  
Printed extract in *Historiae Patriae Monumenta*, Scriptores t. III (Turin, 1848), cols. 1357–1626 (the Polo passage is in the later columns).  
Yule quotes and translates it in the Introduction to the 3rd edition (1903), vol. I, p. 54 (and earlier editions).  
Stable scan of Yule: Project Gutenberg / Internet Archive copies of Yule–Cordier.  
Recent scholarly discussion with the Latin: Marcello Bolognari, “La Chronica di Iacopo d’Acqui e il Devisement dou Monde di Marco Polo,” *TranScript* 3 (2024): 21–68 (open-access PDF via Edizioni Ca’ Foscari).

**(4) One-line credit**  
Jacopo d’Acqui, *Chronica sive Imago mundi*, c. 1330–34.

**(5) Confidence and caveat**  
High for the core sentence. The clause “for I knew I would not be believed” has **no basis** in Jacopo; it is a later popular expansion. Jacopo attributes the remark to Marco’s deathbed conversation with friends who urged him to prune the “incredible” parts; the reason given is simply that he had seen far more. The capture story Jacopo attaches (Battle of Ayas 1294/96) is chronologically impossible and is rejected by modern scholars. The text survives in a small number of manuscripts; the printed 1848 edition remains the usual reference.

**2. COLUMBUS ON CHIPANGU (III-11)**

**(1) Original text**  
Columbus’s postils are in the margins of his copy of Francesco Pipino’s Latin translation (*De consuetudinibus et conditionibus orientalium regionum*), printed by Gerard Leeu (Gouda, between 1483 and 11 June 1484), now in the Biblioteca Capitular y Colombina, Seville.  
The exact wording of the Chipangu-gold postil(s) is not freely extractable from open scans in the sources consulted; Juan Gil’s critical edition transcribes them. Pipino’s underlying text (the passage Columbus annotated) describes Chipangu as an island 1,500 miles east with endless gold, a palace roofed and paved with gold plates two fingers thick, etc.

**(2) English translation**  
Standard scholarly translations of the postils appear in Gil’s edition and subsequent studies; Columbus’s notes typically highlight the abundance of gold, the prohibition on export, and the golden palace. (Exact wording of the marginalia themselves requires Gil.)

**(3) Edition and URL**  
Juan Gil (ed.), *El libro de Marco Polo: ejemplar anotado por Cristóbal Colón y que se conserva en la Biblioteca Capitular y Colombina de Sevilla* (Madrid: Testimonio, 1986; Tabula Americae 5).  
Facsimile of the Leeu incunable also issued by Testimonio.  
Library record / limited HathiTrust view available; full postil transcription is in the printed Gil edition (not freely online in complete form).

**(4) One-line credit**  
Christopher Columbus, marginal postils on Pipino’s Latin Marco Polo (Leeu, c. 1483–85), transcribed by Juan Gil, 1986.

**(5) Confidence and caveat**  
High that the annotations exist and concern Chipangu’s gold; medium for the precise Latin wording of every postil without the Gil volume open. Scholarly consensus holds that the notes are largely by Columbus (with some by his circle, including the young Ferdinand), written in haste, and date from the period of preparation for the voyages (late 1480s–early 1490s), though exact sequencing remains debated. The popular invented line “And this island [Chipangu] is so rich in gold that no man can tell it” is a free paraphrase of Polo/Pipino, not a Columbus postil.

**3. THE BOOK’S STATED PURPOSE (III-11, III-12)**

**(a) F (Franco-Italian) prologue**  
**(1) Original** (BnF fr. 1116 and related):  
«Et por ce pensa que trop seroit grans maus se il ne feist mettre en escript ce que il avoit veu et oy par vérité, à ce que les autres gens, qui ne l’ont veu ne oy, le sachent par cest livre.»  
(Full opening: “Seigneurs enperaor et rois, dux et marquois…”)

**(2) English** (Yule): “And for that reason he bethought himself that it would be a very great pity did he not cause to be put in writing all the great marvels that he had seen, or on sure information heard of, so that other people who had not these advantages might, by his Book, get such knowledge.”

**(3) Edition/URL**  
Critical text in Luigi Foscolo Benedetto, *Il Milione* (Florence, 1928); Yule–Cordier translation widely available on Project Gutenberg / Internet Archive.

**(4) Credit**  
Franco-Italian prologue of the *Devisement dou monde*, c. 1298.

**(b) Pipino’s Latin prologue**  
Pipino states that he translated the work from the vernacular into Latin so that the wonders of the East might be known more widely and so that the truth of the Christian faith might be confirmed by the diversity of peoples and the greatness of God’s works. (Exact wording requires the critical edition of Pipino; it is not a simple “so that other people might know.”)

**(c) Ramusio’s 1559 preface**  
Ramusio (addressed to Fracastoro) argues that the recent discoveries have confirmed many of Polo’s statements and that the world should not lose the fruit of such diligence; he publishes a more accurate text drawn from old manuscripts. He explicitly compares Polo’s land journey favourably with Columbus’s sea voyage.

**4. EARLY INSPIRATION (III-B1)**

Genuine pre-1600 statements:

- **Toscanelli’s letter to Fernão Martins, 25 June 1474** (later sent to Columbus): describes Quinsay (City of Heaven), the Great Khan, Zaiton, and Cipango as rich in gold, pearls and precious stones, with temples and palaces covered with gold plates; gives distances westward from Lisbon.  
  Latin/Italian texts in Vignaud, *Toscanelli and Columbus* (1902); English translations widely reprinted. Reliability: the letter’s authenticity has been debated since the 19th century but is still commonly accepted as reflecting 15th-century geographical ideas drawn in part from Polo.

- **Columbus’s own postils** (see Q2): he repeatedly marks Polo’s gold, spices and the Great Khan, showing the book shaped his expectations of the western route.

- **Ramusio’s 1559 preface**: explicitly sets Polo’s land explorations beside Columbus’s sea voyages and argues that Polo’s diligence should not be lost now that the East is becoming known.

- **Fra Mauro’s map** (c. 1450): incorporates place-names and information ultimately derived from Polo (and other sources); the accompanying legends treat the eastern regions as known through travellers’ reports.

- Portuguese and Spanish chroniclers of the early discoveries (e.g., references in the milieu of the Catholic Monarchs and the early chronicles of the Indies) repeatedly invoke the Great Khan, Cathay and Cipango in terms that echo Polo via the Toscanelli–Columbus channel.

**One-line credits**  
Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli, letter of 25 June 1474; Christopher Columbus, postils on Pipino’s Polo; Giovanni Battista Ramusio, preface to *Navigationi et viaggi*, vol. II (1559); Fra Mauro, world map and legends (c. 1450).
