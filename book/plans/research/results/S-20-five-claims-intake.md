# S-20 — five-claim report: independent intake (2026-11-13)

[Supplied report](S-20-five-claims-raw.md). The spike remains closed; this intake improves its handoff rather than opening a new research round. The report is useful for locating sources but several “safe” conclusions go beyond what its evidence establishes. Independent checks below distinguish primary objects, scholars' arguments and the reporting chain.

| Topic | Evaluation | What was actually inspected |
|---|---|---|
| **A specific Columbus postil** | The report says no locatable, hand-attributed example was found. A separate check located Juan Gil's 2006 discussion of his study of the Seville copy. On p. 30 he attributes to Columbus two short notes on Japan's gold and red pearls at **fol. 57v**; on pp. 30–31 he transcribes a note at **fol. 59v** associating Zayzen with the cape Columbus named Alfaeto in 1494. These are Gil's edition-based transcriptions and handwriting attributions, not an independent inspection of the exemplar; the individual note is not itself a dated document. If the Alfaeto identification is accepted, the name offers an argument that this particular note postdates its naming, not that every annotation shares its date. Gil's historical interpretation of the identifications must remain attributed. | [Gil 2006, pp. 30–31](https://www.academia.edu/90754766/Libro_del_famoso_Marco_Polo_veneciano_Edici%C3%B3n_en_facs%C3%ADmile_de_la_impresa_en_Logro%C3%B1o_por_Miguel_de_Egu%C3%ADa_1529_), text read. Gil's 1986 facsimile edition and the actual book were not inspected; exact Latin should be checked against the edition and photograph before quotation. |
| **Day letter and dating** | Gil explicitly identifies the book sent by John Day with the Seville copy and argues Columbus received it in 1497 and read it before the third voyage in May 1498 (pp. 25–27). He further argues from ink and reading habits that **most**, not necessarily all, postils were made in 1497–98 (pp. 33–34). This supplies a named, substantive case that earlier intakes lacked; it is still an inference from the letter and other evidence. The [Day translation](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/Depts/History/Maritime/Sources/1497johnday.htm) itself does not identify the edition or surviving copy. Gil calls the printing Antwerp; current catalogues identify Gouda (F-19), so retain the modern imprint. Do not turn his interpretation into a proven date for every hand or note. | Gil 2006, pp. 25–27, 33–34, read; Vigneras translation of letter, p. 226, read earlier. The 1497–98 attribution from a facsimile seller is also present but has no dating source of its own: [vendor description](https://www.facsimilefinder.com/facsimiles/columbus-marco-polo-facsimile). |
| **Pittaluga and general date** | Pittaluga cites Gil for a tendency to put annotations in the surviving printed-book collection after 1493, perhaps after 1496 or 1497. This is a scholarly summary concerning a group of books, not an independent dated proof for every Polo annotation. The report's “all the notes are after 1492, most plausibly 1497–98” overstates its own support and should not replace the existing qualified caption. | [Pittaluga 2006](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/223061756.pdf), p. 77 n. 1, read; underlying Gil 1989/1991 passages not inspected. [Bergreen's source note](https://erenow.org/biographies/marco-polo-from-venice-to-xanadu/18.php) reports Fernández-Armesto's contrary view that Columbus consulted Polo before the first voyage; this is a secondary relay and it is unclear from that note whether he means this surviving exemplar. Do not conflate reading a Polo text with dating this specific copy or hand. |
| **Catalan Atlas** | Liščák attributes the Great Khan and Chanbalech legends to Polo; he prints their texts and names the earlier scholarly transcriptions on which he relies. Use this as a named scholarly comparison rather than claim direct inspection or prove an exclusive Polo source. His survey's failure to mention Chipangu is not proof that no label or possible Japan-like feature exists. Continue to cut the unsupported draft claim without asserting absence. | [Liščák 2018](https://ica-proc.copernicus.org/articles/1/69/2018/ica-proc-1-69-2018.pdf), pp. 5–7 (§§5.2.1–5.2.2), article and quoted legends read; map itself not inspected. The publisher records 2018 and DOI 10.5194/ica-proc-1-69-2018; the PDF footer incorrectly says 2017 and cites a 2017 DOI. |
| **Toscanelli** | Existing S20-E31/E42 already cover named disagreement. No need to adopt the report's statement that a particular letter is widely accepted today, or its charge that a particular person forged it; its evidence is not a current survey and Vignaud's proposed culprit is a conjecture. F-19's attributed-dispute or omission treatment stands. | [Apfelstadt 1992 abstract](https://www.academia.edu/80410730/Christopher_Columbus_Paolo_Dal_Pozzo_Toscanelli_and_Fernao_De_Roriz_New_Evidence_for_a_Florentine_Connection), and Van Duzer 2019 previously read. |
| **Nickname** | The report's Jacopo material is useful but its claim that this is the earliest evidence found is incomplete: Pozza's 1319 confraternity entry was already documented via Bolognari and Vogel (S15-H3/S20-E43). The reported explanations are not proof of the name's origin. Date and provenance of the specific “million lies” explanation remain unverified. Earlier intakes already corrected the report's Apfelstadt year, Sangorrín Guallar's first name and the false *pelorce* etymology; the current manuscript was not shown to contain those alleged citation errors. | S20-E27/E43; [Vogel's preface](https://www.academia.edu/4258345/Marco_Polo_Was_in_China_New_Evidence_from_Currencies_Salts_and_Revenues), pp. xxiii–xxiv, previously read. |
| **Yang and Jackson** | Ma's 1941 title and pagination remain a scholarly bibliography report, not an inspected 1941 article. Contrary to the suggestion that only one scholarly witness identifies the record's location, [Zhang Longxi](https://culturahistorica.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/zhang-marco-polo.pdf) explicitly cites the relevant *Yongle dadian* passage, and [Lin and Zhang](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-archaeology/article/chinese-porcelain-jar-associated-with-marco-polo-a-discussion-from-an-archaeological-perspective/DE3C18BB07350811AB6A9A886B8F16BD) also identify it. Ma describes it as *Yuanshi*: document the discrepancy rather than flatten the source evidence, and retain the *Yongle dadian* attribution to those scholars. Ma credits Cleaves with connecting Chinese and Persian sources, but neither Ma nor this report establishes that Cleaves was the first person ever to do so. | Zhang and Lin–Zhang read; [Jackson 1998](https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/Jackson%20Marco%20Polo.pdf), p. 98, text read: he presents Qocha's appearance in the Persian account as **apparent corroboration** of the Polo narrative that the other envoys died, not as the Persian account's explicit survival count. Neither relevant eastern account names the Polos. Yang 1941 and Cleaves 1976 were not independently inspected in this intake. |
