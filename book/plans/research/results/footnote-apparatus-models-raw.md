# Deep Research: Footnote Design, Reader Engagement, and Apparatus Architecture in Popular-Scholarly Nonfiction

**Source:** Deep Research Agent run (2026-11-21)
**Target:** Notes and apparatus architecture for *The Book of the Million* / Marco Polo Retelling.

---

# Make Polo's notes reward, never require

The benchmark works that make notes a pleasure for general readers all do the same four things. They keep the discursive note next to the passage it comments on. They give it a voice that drops a register below the main text. They anchor every aside to a source the reader could check. And they make sure nothing in a note is needed to follow the story. Citations, by contrast, go to the back, where readers who want to check can find them. Gibbon's asides were moved to the foot of the page so that "the joke was not so widely separated from its occasion." Random House puts more than 90% of its annotated nonfiction into unnumbered page-and-phrase endnotes so the books do not "look more academic." Yule's Saianfu note already shows the polyphonic, verdict-giving note a Polo retelling needs, but at a length that has to be cut by roughly three-quarters. For the planned *Il Milione* retelling, the evidence supports a three-flavour system with a different place on the page for each flavour:

- **Curiosity notes** go at the foot of the page, marked with *, written in warm prose, and kept to about 80 words.
- **Contrast notes** also go at the foot of the page, marked with a lozenge (◊), and give at most three named witnesses plus a verdict, in about 110 words.
- **Authority material** gets no mark in the text. It sits in back-matter notes keyed by page number and catchphrase, written in telegraphic shorthand, with verdict labels tied to a reliability ledger. A narrow outer margin carries only the F/Z/R provenance flags recommended in the earlier dossier and ledger pointers.

Two things are missing from the evidence. **No empirical reader study** comparing footnotes, endnotes or sidenotes for general nonfiction readers was found, so every layout claim below rests on the judgement of typographers and editors, not measured behaviour. And the word limits and density caps proposed here are this report's own inferences, not published rules.

## Five benchmarks show trust and delight come from the same move

### Gibbon: an aside to a friend, always attached to a citation

Gibbon is the canonical case. His notes are **"part comment, part chapter-and-verse"** (Gross, NYRB). The placement story holds up in outline. The 1776 first volume printed the notes at the back. Hume, writing through their shared publisher, pressed for them to go at the foot of the page, and later printings moved them there (Gross, NYRB; Kermode, LRB). Hume's argument was about usability: a reader who turns to the back often finds only a bare reference. Gibbon's argument, as Kermode reports it, was about rhetoric: a joke has to sit beside its occasion. Both arguments point the same way for discursive notes.

The surviving notes show the technique. The Origen note (ch. 15, n. 96) cites Eusebius, *Hist. Eccl.* VI.8, and then observes that a man who usually read Scripture allegorically took Matthew 19:12 literally in this one case (CCEL). The Theodora note quotes Victor Tununensis in Latin, gloating over the empress's cancer, and sets beside it Paul the Silentiary praising "St. Theodora" two years later (CCEL). The irony is built entirely from two sources placed next to each other, which makes it a Contrast note before the term existed. Gibbon also says he cut chapters 15–16 "by three successive revisals" (Memoirs), so the compression was deliberate. Churchill's verdict that he was "not even estranged by his naughty footnotes" is the lay reader's response this project is aiming for.

### Grafton: the note as proof, and the gap in his account

Grafton supplies the trust theory. Footnotes are the "outward and visible signs" of critical history. They carry a second narrative, about the research, alongside the story of events (Gross; O'Donnell, BMCR). The reviewers identify what he leaves out. Gross says Grafton neglects the **discursive** note, the home of wit and argument. O'Donnell says he "entirely elides" marginal annotation and ignores physical layout (BMCR). Those two gaps are exactly the design problem this report addresses. Grafton's Bayle story is the warning that comes with them: the "Mozart of the footnote" sometimes cited sources he had not read (Kermode). A digressive note that is not backed by a real source loses the reader's trust.

### The Landmark series: excellent navigation that crowds out comment

The Landmark ancient histories treat notes as an engineered interface rather than a voice. Footnotes "refer place-names in the text to nearby maps," flag scholarly controversies, and send readers to appendices. Side notes and running heads give date and place. In the Arrian volume this works well: the notes are "outstandingly lucid," and the one-sentence side summaries are "exceptionally useful" (BMCR 2011.05.58). Peter Green's review of the Herodotus shows the failure mode. The notes there are **"all-too-brief"** and crowded at the foot of the page by map references, and the 127 maps are "overkill" (Green, NYRB). The lesson is that navigation should be moved out of the footnote band into running heads, margins and a few good maps, leaving the foot of the page free for comment.

### Mary Beard: two registers for two kinds of book

Beard practises both registers. Her scholarly ideal, set out in the column "The damn footnotes," is a precise page reference, modesty instead of bibliographic display, and **"value-added wit,"** so that a reader who checks a note gets an extra thought or a joke. The research notes for this report also describe *SPQR* as having no note cues in the text and a discursive "Further reading" essay organised by chapter. That description of the back matter was **not confirmed from the book itself**.

### Popular narrative history: the curiosity note and the audit trail

Popular narrative history supplies both the curiosity model and the warning about audits. Reviewers found the notes in Dalrymple's *White Mughals* "exhaustive and informative in themselves" but said they "can slow down reading considerably." Other critics objected to a "determination to display all that he has learned" (complete-review). A 2025 review of *The Anarchy* picked apart the glossary instead. It points out that *firangi* means a European, not any foreigner, and that Muharram is a month of mourning, not a "festival" (Brown Pundits). Cultural glosses are the first thing specialists check.

*The Swerve* is the cautionary case for the citation tier. Jim Hinch read the endnotes to test Greenblatt's claims. He found no primary sources for widespread medieval self-flagellation, and he found that a source cited repeatedly contradicted the argument: **"Did he read it?"** (LARB). Unnumbered back-matter notes are invisible to casual readers but fully visible to critics. Each one has to support its claim directly.

Mary Roach shows the reward layer at its best. She uses footnotes for material she "can't bear to cut," calls it "just me being indulgent," and kept a "Flatus Backwards" joke after her editor objected. She also reports that footnotes work badly in e-books and planned to use fewer. Gardner's *Annotated Alice* shows annotation as a franchise: notes added in 1960, merged in 1999, and given 100+ new or updated entries in 2015. A Polo note layer could likewise be designed to grow across editions.

Fiction shows the dividing line. One critic says *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell* can be read "while skipping the footnotes". Langford notes that Gene Wolfe and Alasdair Gray hide essential content in their apparatus, so skipping it means missing the point. He also mocks imitators who assume footnotes are inherently funny. Popular history should use Clarke's model, not Wolfe's.

### Yule–Cordier and Dan Carlin: the ancestor and the oral version

Yule–Cordier is the direct ancestor for Polo. Yule's Saianfu note identifies the city through Rashiduddin, whose form of the name leaves "no room for doubt." It dates the siege from the Chinese annals, which run from late 1268 to its fall in spring 1273, and notes that the Polos could not have reached Kublai before the end of 1274. It adds Wassaf as a third witness. It closes with a first-person hedged verdict: "very difficult in this case to justify Marco's veracity," yet "I am very unwilling to believe that there was no justification" (NII Digital Silk Road, Yule–Cordier II p. 211). Kinoshita's Hackett translation sits at the opposite pole. Its notes are "short yet informative, inserted when needed," the stated aim is "without overwhelming the general reader," and genealogies and seven regional maps go to the back (Cruse, TMR).

Dan Carlin's written "footnote" is a bare list of about 35 affiliate-linked books with no annotations. His real annotation happens orally: the "audio footnotes" and on-air disagreement between historians covered in the earlier dossier. That matters for the audiobook design below.

### What separates a cabinet of curiosities from a chore

No critic found uses the phrase "cabinet of curiosities" or "audit note"; those terms come from the brief. The distinction itself runs through every source above. A cabinet note does six things:

- It shows one concrete object or detail, not a summary.
- It turns on something: irony, juxtaposition or reversal, as in Gibbon's Theodora note.
- It is anchored to a checkable source (Gibbon's Eusebius, Beard's page numbers).
- It is safe to skip (Clarke, not Wolfe).
- It sits next to the passage it comments on (Kermode on Gibbon).
- It has been compressed (Gibbon's three revisions).

A chore note fails in one of six ways:

- It is a bare reference behind a cue (Hume's complaint).
- It is a navigation pointer crowding the page (Green on the Landmark Herodotus).
- It displays learning (the critics of *White Mughals*).
- Its cue gives no hint of what the note holds, so readers open everything "just in case".
- It is unsupported (Bayle, *The Swerve*).
- It is load-bearing, so the reader cannot actually skip it.

| Benchmark | Convention worth copying | Documented failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Gibbon | Ironic aside welded to a precise citation; footnote, not endnote | Learned-language prudery; untranslated Latin |
| Grafton | Note as proof and second narrative | Ignores the discursive note and layout |
| Landmark | Running heads, side summaries, map and appendix routing | Map pointers crowd out comment; "overkill" maps |
| Beard | Precision first, "value-added wit" | (Back-matter format unverified) |
| Dalrymple | Curiosity footnotes plus endnote citations | Pacing drag; display; glossary errors |
| Greenblatt | (Cautionary) | Endnotes that fail an audit |
| Roach / Gardner / Clarke | Reward layer; expandable franchise; skip-safe | Roach: footnote jokes die in e-books |
| Yule–Cordier | Three-witness note with hedged verdict | Length (encyclopaedic scale) |
| Kinoshita | Short identifying notes; maps and genealogies at back | Deliberately avoids verdicts |
| Carlin | Oral attribution in the narration | Written source list is unannotated |

## Three flavours, three registers: editorial rules with worked Polo notes

The flavours follow the three-way split that the research notes infer from the benchmarks: discursive or curiosity, navigation, and audit. Here navigation is folded into the Authority flavour, and the polyphonic note gets its own type because Polo's material demands it. Each flavour gets its own placement, mark, register and length cap.

| | Curiosity (*) | Contrast (◊) | Authority (no cue; margin pointer optional) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | Gloss an object, custom, word or person; delight | Stage two or three witnesses on one event; let the reader see the gap | Give the verdict, the edition and source locator, and point to maps or appendices |
| Placement | Foot of page | Foot of page, set off from Curiosity notes | Back-matter notes keyed by page and phrase; reliability-ledger appendix |
| Register | Full prose, first person allowed, Gibbon/Roach warmth | Full prose; witnesses named as characters; closing verdict in the guide's voice | Telegraphic shorthand plus at most one plain-prose verdict sentence |
| Length cap | ~80 words / ~500 characters | ~110 words / ~700 characters; Polo + max two witnesses; one sentence each | Citation ≤40 words; verdict ≤25 words |
| Must contain | One concrete detail, one turn, one source anchor | Witness names with dates or roles; a verdict label; one pointer (map or appendix) | Catchphrase (4–6 words), edition, locator, verdict label where relevant |
| Never | Carry information needed for the plot; show off | Exceed three voices; rerun the "Did he go?" debate | Cite unread sources; claim more than the source says |
| Overflow rule | Longer → fold into body or cut | Longer → appendix essay ("The Witnesses") | Longer → appendix or ledger entry |

### Recommended print specification

- **Footnotes (Curiosity *, Contrast ◊):** 9/11 pt, same family, full measure; short rule or a line space above; never broken across pages; max 2 per page, target ≤1 per 400 words.
- **Outer margin (~15–20 mm):** Provenance letters Z/R and ledger pointers only, 7.5–8 pt small caps.
- **Running heads:** Verso: part title; recto: region · modern country.
- **Back-matter notes:** Unnumbered; head "Notes to pages 201–232"; page number + bold 4–6-word catchphrase; 9/11 pt; grouped by chapter.
- **Appendices:** A Which Polo? B The Witnesses (Contrast overflow). C Did he go? D Money, Salt and Paper. E Gazetteer. F Reliability Ledger. Plus maps.

## Budget the apparatus before writing it

- On-page notes average no more than one per ~400 words of narrative and never more than two per page.
- Each chapter has one to three Contrast notes, reserved for set pieces: Saianfu, Ahmad, the great cities.
- Every Curiosity and Contrast note is promoted or demoted at copy-edit. If the story needs it, it moves into the body. If it runs over its cap, it becomes appendix material. Otherwise it is cut.
- Map and cross-reference pointers never occupy the foot of the page on their own. They live in running heads, margin pointers or the end of a substantive note.
