# Chapter III-9
## The Princess Cocachin: Departure, Sea Passage, and the Way Home

> *“Now it came to pass in those days that the Queen BOLGANA, wife of ARGON, Lord of the Levant, departed this life… The Kaan… sent for a lady whose name was COCACHIN… They put forth to sea… nearly all died by the way… And this was in the year 1295 of Christ’s Incarnation.”*
> — Marco Polo, Prologue, chs. 17–18 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**


---

A sewn-in pocket of rubies on the quay at Hormos (Old Hormuz) does a merchant little good if he cannot walk through the palace gate at Cambaluc (Beijing) to spend them. Rewind from the blistering Persian Gulf shore to the Great Kaan’s northern court, where Niccolò, Maffeo, and Marco had spent years in imperial service, gathering immense wealth in gold and precious stones. One cold worry kept all three awake at night: Cublay (Kublai) Kaan had grown very old [1]. They knew too well what befell foreign merchants when a great patron died and his successor took the throne; if the emperor died before his Venetian servants departed, rival princes and jealous court factions would never shield three wealthy foreigners on the road to the Black Sea. Time and again they knelt politely to beg leave to go, yet Cublay loved having them about his court too dearly to let them slip away [2]. Every extra winter in Cambaluc filled their chests tighter and narrowed their chance of getting home with their fortunes intact.

Rescue rode in from Persia on mourning saddles. Queen Bolgana (Bulughan Khatun), wife of Ilkhan Argon (Arghun), had died beside the Kur in the spring of 1286. On her deathbed she charged her husband that no bride should take her throne unless chosen from her own Cathayan kin [3]. To honour her will, Argon dispatched three barons—Oulatay, Apusca, and Coja—with a gallant retinue across Asia to fetch a bride from Cambaluc [4]. Imagine the three Persian lords riding into the northern capital after months in the saddle, bearing letters of mourning and formal marriage gifts from the Ilkhanate. They had crossed mountains and deserts to bring back a royal kinswoman who could step into Bolgana’s place. Only a bride of the same noble blood could satisfy the dead queen’s dying charge.

Cublay welcomed the Persian lords with royal feasts and summoned seventeen-year-old Cocachin (Kököchin), a graceful maiden of Queen Bolgana’s Bayaut lineage [5]. One look satisfied the envoys that they had found their lady. Yet when they turned westward overland, an eight-month march slammed straight into fresh wars between Mongol princes. Fighting along the steppe forced the entire bridal train back to Cambaluc [6]. With mountain roads slammed shut by rival armies, only the southern ocean remained open—and none of the Persian barons knew how to manage a fleet across strange monsoon seas. A landlocked caravan master could lead camels across Persia, not steer four-masted junks through the Straits of Malacca. Without experienced mariners to read winds and harbours, the royal bride could never reach her bridegroom’s court.

At that exact moment, Marco sailed back up the coast from an imperial mission across Indian waters. He spread fresh sea-charts and port intelligence before the throne, recounting harbours, currents, and winds he had just sailed through [7]. Seeing three seasoned Latins who knew both ships and southern seas, Oulatay, Apusca, and Coja begged Cublay as a favour to send the Venetians with their bridal fleet. Who better to guide a royal bride by sea than three Europeans who wished above all else to sail west toward home? Reluctantly—with visible displeasure darkening his face—the aged Kaan granted his permission at last [8]. Cublay could scarcely refuse a dying kinswoman’s last wish or an allied Ilkhan’s envoys when the sea lane was the only road left open. For the Polos, that royal nod meant freedom after more than twenty years abroad, provided they could survive the long voyage around Asia’s southern rim.

---

Imperial leave brought instant royal provision. Calling all three Polos before him, Cublay pressed two golden tablets of authority into their hands. Those tablets guaranteed free horses, rations, and safe passage across every province of his realm [9]. He charged them with letters for Christendom’s kings—whose names shift across traditions from France and Spain to England—and bid them carry his greeting to the Pope [10]. Then he ordered thirteen stout ocean ships fitted out for the voyage. Each vessel carried four masts, up to twelve sails, and two years’ sea stores, with crews numbering 250 or 260 men on the largest ships [11]. The dockyards clattered day and night to salt beef, fill water butts, pitch seams, and stow biscuit for hundreds of sailors and courtiers before a single anchor rose. Every rope, spar, and provision cask had to hold together across two different monsoon oceans. When the fleet finally weighed anchor, it carried not just three Venetians and a royal bride, but an entire floating court bound for Persia.

The Prologue leaves the port of departure unnamed, though Zayton (Quanzhou) fits every harbour clue in Volume Two [12]. A surviving Chinese administrative order preserved from April and May 1290 logs dockyard preparations for three Persian envoys and about 160 companions, yet whether the ships caught the wind late in 1290 or waited until early 1292 remains an open puzzle of monsoon calendars [13]. Preparation orders prove a fleet was provisioned; they cannot fix the morning its sails cleared the headland. Between the clerks’ ink in 1290 and the fleet’s arrival in Persia lies a silent stretch where only wind tables and survival logs speak.

After three months at sea, the Prologue names a first landfall at “Java”—almost certainly Java the Less (Sumatra), echoing the five-month monsoon halt at Samara (Samudera Pasai) described earlier in Volume Three [14]. Yet the Prologue itself never mentions Samara, palisaded trenches, five wooden towers, or five months ashore; it states simply that the fleet spent eighteen months more crossing Indian waters toward Argon’s realm, bringing the battered hulls to the Persian shore around late 1293 or early 1294 [15]. Week after week, salt damp, bad water, and tropical fever wore down crews and passengers alike. Crossing from the China Sea through Indian straits and up toward Arabia meant waiting out seasonal winds, nursing leaking hulls, and burying comrades overboard. Even seasoned mariners dreaded a passage that stretched past two years without fresh pasture or clean wells.

Death thinned the fleet mercilessly along that long salt road. Out of roughly six hundred passengers setting out besides mariners, only eight—or eighteen in one early count—survived to step ashore [16]. Another Venetian witness reckons roughly six hundred dead including crewmen, claiming that Coja alone survived among the three envoys while only one waiting-woman died [17]. Whatever ledger you trust, a glittering royal embassy limped into the Persian Gulf broken to a handful of survivors. Think of young Cocachin watching two of her three grey-bearded Persian escorts—Oulatay and Apusca—committed to the deep or buried on distant shores before a single Gulf headland rose into view, while fever hollowed the cheeks of her ladies and mariners fell from the yards. Yet all three Polos—father, uncle, and son—stepped onto the Persian beach alive, bringing their royal charge safely through. Two years of salt damp and foul cask-water leave a sobering ledger: an imperial expedition that sailed from China with trumpets and royal silk ended with a handful of gaunt survivors stepping onto barren sand.

---

Worse news waited ashore: the bridegroom was dead. Argon had died on 10 March 1291, leaving his brother Kiacatu (Gaykhatu) seated on the Ilkhanate throne since July [18]. Word went up from the landing coast to Kiacatu, who sent orders back that the surviving escort should deliver Cocachin to Argon’s son Casan (Ghazan). Riding inland to the Khorasan frontier near the Arbre Sec (the Dry Tree), where Casan guarded the eastern passes with thirty thousand horse, the three Venetians handed the young princess over to her late betrothed’s heir before turning back to Kiacatu’s own court [19]. Even here, the coastal dispatch leaves the exact Persian landing harbour unnamed, though Hormos fits every maritime clue along the Gulf [20].

Outside the Book, independent chronicles confirm the bride’s arrival without naming her Venetian escorts. Persian court history records that Cocachin was brought from Cathay to Abhar, where Casan married her amid royal gifts from the Great Kaan [21]. A Chinese court order preserved in the *Yongle dadian* likewise lists provisioning for Oulatay, Apusca, and Coja alongside some 160 companions—yet neither the Persian history nor the Chinese ledger breathes a single word about any Venetian named Polo [22]. That double silence is why the marriage embassy stands as both the Book’s finest historical anchor and its sharpest courtroom riddle.

When parting day came at the Persian camp, Cocachin wept bitterly, looking upon the three Venetians like fathers. Beside her, a second royal ward—the daughter of the king of Manzi (South China)—wept as they said farewell [23]. Picture the scene outside those Khorasan felt tents under the high dry sun: after more than two years of monsoon gales, leaking hulls, and desert tracks, three weathered merchants stood in riding dust watching a royal bride step into another empire’s court—carrying nothing of their long duty left except the warmth of a princess’s tears. Little else survives of her voice; Persian chronology places her early death less than three years later, in June 1296 [24].

From Kiacatu’s court, where they rested nine months, the three Venetians rode west carrying four fresh golden tablets of authority—two blazoned with gerfalcons, one with lions, and one plain. Those tablets commanded fresh horses, rations, and escorts of up to two hundred horsemen along restless roads [25]. Soon grim riders brought news across the plateau that Cublay himself had died in early 1294, snapping the last thread that could ever have drawn them back to Cambaluc [26]. Their old fear back in China had come true almost to the season, leaving them grateful to have cleared the court before the succession storm broke and carrying Kiacatu’s safe-conducts all the way to the Black Sea. Picture the three of them reading that news beside a Persian caravanserai fire, knowing the master who had kept them twenty years in Asia could summon them back no more.

At Trebizond (Trabzon) on the Black Sea, a later Venetian will of uncle Maffeo dated 6 February 1310 (1309 Venetian style) reveals a sharp sting at the threshold of home: nearly four thousand hyperpera lost in dealings involving the Comnenus ruler and his territory, alongside a receipt to Marco for one thousand *lire* covering joint business [27]. Golden tablets that commanded horsemen across Persia could not shield a merchant’s purse once his party stepped onto Black Sea wharves. From Trebizond the Polos sailed onward past Constantinople (Istanbul) and Negropont (Chalkida on Euboea), stepping onto the paving stones of Venice (Venezia) in 1295 after more than two decades in Asia [28].

Here the twenty-four-year physical journey ends—and the seven-hundred-year voyage of the Book itself begins. Carry their sea-stained Tartar coats down a narrow Venetian alley in the parish of San Giovanni Grisostomo and knock on the door of Ca’ Polo, where startled cousins peer sceptically through the half-open shutter, measure the weathered strangers from head to foot, and refuse to believe these foreign-speaking merchants belong to the house at all.

## Notes

[1] ~ Ramusio-only. The Polos’ gathered gold and jewels and their dread of what might happen if the aged Cublay died before they left China appear inside Yule’s brackets from Ramusio [@yc, Prologue 17 n. 1]. Yule compares Pegolotti's commercial manual on the danger foreign merchants faced when a local ruler died.

[2] ~ Cublay’s deep affection for the three Venetians and his repeated refusals to grant them leave to depart stand in the base Prologue text [@yc, Prologue 17]. The narrative turns their long stay at court into a mark of imperial favour rather than detention.

[3] ✓ Persian sources confirm Queen Bolgana’s (Bulughan’s) death beside the Kur River in spring 1286 [@yc, Prologue 17 n. 2]. The Prologue preserves her dying wish that Argon marry a new consort chosen from her own Mongol lineage in Cathay.

[4] ✓ Oulatay, Apusca, and Coja appear in the Prologue as Argon's three barons and match the three envoy names (Wuludai, Abishiha, and Huojia) preserved in the Chinese *Yongle dadian* provisioning notice [@yc, Prologue 17; @ma2025chinese]. See Note 22 for the scope of that documentary match.

[5] ~ Cocachin’s (Kököchin’s) kinship to Bolgana and her age of seventeen come from the Prologue [@yc, Prologue 17 n. 3]. Persian chronicles confirm her selection from the Bayaut tribe and her arrival in Persia without stating her exact age [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 6].

[6] ~ Ramusio-only. The eight-month overland attempt turned back by fresh war among Tartar princes appears solely in Ramusio’s version [@yc, Prologue 17 n. 3]. Yule treats the blocked land corridor across Central Asia as historically plausible given Caidu's campaigns.

[7] ~ Marco’s timely return from an Indian Ocean mission gave the stranded Persian envoys a practical reason to request the three Venetians as sea guides [@yc, Prologue 17]. The episode neatly connects Marco's southern naval errands with the family's homeward ticket.

[8] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Base manuscripts record Cublay’s reluctance and eventual consent, while Ramusio adds the visible displeasure crossing the emperor’s face at letting them go [@yc, Prologue 17]. Both versions emphasize how hard it was to win leave from the court.

[9] ~ Two golden *paiza* tablets guaranteeing provisions, ships, and free passage across the Great Kaan's dominions stand in the Prologue [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 1]. Compare the post-mortem inventory of Marco's effects in Chapter III-10, which still listed a golden tablet from the Magnificent Tartar.

[10] ~ Diplomatic letters entrusted to the Polos for the Pope and the kings of France, Spain, and other Christian realms belong to the Prologue [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 2]. Yule notes that the King of England appears only in Pauthier’s manuscript.

[11] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Thirteen four-masted ships provisioned for two years come from the base Prologue, while Ramusio adds crew counts of 250 or 260 mariners on the largest hulls [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 3]. Those four-masted vessels match the big Fujian junks described in Chapter III-1.

[12] ? Identifying the unnamed departure haven with Zayton (Quanzhou) is a standard editorial reconstruction based on southern fleet logistics and Book II's close at Zayton [@yc, Prologue 18; @yc, Introduction § 22]. Neither the Prologue nor Rashīd al-Dīn names the embarkation port explicitly.

[13] ? Jackson cites the *Yongle dadian* provisioning order of April–May 1290 [@jackson1998travels, pp. 98–99]. Ma reports Yang Zhijiu’s inference of a late-1290 or early-1291 sailing, whereas Yule prefers early 1292 based on the monsoon calendar [@ma2025chinese; @yc, Introduction § 22].

[14] ? Linking the Prologue’s three-month voyage to an island called “Java” directly to Samara’s five-month monsoon wait in Book 3 is Yule’s editorial reconstruction rather than an explicit Prologue statement [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 4; @yc, 3.10; @yc, Introduction § 22]. See [Chapter III-3](ch-03-java-less-sumatra.md) and [Chart C11, the monsoon](#fig-c11-monsoon-cocachin).

[15] ? The Prologue specifies “eighteen months more” in the Sea of India between “Java” and Argon’s territory without mentioning Samara [@yc, Prologue 18]. Yule weighs two ways of fitting the Book’s five-month Sumatran halt into that span—twenty-six months in all (`3 + 5 + 18`, sailing January 1292 to March 1294) or counting the five months inside the eighteen to reach Hormos around November 1293 [@yc, Introduction § 22 n. 19].

[16] ~ Manuscript witnesses diverge sharply on passenger survival: Yule prints eight surviving men out of six hundred (excluding mariners), whereas witness F counts eighteen survivors [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 5; @moule1938description, vol. 1, p. 91]. Either figure points to heavy mortality during a two-year tropical sea passage.

[17] ~ Ramusio-only. Counting six hundred total dead including sailors, naming Coja as the sole surviving Persian envoy, and noting that only one lady died come from Ramusio [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 5]. Waṣṣāf independently notes heavy mortality on a separate embassy returning from Cathay [@wassaf1871tarikh, p. 47].

[18] ✓ Argon died on 10 March 1291 and his brother Kiacatu (Geikhatu) acceded in July, while Argon's son Casan (Ghazan) guarded the Khorasan frontier [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 6]. By the time the bridal fleet reached the Persian Gulf, the royal bridegroom had been dead for over two years under Yule’s 1292–94 chronology—or more than a year if the fleet sailed and arrived earlier.

[19] ✓ Kiacatu's directive to deliver Cocachin to Argon’s son Casan matches Rashīd al-Dīn’s Persian account of the marriage [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 6; @thackston1998rashiduddin, p. 606]. Under Mongol levirate custom, a son could inherit his late father's betrothed bride (other than his own mother).

[20] ~ Ramusio-only in part. Sending word from the coast to Kiacatu and riding to Casan's camp near the Arbre Sec (Dry Tree) region of Khorasan come from Ramusio without naming the landing port [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 6]. Identifying the disembarkation harbour with Hormos is Yule’s geographic inference.

[21] ✓ Rashīd al-Dīn places Cocachin's marriage with Casan at Abhar, west of Qazvin [@thackston1998rashiduddin, p. 606; @jahn1940geschichte, p. 40]. The passage carries no exact day, though its position in the chronicle points to 1293.

[22] ✓ Ma reports Yang Zhijiu and Cleaves matching the *Yongle dadian* envoys to Polo’s trio (and Cleaves's opening page collates the envoy forms *Oulatai*, *Apusca*, and *Coja*), while Jackson stresses that neither the Chinese notice nor Rashīd al-Dīn's Persian chronicle names the Polos; Cleaves's full text and the *Yongle dadian* scan remain uninspected directly [@ma2025chinese; @cleaves1976chinese, p. 181; @moule1938description, vol. 1, pp. 88–93; @jackson1998travels, pp. 98–99]. Boyle reads Rashīd's singular title *khwāja* as the proper name Coja [@boyle1970rashidfranks, p. 66].

[23] ~ Cocachin’s weeping farewell to the three Venetians who had guarded her like a daughter stands in the Prologue, while the companion daughter of the king of Manzi appears in F and the Crusca manuscripts [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 8]. The scene gives the Polos a warm personal testimonial at the close of their eastern service.

[24] ? Yule renders Shaʿbān 695 AH in the Persian history as June 1296 for Cocachin’s early death, though variant dates circulate in secondary literature [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 6]. She lived less than three years after her wedding at Abhar.

[25] ~ Four blazoned gold tablets of authority—two with gerfalcons, one with lions, and one plain—together with escorts of two hundred horse through Kiacatu’s unsettled realm come from the Prologue [@yc, Prologue 18 n. 7]. Without royal safe-conducts, merchants carrying jewels across Persia in 1294 risked brigandage at every pass.

[26] ~ Ramusio-only. Learning along the Persian road that Cublay Kaan had died (in February 1294) and reflecting that their return would have been perilous without his protection appear in Ramusio [@yc, Introduction § 22; @rossabi1988khubilai, ch. 9]. The news closes the Great Kaan's reign just as his former envoys reach the Black Sea.

[27] ✓ Maffeo’s will of 6 February 1310 (1309 Venetian style) records nearly 4,000 hyperpera in losses involving the Comnenus ruler of Trebizond and his district [@moule1938description, vol. 1; @orlandini1926marco; @yc, Introduction § 23]. It also gives Marco a receipt for one thousand lire that includes other business, while leaving the exact mechanism of the Trebizond loss open.

[28] ~ Trebizond, Constantinople, Negropont, and Venice in 1295 form the Prologue’s homeward arc [@yc, Prologue 18; @yc, Introduction § 22]. The Prologue’s “six-and-twenty years” describes the Polos’ years spent in the East—a loose rounding of the twenty-four-year absence from 1271 to 1295 (since 1271 plus 26 would yield 1297), which Haw and Larner treat as usable biographical tradition [@haw2006marco, ch. 3; @larner1999marco, ch. 2].
