# Chapter III-5
## Maabar: Pearls, Suttee, Horses, and St Thomas

> *“When you leave the Island of Seilan and sail westward… you come to the great province of MAABAR which is styled INDIA THE GREATER… In this manner pearls are fished in great quantities, for thence in fact come the pearls which are spread all over the world… The Body of Messer St. Thomas the Apostle lies in this province of Maabar…”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 3, chs. 16–18 (Yule–Cordier)

**[M]**


---

Sixty miles west of Seilan (Sri Lanka) across emerald shallows, Maabar (the Coromandel Coast) swings low into view—a rich mainland realm the Book hails as India the Greater [1]. Five royal brothers share rule across these steaming coastal plains, though Marco’s chapters soon skip northward and double back out of strict shoreline order [2]. At this easternmost gulf reigns Sonder Bandi Davar, identifiable with the Pandyan king Jaṭāvarman Sundara Pāṇḍya, whose seventeenth regnal year fell in 1292–93 [3]. Before entering his hall of gems, the fleet takes us out into morning glare to watch hired divers drop down weighted lines.

Every April until mid-May, merchant syndicates muster fleets off Bettelar and steer sixty miles into the shallow pearl gulf between island and mainland. Soundings run shallow—two fathoms in places, and four to twelve where the men fish. Tall merchant ships drop stone anchors, while wage-crews hired by separate merchant companies climb aboard light fishing skiffs [4]. Over the gunwale slips a diver towards the oysters. A man who stayed in the skiff could count how often they went down without ever seeing what it was like to find a shell in the dim light. Tying a net bag round his waist, the diver sinks to the sandy bottom, packs rough shells into the mesh, bursts back up to gulp air, catches his breath across the boat’s rail, and plunges down again till dusk [5]. Picture an impatient trader counting daily wage silver on deck while wet oyster baskets pile up around wooden tubs. Clerks slit shells open one by one under hot awnings, poking through pale flesh for pea-sized gems or barren grey muscle. From these beds, Marco vows, flow the finest pearls prized across every kingdom on earth, carried by merchant galleys from Cairo to Cambaluc (Beijing).

Royal clerks divide every haul before oysters cool in sun. First the king claims an unyielding tenth of the entire catch as his royal due [6]. Next step forward the Abraiaman fish-charmers, paid one twentieth of the haul to spell away great sharks from tearing divers under dark water [7]. Listen closely to the Book’s exact terms at dusk: Marco records that *“their charm holds good for that day only”*, dissolving each night *“so that the fishes can work mischief at their will”* [8]. These Abraiaman claim power to charm beasts, birds, and every living thing across land and sea. Come mid-May, pearl oysters vanish cleanly from this gulf. They open three hundred miles distant again only from September through mid-October [9]. Tide, wages, royal tax, and shark-spells lock tight like coastal clockwork before a single pearl rolls across velvet scales.

---

Ashore sits the sovereign collecting that crisp tenth. Across all Maabar no tailor ever threads a needle or cuts a coat, because king and labourer alike go bare save for a fine loincloth [10]. Round Sonder Bandi Davar’s brown shoulders, however, hangs a rolling kingdom’s fortune. A broad collar blazing with rubies, sapphires, and emeralds rests above three golden armlets thick with pearls, paired with jewelled anklets and pearl-ringed toes worth more than a city’s ransom [11]. Over his chest drapes a fine silk cord carrying 104 large pearls and rubies, slipped gently past his fingers morning and evening for 104 daily prayers handed down by his royal ancestors [12], murmuring the sacred invocation *Pacauta! Pacauta!* round the loop [13]. Where the Book counts 104 beads, the traditional Hindu rosary carries 108—a four-bead slip easily lost in coastal retelling [14]. Every gem hanging on the king’s chest advertises a coastal court where wealth is worn in open daylight rather than locked behind stone walls.

Any merchant’s own prize gems stay locked safely inside belted chests. Royal law bans exporting any pearl heavier than half a *saggio* unless smuggled out secretly, while heralds cry several times each year that the king pays twice cost price for every rare jewel brought to court [15]. Small wonder five hundred wives crowd his palaces—even a brother’s handsome wife, seized openly while the prudent brother holds his tongue [16]. Beside the throne ride sworn barons called the Trusty Lieges. They eat at the king’s own table, ride beside his banner, and enjoy high honour across every province. Yet when death lays the king upon a sandalwood pyre, those loyal comrades fling themselves alive into roaring flames to ride beside their lord in the world beyond [17]. Even mountains of royal gold stay sealed untouched by heirs, forcing every new sovereign to pile a fresh treasury [18]—though Persian chroniclers marvel at immense hoarded Pandyan wealth without repeating that inheritance taboo [19].

Where does half that kingdom’s bullion pour straight back out to sea? Into Arabian cavalry horses—over two thousand fine chargers wanted every year by Sonder Bandi Davar and equal droves by his four brothers, priced at five hundred gold *saggi* or over a hundred silver marks apiece [20]. Out from Kisi (Kish), Hormos (Old Hormuz), Esher (Ash-Shihr), Dufar (Dhofar), and Aden sail packed horse-dhows across monsoon swells. Yet Marco swears that before twelve months pass, scarcely a hundred horses remain alive out of two thousand landed [21]. Contemporary Persian shipping contracts confirm huge Gulf cargoes crossing to Maabar in failing condition [22]. Marco accuses Gulf dealers of deliberately keeping every farrier ashore so annual demand never cools [23]. Yet feeding high-bred stallions boiled rice and cooked meat—alongside failed local stud trials breeding wry-legged colts unfit for war—leaves open whether tropical feed, salt voyages, or sharp horse-traders drove those losses [24]. Think of a stablemaster unloading fine desert stallions at dawn only to watch grooms ladle steamed rice and mutton broth into their mangers.

Fire and steel mark public death as starkly as stable ledgers. Faithful widows step boldly onto their husbands’ blazing funeral pyres amid loud public praise from kin [25]—an observance of *satī* echoed by medieval Arab and European travellers, even as the Book’s next spectacle turns darker. Stranger still unfolds a condemned criminal’s religious end. Granted leave to perish for a chosen god, he mounts a festive wooden cart bearing twelve sharp knives while kinsmen parade him down crowded streets shouting his vow. Stopping at the death-place, he cries aloud that he slays himself for love of his idol, drives knife after knife straight through both arms, thighs, and belly until life gives out, and wins joyful cremation from weeping kin [26].

---

On clean packed earth in Maabar’s heat, daily customs draw tight boundaries around every meal. Bare infantry carry only shield and lance into battle, killing neither bird nor ox and paying local Muslims to butcher whatever meat anyone eats [27]. Men and women scrub the entire body twice every day before touching rice, scorning unwashed folk like northern heretics [28]. Diners lift rice strictly with the right hand, reserving the left solely for unclean washing, and hold private bronze flasks high aloft so cool water pours cleanly down the throat without lips brushing the rim—never lending a personal cup to a stranger, though hosts willingly tilt water straight into a traveller’s cupped palms [29]. Cattle enjoy sacred shelter in Polo’s account. He says the people he calls *Govy* eat beef when an animal has died, though they would never kill an ox themselves [30]. Townsfolk plaster cottage walls and floors smooth with cow-dung and sit straight upon packed earth because human flesh springs from soil and returns to it [31].

Credit and street courts obey borders just as sharp. Magistrates disqualify both wine-drinkers and ocean sailors alike as legal sureties [32]. In one unforgettable street tableau, a patient creditor stops any debtor simply by scribing a crisp chalk circle round his boots—leaving the man trapped until he settles the claim or surrenders his goods, and Marco swears he once watched even the king check his horse inside a foreign merchant’s drawn ring until silver clinked into the creditor’s palm [33]. Picture every skipper along the pier grinning quietly behind a barrel while royal hoofs wait on chalk dust.

Heavy rains fall solely across June, July, and August, leaving clear skies nine months a year [34]. Even in dry sunshine, omens rule every morning’s walk: a single sneeze halts travellers mid-step until they sit down and wait out the ill hour, while astrologers chart every newborn baby’s exact birth-hour and lunar sign [35]. Along the sandy beaches, bright thirteen-year-old boys pace the surf with twenty or twenty-four silver groats tucked inside their belts, snapping up odd bargains off incoming skiffs and flipping pearls indoors to idle merchants who keep out of midday sun for a brisk Venice groat’s gain [36]. At temple festivals, consecrated girls dance, sing, and lay hot rice dishes before carved idols—leaving platters spread long enough for a baron to dine before sharing the leftovers [37]. Priests summon those temple dancers whenever god and goddess quarrel, coaxing celestial harmony back into heaven so harvest rains never fail [38].

---

Away from the crowded pearl stalls, quiet dusty lanes lead inland to Mailapur (Mylapore), a quiet little town holding scarce trade wares yet drawing Christians and Muslims alike to the tomb of Messer St Thomas [39]. Muslim pilgrims venerate the apostle as a great prophet under the Arabic title *Avarian*, meaning holy man. Fever-stricken visitors scoop red earth from the martyrdom floor to stir into cool water against stubborn tertian and quartan agues, swearing sick men rise healed [40]. The Book also preserves a local wonder: not even twenty men, it claims, can hold one of the *Govy* inside the chapel, because their forebears caused the apostle’s death.

In 1288, local shrine brethren told Marco a sharp miracle over pilgrim rooms [41]. A pagan baron had packed every church guest-house to the rafters with harvested rice. Weary travellers were shut out into bare fields despite repeated pleas from the keepers. That night St Thomas stood over the baron’s bed, pressing an iron fork hard against his throat, and warned him to empty the pilgrim houses at once or die an evil death [42]. By sunrise every sack was hauled clean away, and the shaken baron stood in the courtyard confessing his terror aloud. Beside the shrine lamps sits a crisp household balance-sheet: green groves of coconut palms keep the small church alive, though one of the royal brothers taxes them six Venetian groats per tree each month [43]. Even guardians of a martyr’s tomb count rent down to the half-groat.

Asked how Thomas died, those brethren lead visitors out toward wooded crags where wild peacocks strutted round the saint at prayer [44]. A Govy archer loosed a shaft through branches toward a fan of bright feathers and struck the praying apostle cleanly in the right side. Dying, the apostle still kept his eyes turned toward his Creator, and pilgrims still gather the red earth beneath his tomb as a living relic—a well-attested medieval pilgrimage cult shared by Eastern Christians and Muslims, paired here with local shrine legends rather than earlier Syriac martyrdom accounts. Across Maabar, mothers rub dark babies weekly with warm sesame oil to deepen glossy ebony skins—painting holy idols pitch-black and wicked devils chalk-white. Meanwhile, cavalrymen twine stiff wild-ox tail hairs round spear-shaft and horse-mane alike, trusting that rough charm to bring both rider and mount untouched out of war [45].

From Mailapur’s red courtyard, the coast runs north toward Mutfili (Motupalli) [46], where a wise queen guards shipwreck cargoes and snakes coil beneath valleys glittering with uncut diamonds.

## Notes

[1] ✓ Sailing sixty miles west from Seilan reaches Maabar (the Coromandel Coast), styled India the Greater across Book 3 [@yc, 3.16 n. 1]. The name derives from Arabic *Maʿbar* (“the Passage” or ferry across from Ceylon).

[2] ~ Map A17 and Yule’s comparative itinerary distinguish the Book’s inland-jumping chapter order from the real geographic sweep round Cape Comorin [@yc, 3.30 n. 2]. See [Map A17, the Book's order and the Indian coast](#fig-a17-book-and-coast).

[3] ~ Sastri identifies Sonder Bandi Davar with Jaṭāvarman Sundara Pāṇḍya (acc. c. 1276, regnal year 17 in 1292–93), while Waṣṣāf records AH 692 (1293 CE) for a Pandyan ruler’s death [@nilakantasastri1929pandyan; @wassaf1871tarikh, pp. 32–35]. Multiple Pandyan brother-kings shared rule across the Tamil country in this period [@yc, 3.16 n. 1].

[4] ~ April–May pearling fleets anchoring off Bettelar and working small skiffs across two-to-twelve-fathom banks appear in Yule’s base account [@yc, 3.16 nn. 1–2]. Calm spring waters before the south-west monsoon burst provided the annual diving window in the Gulf of Mannar.

[5] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The dives and their depths are in the base text. Ramusio adds the waist net bag and the repeated dives, while Mannar shell-midden studies confirm the long history of the pearl banks [@yc, 3.16 n. 2; @siriwardana2024pearl].

[6] ~ Claiming a tenth royalty from every catch records royal fiscal control over the Gulf of Mannar beds [@yc, 3.16]. Pearl revenues formed one of the Pandyan crown's chief sources of wealth.

[7] ~ Paying Abraiaman shark-charmers a twentieth of the oyster haul reflects hereditary Gulf of Mannar fishery custom [@yc, 3.16 n. 2]. Tennent and Ostroff trace later colonial testimony and the persistence of the *kadal-katti* (“sea-binders”) well into the nineteenth century [@tennent1859ceylon, vol. 2, pp. 564–565; @ostroff2016beds, pp. 133–134].

[8] ~ Attributing daytime protection and nighttime release of the sharks to priestly spells preserves the fishermen’s own explanation of the charmers' trade [@yc, 3.16 n. 2]. Polo shrewdly guesses that lifting the charm at sunset prevented unauthorized divers from working the beds after hours.

[9] ~ Shifting the harvest to a second bed roughly three hundred miles off in September–October extends the Book’s seasonal calendar [@yc, 3.16]. Yule notes that the historic Mannar and Tuticorin banks were actually fished in the spring season.

[10] ~ Claiming Maabar needs no tailors because everyone goes bare apart from a small loincloth reflects hot tropical dress habits [@yc, 3.17 n. 1]. To a Venetian accustomed to heavy woollens and furs, South Indian cotton minimalism looked like total nudity.

[11] ✓ Royal jewelled collars, three golden arm-bracelets, anklets, and pearl toe-rings vividly match Pandyan and Chola royal temple bronzes [@yc, 3.17 n. 2]. Prestige in southern courts was displayed through gemstones and gold worn directly on bare skin.

[12] ~ Wearing 104 large pearls and rubies on a silk cord for morning and evening prayer devotions appears in the base manuscripts [@yc, 3.17 nn. 2–3]. Compare Note 14 on the traditional Hindu rosary count.

[13] ~ Ramusio-only. Repeating the word *Pacauta* 104 times appears in Yule’s brackets solely from Ramusio [@yc, 3.17 n. 3]. The detail supplies the spoken invocation accompanying the king's prayer beads.

[14] ~ Caldwell, cited in Yule, compares the standard 108-bead Hindu rosary (*mālā*) and proposes a Tamil or Sanskrit devotional invocation such as *Bhagavata* behind *Pacauta* [@yc, 3.17 n. 3]. The slight shift from 108 to 104 beads is an easy slip in oral transmission.

[15] ~ Banning the export of pearls or gems over half a *saggio* while proclaiming double purchase prices illustrates royal pre-emption over top stones [@yc, 3.17 n. 4]. Generous court payments kept the finest Mannar pearls flowing into the king's treasury rather than smuggling skiffs.

[16] ~ Five hundred royal wives and seizing a brother’s consort belong to Polo’s court gossip without outside confirmation [@yc, 3.17]. The anecdote sets up the peacemaking mother in Chapter III-6.

[17] ~ Sworn royal companions leaping onto the sovereign’s funeral pyre (*Trusty Lieges*) align with South Indian *garuda* and *vēḷaikkārar* bodyguard vows cited by Yule [@yc, 3.17 n. 5]. Inscriptions and Arab travellers confirm retainers who bound themselves to die with their king.

[18] ~ Requiring heirs to leave a predecessor's royal treasure sealed intact offers Polo’s fiscal explanation for Maabar’s vast accumulated jewels [@yc, 3.17 n. 6]. Compare Waṣṣāf's Persian account in Note 19.

[19] ~ Waṣṣāf independently reports immense hoarded royal bullion and jewel coffers at Sundar Pandi’s death without noting Polo’s inheritance taboo [@wassaf1871tarikh, pp. 34–35; @yc, 3.17 n. 6]. Both authors marvel at how generations of spice and pearl surpluses piled up in the Pandyan vaults.

[20] ~ Paying 500 gold *saggi* (over 100 silver marks) per imported charger reflects the high-value Gulf–South India cavalry trade [@yc, 3.17 n. 7; @wassaf1871tarikh, pp. 33–34]. Waṣṣāf prices each imported horse at 220 gold dinars of red gold, payable even for beasts that died on the passage.

[21] ~ Claiming over two thousand horses shipped annually per king with under a hundred surviving a year matches Polo’s high mortality estimate [@yc, 3.17 n. 7]. Tropical heat, monsoons, and lack of grazing pastures made southern India a graveyard for imported Arabian and Persian mounts.

[22] ✓ Waṣṣāf documents an actual Kisi–Maabar contract for 10,000 horses costing 2,200,000 dinars, noting heavy losses and physical decline aboard ship [@wassaf1871tarikh, pp. 33–34]. That Persian record independently confirms the scale and attrition of the Gulf horse traffic.

[23] ? Alleging that Gulf horse-dealers deliberately blocked farriers from sailing to India to protect their repeat sales is Polo’s mercantile deduction [@yc, 3.17 n. 7]. A merchant naturally looked for a cartel agreement behind an unending demand for replacement mounts.

[24] ? Blaming boiled rice, meat, and wry-legged colts on poor stable management leaves the underlying veterinary and fodder ecology open [@yc, 3.17 nn. 13, 17]. Waṣṣāf tells the same story of Indian grooms feeding warhorses boiled grain, butter, and cow's milk [@wassaf1871tarikh, pp. 33–34].

[25] ✓ Widow-burning (*satī*) on husbands’ funeral pyres is independently corroborated by contemporary medieval travellers and inscriptions [@yc, 3.17 n. 9]. Polo notes both the social praise heaped on widows who mounted the pyre and the ostracism of those who refused.

[26] ~ Twelve-knife votive self-immolation by condemned prisoners mixes attested South Indian sacrificial vows with Polo’s dramatic staging [@yc, 3.17 n. 8]. Religious self-decapitation and hook-swinging vows before fierce deities were recorded by both Arab and European witnesses.

[27] ~ Bare-bodied soldiers armed with only lance and shield and relying on Muslim residents to butcher animals highlight stark religious food taboos [@yc, 3.17]. The observation distinguishes Hindu purity rules against slaughtering cattle from meat consumption among certain castes.

[28] ✓ Washing the whole body twice daily before eating accurately records South Indian ritual ablutions remarked on by foreign visitors [@yc, 3.17]. Refusing to dine before bathing struck medieval Europeans as an extraordinary daily discipline.

[29] ✓ Ramusio-only. Eating strictly with the right hand and pouring water aloft from private drinking flasks (*kundikā* or *lotā*) without lip contact sit inside Ramusio’s brackets [@yc, 3.17]. Both customs remain everyday markers of traditional South Indian dining etiquette.

[30] ~ Polo describes an outcaste group he calls *Govy* and makes a sharp distinction between eating the flesh of an animal that died naturally and killing a cow [@yc, 3.17 n. 10]. Yule discusses the Tamil name *Pariah* and proposed etymologies for *Govy*.

[31] ✓ Plastering house floors with cow-dung and sitting directly on the clean packed earth reflect widespread Indian domestic hygiene and ritual reverence [@yc, 3.17 n. 11]. Dried cow-dung plaster provided a smooth, dust-free floor surface across the subcontinent.

[32] ~ Disqualifying wine-drinkers and seafaring men as legal witnesses or sureties reveals sharp local inland suspicion of transient sailors [@yc, 3.17]. Polo reports that a mariner was treated as a desperate fellow who would not be at sea if he were steady ashore.

[33] ~ Ramusio-only. Drawing an arresting circle around a debtor on the ground—and Polo’s claim to have seen the king himself halt on horseback inside one until he paid a merchant—originates in Ramusio [@yc, 3.17 n. 14]. Yule compares numerous accounts of this custom (*dharnā* or threshold arrest) along the Malabar and Coromandel coasts.

[34] ~ Limiting Maabar rains strictly to June, July, and August sketches the south-west monsoon contrast, though the eastern Coromandel coast actually receives its heaviest rains from the north-east monsoon in October–December [@yc, 3.17 n. 15]. See [Chart C11, the monsoon](ch-09-cocachin-return.md#fig-c11-monsoon-cocachin).

[35] ~ Halting journeys after an ill-timed sneeze and casting birth-hour horoscopes capture widespread Tamil divination and *pañcāṅga* astrology [@yc, 3.17 n. 16]. Every hour of the day and night carried its own auspicious or unlucky planet.

[36] ~ Ramusio-only. Thirteen-year-old boys buying beach pearls with twenty groats and reselling them indoors to shade-loving wholesalers appear solely in Ramusio [@yc, 3.17]. The vignette illustrates early apprenticeship inside South Indian merchant castes.

[37] ~ Consecrated temple dancers laying food dishes before shrines and singing before dining match historical *devadāsī* temple service [@yc, 3.17 n. 18]. Dedicating young girls to dance and chant at major temples was a well-documented institution under the Cholas and Pandyas.

[38] ~ Ramusio-only. Explaining those temple dances and songs as a peace-making remedy whenever the god and goddess quarrelled appears inside Ramusio’s addition [@yc, 3.17 n. 19]. The passage reflects popular temple festival rituals celebrating the reconciliation of divine consorts.

[39] ✓ Yule identifies the quiet coastal shrine town with Mailapur (Mylapore, today part of Chennai) and links the Muslim title *Avarian* for St Thomas to Arabic *ḥawārī* (“apostle”) [@yc, 3.18 nn. 1–2]. Both Eastern Christians and Muslims venerated the tomb site.

[40] ~ Pilgrims administering Mailapur’s red earth mixed in water against fevers is corroborated by John of Marignolli and eastern Syrian traditions cited by Yule [@yc, 3.18 n. 3]. Distributing blessed dust (*hnānā*) from martyrs' tombs was an ancient Church of the East practice.

[41] ~ Dating the granary miracle precisely to 1288 provides one of Polo’s rare chronological pegs on the South Indian leg [@yc, 3.18]. The year aligns with the late-1280s Indian Ocean mission inferred by Yule.

[42] ~ St Thomas appearing in a night vision with an iron fork at a local ruler's throat preserves living shrine legend over pilgrim lodging [@yc, 3.18]. Such protective saintly threats were a familiar genre at contested pilgrimage sanctuaries.

[43] ~ Ramusio-only. Church coconut groves paying six groats monthly per palm to one of the Pandyan king's brothers enter solely via Ramusio [@yc, 3.18]. The detail grounds the shrine's endowment in local palm-grove taxation.

[44] ? The Mailapur brethren’s tale of a stray peacock arrow fired by a *Govy* fowler is one local branch among diverse St Thomas martyrdom traditions [@yc, 3.18 n. 4]. Earlier Syriac *Acts of Thomas* described the apostle executed with lances by royal soldiers on a mount outside the city.

[45] ~ Weekly sesame-oil rubbing, pitch-black temple images paired with chalk-white demons, and wild-ox (*bos grunniens* or yak) tail-hair battle charms combine local observation with striking cultural inversion [@yc, 3.18 nn. 5–6]. Yak-tail fly-whisks and plume ornaments (*chauri*) imported from the northern mountains were prized military insignia across India.

[46] ✓ Heading north from Maabar opens the diamond-trading kingdom of Mutfili (Motupalli) at the opening of Chapter III-6 [@yc, 3.19 n. 1]. That coastal stage moves the narrative from Tamil Pandya country into the Telugu Kākatīya realm.
