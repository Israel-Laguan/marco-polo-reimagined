# Chapter II-13
## Kinsay: Capital of Manzi

> *“And since we have got thither I will enter into particulars about its magnificence; and these are well worth the telling, for the city is beyond dispute the finest and the noblest in the world.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 76 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

At Changan (Jiaxing) they weave sendal. Three days farther south, a trader carrying a bolt of it into Kinsay (Hangzhou) steps into a metropolis threaded from wall to wall with living water. A cargo lighter glides silently between timber houses below your boots. Glance up from its gunwale and a horse-cart rumbles across a high stone arch overhead. The Book crowns this metropolis the City of Heaven [1]. When you watch what it takes to keep loaded carts and freight barges moving past each other from sunrise to dusk, the builders on the ground deserve half the wonder.

Our first glimpse of Kinsay arrives through a desperate written appeal. When Bayan’s banners appeared outside the gates, the queen of Manzi (southern China) sent a detailed inventory of the city’s wealth to Bayan for Cublay (Kublai) Kaan, hoping the conqueror would spare so peerless a capital. Marco adds that he later walked those streets and verified her figures with his own eyes [2]. Imagine drafting that plea for a Mongol emperor who could either level the city to ash or pocket its taxes every autumn. You make sure every bridge, guildhall, and market square shines on the parchment.

A hundred miles round and twelve thousand stone bridges, the account begins—every arch vaulted high enough for masted vessels to glide cleanly underneath [3]. Cunningly graded street approaches allow heavy horse-carts to roll smoothly over the top [4]. Look at that engineering detail first before counting arches. Any carter who has ever backed a straining team down a steep humpback bridge recognizes at once what graded stone ramps do for city traffic. Other witnesses pull those headline numbers in different directions. Friar Odoric echoes the hundred-mile circuit and twelve thousand bridges, whereas Ibn Baṭṭūṭa paces three days across six walled quarters and Persian chroniclers count twenty-four parasangs and three hundred and sixty bridges. Later local gazetteers measure thirty-five *li* and a few hundred spans—revealing how swiftly visitors multiplied a canal city that dwarfed every town in Europe.

Down in the workshops, the Book sets twelve great craft guilds, each holding twelve thousand premises employing twelve to forty workmen apiece [5]. Master craftsmen and their wives live like gentlefolk off the labour of hired hands supplying distant markets. Even a guild master worth a hundred thousand gold bezants remains bound by ordinance to follow his father’s trade [6]. A fortune buys silk robes and lake villas; it still keeps your son’s name entered on the workshop roll.

Beyond the western walls gleams the West Lake, thirty miles around by Polo’s reckoning, ringed with palaces and temples and cradling two islands crowned with grand banqueting pavilions [7]. Any citizen planning a wedding feast crosses by boat to either island. There wait silver plate, trenchers, table linen, and napkins stacked ready for a hundred simultaneous wedding parties, each celebrating undisturbed in its own set of rooms [8]. Think of a shopkeeper who has spent all morning stepping round silk bales in a narrow alley. Out here on the water, somebody else has already set the silver bowls in order.

Turn round from one of those lake islands and look back over the sea of wooden houses. Rising among the timber roofs stand tall stone towers where families stow valuables against fire [9]. After the conquest, ten of the Kaan’s guards keep watch atop every bridge [10]. Night watchmen strike a wooden clapper and a metal basin to mark each hour. Patrols chalk the door of any house burning a lamp past curfew and haul night-walkers before the magistrates at dawn [11].

When sparks jump a timber roof, fire has a sharper signal. From a watchtower on a rise, a sentinel hammers a wooden board with a heavy mallet [12]. Guards sprint in from neighbouring bridges while householders drag iron-bound chests toward stone towers or load them onto lake boats bound for the islands [13]. In an emergency, the owner may step outdoors to save his goods while everyone else stays behind barred doors. By daylight, those same bridge watchmen carry the sick and infirm to hospitals built by the old kings and set idle hands to useful work [14].

Think for a moment of what it means to know the night noises of your own street. Through a shuttered wooden window in the dark, you hear the difference between two calm strikes on a bronze basin and a frantic mallet clatter from the watch-hill. That sound alone tells your household whether to turn over in bed or haul the silver chest straight down to the boat landing.

When winter rains lash the valley, Kinsay avoids turning into a mire thanks to stone-and-brick paved streets. Builders leave a soft unpaved lane along one flank for the Kaan’s galloping couriers and a gravelled centre strip drained neatly by vaulted culverts into the canals [15]. Off those avenues steam three thousand hot public bathhouses, the largest comfortably holding a hundred bathers at once [16]. Supplied by fresh spring water, they welcome citizens every day who prize clean linen.

Ask a sea-captain where salt water meets Kinsay’s commerce: twenty-five miles downriver stands the port of Ganfu (Ganpu, in the Haiyan area), welcoming great vessels straight from India up to the river quays [17]. Ask a treasury clerk how far the city’s ink reaches: Kinsay rules one of Manzi’s nine grand divisions with a hundred and forty cities under its seal [18]. Mixed garrisons of northern cavalry and infantry from Cathay (northern China) hold the region alongside Mongol troopers [19]. Those occupying soldiers are a sight the gentle, trade-loving townspeople still resent [20].

Every front doorway carries its own census. Householders tack up a name board listing every family member, servant, enslaved worker, and horse inside. They cross out names at a death and add fresh ink at a birth, while innkeepers keep a strict ledger of every arriving and departing guest [21]. Those door-boards lend an air of exact arithmetic to the Book’s staggering tally of one million six hundred thousand hearths [22]. Alongside them stand a single church of the Christians of the East [23] and crowds strolling in fine local and imported silks [24]. Look from the painted board above the lintel down to the silk robe in the doorway. The imperial tax clerk and the cloth merchant walk the exact same lane, counting two entirely different ledgers.

Before sailing or setting out on business, cautious citizens carry the exact hour and planet of their birth to street astrologers [25]. When death visits a house, mourners dressed in coarse hemp follow the bier with music. They cast cut-out paper horses, armour, gold coins, and silk robes into the funeral fire so the dead enter the next world richly furnished [26]—another tidy inventory entrusted to flame.

---

Step out toward the eastern edge of Kinsay to inspect the city’s great outer waterworks [27]. On that side, builders excavated a channel wide as a river to drain floodwaters safely away. They piled the dark spoil along its inner bank as an earthen rampart defending the city [28].

Now walk round behind the main market squares. Ten colossal markets—each half a mile on a side—sit neatly between the broad paved avenue in front and a parallel warehouse canal behind. On market days, forty or fifty thousand buyers jostle in each square [29]. Here a single Venetian groat buys two plump geese or four ducks [30], while fresh sea fish and lake carp empty off the stalls in a few hours. Shambles display calf, beef, kid, and lamb—curiously omitting pork from the grand meat list [31]. Nearby sit fragrant pears weighing ten pounds apiece, peaches in season, fresh rice wine, spices, and pearls, flanked by magistrate courts right on the square to settle trade disputes and inspect bridge patrols [32].

Pepper carries a ledger all its own. On the quay, a customs officer swears to Marco that Kinsay alone absorbs forty-three loads of black pepper every single day, each load weighing two hundred and twenty-three pounds [33]. Do the quick kitchen arithmetic in your head for a second: nearly ten thousand pounds of black spice emptying onto city docks between sunrise and supper. A sharp harbour clerk certainly knows how to count spice sacks. Yet a merchant who ships wholesale knows the real question to ask next: how many of those sacks stayed in Kinsay’s cooking pots, and how many rode right back out on canal barges?

Once market shutters close, wealth heads out onto the West Lake. Pleasure barges decked in timber carry furnished cabins seating tables and chairs for twenty guests. Crews pole them smoothly from the flat roof overhead while diners slide open painted windows to watch lakeside gardens and monasteries drift past [34]. On shore, six-seat curtained carriages roll down broad avenues to lakeside pavilions until dusk [35]. Anyone with silver to spare sees the City of Heaven from almost every angle except that of the sweating boatman poling overhead.

Walk beside an old merchant through the silent courtyards of Facfur’s former palace—once a sprawling complex of gardens, lakes, and a thousand gilded rooms. The old guide points toward ruined pavilions, vanished women’s courts, and crumbling garden walls [36]. He recalls vanished royal banquets that seated ten thousand guests in silk and gold. Standing beside weeds where a throne hall used to hum makes a conqueror’s splendour feel suddenly fragile. You smell wet cedar and moss where painted silk hangings once hung, and realize how swiftly an empire’s richest court turns into an old man’s memory.

---

Back inside the treasury counting-house, two immense revenue columns close the portrait of Kinsay. First stands salt: eighty *tomans* of gold, reckoned at five million six hundred thousand *saggi* [37]. That wealth flows from coastal evaporation pans where summer sun bakes enough brine to supply five neighbouring provinces besides [38]. You can credit a sharp imperial eye watching the coastal salt lagoons without swallowing every zero turned out by the gold conversion.

Clear salt from the desk, and the second column sweeps in cane sugar, rice wine, coal, and shop dues. Officers charge three and a third per cent on ordinary spices and wares [39], and ten per cent on silk and sea imports arriving from India [40]. All told, that second column reaches two hundred and ten *tomans*, or fourteen million seven hundred thousand *saggi* of gold [41]. The sum swells dramatically when a storyteller converts silver or paper *ting* units directly into pure gold coin. Some tellers send Marco personally to inspect those annual receipts; others simply have him listen closely while treasury clerks run the totals on their boards [42]. Either way, you hear the merchant’s son glowing with pride as he recites the largest balance sheet on thirteenth-century earth.

At the southern gate of Kinsay, the city walls fall behind, yet the Kaan’s tax ledger still holds the road. Follow the garden-lined highway south for one day through unbroken suburbs, arched bridges, and clacking silk looms. At the next walled market, shopkeepers still take the emperor’s black paper notes without a blink—and steep wooded hills rise ahead to carry us toward the sea.

## Notes

[1] ~ Kinsay is the former Sung capital at Hangzhou. The name represents a term for the capital, while “City of Heaven” belongs to the Book’s reading of it. See [Map A12, the canal spine](ch-10-manzi-sung.md#map-of-the-canal-spine) [@yc, 2.76 n. 1].

[2] ? The Book calls the queen’s written account the source for the description and adds that Polo later saw the city’s wonders for himself. Mascherpa identifies the claimed document with a dispatch of 1276. The attribution in the narrative cannot establish the document’s independent survival [@yc, 2.76; @mascherpa2024quinsai, p. 138].

[3] ✗ The 100 miles and 12,000 bridges come from the Yule-based Book, while Odoric gives similar figures (with variants across his texts) and Ibn Baṭṭūṭa gives a length of three days and six walled divisions in Lee’s translation (ch. XXIII) [@yc, 2.76 n. 2; @odoric1913travels, ch. 32, p. 195]. Yule himself cites the *Hang-chau Fu-Chi* (*Hangzhou fuzhi*) for a modern 35-*li* circuit (after 36 *li* under the Sui, 70 under the Tang, and about 100 under the Southern Song), Waṣṣāf’s 360 bridges, and Bishop G. E. Moule’s counts of 848 bridges across the department (*fu*) and 111 on a modern city map [@yc, 2.76 n. 2]. Mascherpa pairs 35 *li* with 347 bridges (A. C. Moule’s figure, reported second-hand as 117 in the city and 230 in the environs), though A. C. Moule’s pages and the underlying Chinese gazetteer counting boundaries remain uninspected here [@mascherpa2024quinsai, p. 138; @moule1957quinsai].

[4] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule brackets the design of the approaches that lets loaded traffic use the high bridges. The bridges and the claim about boats passing below are already in the base account [@yc, 2.76 n. 2].

[5] ✗ Polo assigns twelve thousand premises to each of twelve trades, and puts twelve or more men in each. The repeated scale puts an extraordinary number on the real concentration of crafts [@yc, 2.76].

[6] ~ The Book calls the inheritance of a father’s trade an ordinance binding even the wealthy. Yule considers a much less rigid custom more likely [@yc, 2.76 n. 3].

[7] ~ The West Lake and its waterside buildings are identifiable. Mascherpa gives the lake a circuit of about six miles, against the Book’s thirty, without establishing a set of identical limits for the two figures [@yc, 2.76 n. 4; @mascherpa2024quinsai, p. 138].

[8] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The tablecloths, napkins and hundred simultaneous parties are bracketed in Yule. The island halls and the plate for banquets belong to the earlier description [@yc, 2.76 n. 4].

[9] ~ The Book describes fireproof stores among wooden houses. It is a practical observation about protecting property in a city prone to fires [@yc, 2.76].

[10] ✗ The Book assigns the same guard strength to all twelve thousand bridges. The size of the force depends on accepting an implausible bridge count [@yc, 2.76 n. 2].

[11] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The bridge guards are in the base text. The hourly signals, marking of doors and night arrests are in the account Yule brackets [@yc, 2.76].

[12] ~ The alarm tower with its mallet and suspended board follows the bracketed bridge-watch account. Yule prints it in the base narrative [@yc, 2.76].

[13] ~ Ramusio-only. Boats to carry property to the islands are one of the responses to a fire in Ramusio’s description of the watch. The stone stores are already present in the earlier account [@yc, 2.76].

[14] ~ Ramusio-only. The description of the bridge guards’ duties includes taking people unable to work to hospitals and finding work for others. Yule’s notes compare the account of charitable provision with other descriptions [@yc, 2.76 n. 6].

[15] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The pavement is in the base account, and the provision for couriers and the street’s vaulted drains are additions bracketed by Yule [@yc, 2.76 n. 7].

[16] ~ Yule compares descriptions of public baths and records Ramusio’s different account, in which the inhabitants favour cold water. The number and capacity here are the Book’s estimates [@yc, 2.76 n. 8].

[17] ~ Ganfu is generally identified with Ganpu. Yule’s notes describe changes to the estuary and shipping arrangements that complicate a direct comparison with Polo’s account [@yc, 2.76 n. 9; @yc, 2.77 n. 2].

[18] ~ Polo counts nine divisions in the south and gives one hundred and forty cities to Kinsay. Yule’s note considers the administrative divisions behind these numbers [@yc, 2.76 n. 10].

[19] ~ The mixed composition of the garrisons matters more than the enormous headcount that follows from Polo’s figures. Those figures give a thousand as the minimum and sometimes ten or twenty times as many [@yc, 2.76 n. 11].

[20] ~ Ramusio-only. The continuation depicts a generally friendly city that welcomes foreign merchants and resents the occupying troops. That contrast is the account’s judgement of its inhabitants [@yc, 2.77 n. 8].

[21] ~ The physical detail of names above a door and the separate register for visitors belong to the Book’s account of how the government tracks a population. Yule cites comparisons with household registration [@yc, 2.76 n. 14].

[22] ✗ The Book counts 160 *tomans* of hearths, at ten thousand a *toman*. A population register and a number of this scale are separate claims [@yc, 2.76].

[23] ~ Polo reports a single church belonging to the Christians of the East. The number belongs to his description of the former capital [@yc, 2.76].

[24] ~ Ramusio-only. The bracketed description gives the people silk clothes and the district both local silk and imports. It leaves the quantities unspecified [@yc, 2.76 n. 5].

[25] ~ Polo describes recording a birth’s hour, planet and sign for use in astrological advice. Journey dates are one of the occasions the account gives for consulting an astrologer [@yc, 2.76].

[26] ✓ Yule’s notes discuss hemp mourning and the burning of representations of things for the dead. The Book describes the practice along with the return of the funeral music in the next world [@yc, 2.76 nn. 12–13].

[27] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule prints the long description separately. Mascherpa proposes a fuller, Z-related source for Ramusio’s account and discusses a family-notes hypothesis alongside possible interpolation [@yc, 2.77 n. 1; @mascherpa2024quinsai; @mascherpa2018fonte, pp. 62–63].

[28] ~ Ramusio-only. The relief channel and its embankment are part of Ramusio’s longer account. Yule compares older city plans showing a canal along the east and north sides, with the reported length open to question [@yc, 2.77 n. 3].

[29] ~ Ramusio-only. The text sets ten main squares between a broad road and a canal with warehouses. Yule compares descriptions of the canal and the squares and doubts the enormous dimensions and turnouts can be taken at face value [@yc, 2.77 nn. 3–4].

[30] ~ Ramusio-only. The price compares a Venetian silver coin to the buying power it would have for fowl in Kinsay. The cleared fish stalls belong to the account of how quickly the markets absorb supplies [@yc, 2.77 nn. 4–5].

[31] ? Ramusio-only. Ramusio’s list of the bigger animals sold at the shambles omits pigs. Yule points out that Odoric specifically mentions them in his account of this city. The reason for the omission is open [@yc, 2.77 n. 4; @odoric1913travels, ch. 32].

[32] ~ Ramusio-only. The text places shops and trades round the markets and gives the officers overseeing them jurisdiction over the bridge guards. The varieties of food and goods in the square are part of the same description [@yc, 2.77 nn. 4–6].

[33] ? Ramusio-only. The quantity comes from an unnamed customs officer in the text. Yule’s note includes observations of pepper in street cookery, which help place the commodity in the city without verifying the tally [@yc, 2.77 n. 7].

[34] ~ Ramusio-only. The flat-bottomed barges, their roof decks and the furnished cabins are features of the fuller account. The comparison to Chinese accounts of city life and lake outings gives context for the scene [@yc, 2.77 n. 9; @wu2015mengliang; @gernet1962daily].

[35] ~ Ramusio-only. The carriages furnish a second way of going out for recreation in the Venetian account. Their cushions, curtains and six places are the particulars it gives [@yc, 2.77 n. 10].

[36] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The palace’s scale appears in the earlier description. The old merchant’s tour and the recent alterations to the grounds belong to Ramusio. The Sung palace itself is known from Chinese sources [@yc, 2.76; @yc, 2.77; @moule1957quinsai].

[37] ✗ Yule’s text converts the salt income into eighty *tomans* of gold. Vogel’s examination of its bullion equivalent shows the problem with taking the printed total as an amount literally received in gold [@yc, 2.78 n. 2; @vogel2013marco, ch. 6].

[38] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule brackets the description of the coast’s lagoons, salt marshes and summer production. The large salt income is already present in the base account [@yc, 2.78].

[39] ~ The Book distinguishes ordinary duties at three and a third per cent from higher rates on goods such as silk. Its catalogue names several sources of revenue besides salt [@yc, 2.78 n. 2].

[40] ~ Ramusio-only. The ten per cent rate for seaborne imports is in brackets in Yule. The ten per cent on silk is in the base account [@yc, 2.78].

[41] ✗ The conversion of 210 *tomans* into gold poses the same problem as the salt figure. Yule considers the *ting*, an accounting unit with a silver basis, and Vogel compares what the figures imply when expressed in bullion [@yc, 2.78 n. 2; @vogel2013marco, ch. 6].

[42] ? Pauthier’s text has Polo sent to inspect the revenue, while the Geographic text has him hearing the calculations. Vogel weighs the stronger claim as part of his evaluation of the revenue account [@yc, 2.78 n. 1; @vogel2013marco, ch. 6].
