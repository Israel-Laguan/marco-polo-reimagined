# Front Matter II-1
## Yuan China: The World You Are Entering

> *"To this city also are brought articles of greater cost and rarity, and in greater abundance of all kinds, than to any other city in the world."*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 22 (Yule–Cordier)

Shake the Gobi dust from your cloak. Volume I marched us across mountains and singing sands like a caravan atlas with boots on, and left us in the tall grass at Chandu (Shangdu), watching Cublay (Kublai) Kaan scatter white mares’ milk to the sky. Now Volume II changes gear. We are walking through the palace gates into the most sophisticated state machine on thirteenth-century earth.

Before the doors of the great hall swing wide, take your bearings. Volume II moves in three grand acts. First, it shows how Cublay won and held his throne against a rebel kinsman on the steppe, then takes us inside his winter capital at Cambaluc (Beijing)—a world of twelve thousand palace guards, tigers riding in covered hunting carts, midnight assassination in a lighted hall, mulberry-bark paper money, three hundred thousand post-horses, and black stones that burn all night. Next, the Kaan sends his commissioner out along two great spokes of the imperial wheel: four months south-west into the river gorges, gold-dust valleys, and war-elephant forests of Yunnan and Mien (Burma), and then south-east down the Grand Canal into conquered Manzi (southern China), culminating in the twelve thousand bridges of Kinsay (Hangzhou) and the pepper wharves of Zayton (Quanzhou). Finally, it looks back across the frozen northern dark and the steppe battlefields where rival Mongol cousins challenge the Kaan’s peace.

Across these fifteen chapters, our two-layer pact matters more than ever. Marco walks palace corridors he saw with his own eyes and describes outlying provinces he knew partly from court table-talk; sixteenth-century scenes from Giovan Battista Ramusio’s 1559 Venice print enrich the stage and are flagged in the Notes as “Ramusio-only.” or “Ramusio-only in part.” Enjoy the court pageantry onstage, and let the Notes audit the headcounts backstage.

### Kublai's China — not "timeless Cathay"

The ruler at the centre of Volume II is Cublay, grandson of Chinghis (Chinggis Khan), Great Khan of the Mongol world in claim and emperor of a Chinese-style state in practice. He gave his state the Chinese dynastic name Yuan and built rule on a double foundation: Mongol conquest elite and Chinese administrative technique. Modern biographies alongside Yuan sources and Persian chronicles supply the outer historical frame [1].

![Cublay, known after his death as Shizu, in a fur-trimmed cap and a plain white coat. *(Album of Yuan emperors, National Palace Museum, Taipei. Detail via Shuge and Wikimedia Commons, marked public domain in the United States.)*](../media/public-domain/II-front_npm-yuan-album-khubilai.jpg){#fig-khubilai-portrait width=76%}

Two capitals anchor the volume. **Chandu** is the summer court you met at the end of Volume I—hunting parks, ceremonial horses, and religious specialists. **Cambaluc** (Khanbaliq / Dadu, today’s Beijing) is the winter seat: walls within walls, palace compounds, markets, and an empire-wide bureau taxing grain, salt, and trade on a scale Latin cities could barely imagine [2].

Polo’s Cambaluc is neither a Qing postcard nor a Song scroll. It is a Mongol imperial capital raised on the North China plain, staffed by Mongols, Central Asians, northern Chinese subjects, and foreign specialists—including, if the Book is believed, the occasional Venetian on imperial errands. When Volume II marvels at palace marble and bustling suburbs, listen for a guest describing power’s theatre.

### Dadu and Shangdu — seasonal gravity

Think of Yuan sovereignty as seasonal gravity. Summer pulled the court north to Chandu’s cooler plateau; autumn and winter pulled it back behind Cambaluc’s walls. Hunt calendars, birthday banquets, and palace architecture snap into focus once you let the court move—driven by the *yam* postal relay of fresh horses, hostels, and inscribed *paiza* tablets [3].

When Volume II speaks of twelve thousand Keshican guards or matching robes of beaten gold, you are hearing court anthropology as a merchant-guest remembered it: impressed, sometimes rounding up to chivalric scale, yet sharp on logistics and colour. Believe the spectacle; audit the headcount in the Notes.

### The Sung conquest horizon

While Cublay’s northern machine hummed, the conquest of the Southern Song—Polo’s **Manzi**—was newly finished business. Saianfu (Xiangyang) fell in the 1270s; Kinsay surrendered in 1276; residual Song resistance ended in a sea battle off the Canton coast early in 1279. Volume II’s southern chapters walk through a newly pacified world where the Saianfu mangonel story even claims a Polo-family cameo—a claim historians still test [4].

When the Book praises Kinsay’s lakes, stone bridges, and household tax registers, it is describing the old Song capital under fresh Mongol management: wonder is conquest’s inheritance as much as eternal splendour.

### Paper money, coal, and the *yam* world

Volume II is also where Europe first met everyday Yuan wonders that still startle: stamped mulberry-bark currency, black burning stones, and an empire-wide postal relay—what Polo will describe, and why it astonished Latin readers [5].

### How to listen in Volume II

Trust bridges, post-stations, paper notes, and market traffic more readily than round army totals. Treat governorship boasts and family siege heroics as contested until the Notes weigh in. Keep the Book’s own names in your ear; modern equivalents sit beside them in brackets on first mention so you never lose your footing on the map.

You are entering a world where a Venetian could watch a Mongol emperor feast in robes of one colour, pay an innkeeper with mulberry-bark paper, burn black stones against the winter cold, and ride south into a canal metropolis larger than any city in Christendom. That is the Volume II stage. Step back onto the grass beyond Chandu first—where battle horns are blowing, an elephant-borne wooden fortress is lumbering into line, and a rebel prince carrying the Christian cross on his banner is about to test what Cublay Kaan’s throne is made of.

## Notes

[1] ✓ Cublay gave his state the Chinese dynastic name Yuan and ruled as both Mongol Great Khan and Chinese-style emperor [@yc, 2.1 n. 1; @rossabi1988khubilai, ch. 5]. Allsen [-@allsen1997commodity] examines how the court marshalled artisans, textiles, and administrators across the wider Mongol commonwealth.

[2] ✓ Chandu (Shangdu) and Cambaluc (Khanbaliq / Dadu) functioned as the dual seasonal capitals of the Yuan realm [@yc, 1.61; @yc, 2.10-11; @steinhardt1990chinese]. Both walled sites have been surveyed by modern Chinese archaeologists; see [Map A9, Dadu city plan](ch-02-person-palace.md#fig-a9-dadu-city-plan). Xu [-@xu2020dadu] reviews a century of Dadu reconstruction work: much of the palace layout is still inferred, and Ming builders moved the north wall south after 1368, so Polo's Cambaluc does not map neatly onto later Beijing.

[3] ✓ The *yam* postal relay network linked the capitals to the provinces through horse stations, foot-runners, and metal tablets of authority (*paiza*) [@yc, 2.26]. Chinese administrative codes and Persian chronicles corroborate the system in detail; see [Chapter II-5, Notes 20-25](ch-05-ahmad-paper-coal.md#notes).

[4] ✓ The final conquest of the Southern Song ran from the siege of Xiangyang (1268-73) and Bayan's capture of Hangzhou (1276) to a sea battle off the Canton coast early in 1279 [@yc, 2.65, 2.70]. The Book's claim that the Polos supplied the counterweight trebuchets at Saianfu clashes with the siege chronology; see [Chapter II-11, Notes 21-37](ch-11-yanju-saianfu.md#notes).

[5] ✓ Yuan paper money began with the Zhongtong issue of 1260; in 1287 the new Zhiyuan notes were officially set at five Zhongtong each, and the old notes stayed in use [@guan2024yuan]. Details are in [Chapter II-5, Notes](ch-05-ahmad-paper-coal.md#notes).
