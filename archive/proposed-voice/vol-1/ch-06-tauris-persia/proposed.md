# Chapter I-6
## Tauris, Persia of the Three Kings, and the Eight Kingdoms

> *“In Persia is the city of Saba, from which the Three Magi set out when they went to worship Jesus Christ.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 13 (Yule–Cordier)

---

The silk and gold cloth of Baudas (Baghdad) turns up again under the vaulted skylights of Tauris (Tabriz)—and it has glittering company. Hold a ruby or a pearl up where the street-light cuts through the bazaar dust, and you see why Genoese and Latin merchants rode this far inland to trade alongside goods hauled up from India, Baudas, and Cremesor (the Persian Gulf coastlands) [1]. By 1280 Genoese factors were already well established in the city, drawn by the crossroads where Indian Ocean sea-lanes met the Anatolian caravan highways—though exactly when young Marco himself first walked these alleys remains an open question [2]. What a merchant cares about first is simple: Tauris puts a silk-weaver within arm’s reach of a Gulf spice-factor and a Latin buyer with silver in his purse. A gemstone lets you carry a fortune inside your belt without hiring another packhorse—provided you know a true stone from glass when a dealer lays it in your palm.

See [the map of the outbound road, I-3](ch-03-second-setting-forth.md#map-of-the-outbound-road).

Tauris actually stands in Azerbaijan, yet the Book places it inside Yrac (Iraq)—a geographical slip born from hearing the Mongol sovereign of Persia styled the lord of Iraq, so that a royal title drifted onto the capital itself [3].

Step outside the city gates among the irrigated orchards, and the Book marvels at the size and sweetness of the fruit watered by clear channels [4]. Its welcome for the townsmen is far frostier. While distinguishing Armenians from Georgians and Nestorians from Jacobites, it lumps all Muslim townsfolk together as Taurizi and damns them in a single breath as faithless, quarrelsome, and hard to deal with [5]. A buyer inspecting a ruby in a shaded booth had better judge the individual dealer before him rather than trust a blanket travelogue curse.

Stand inside that covered bazaar for a moment and listen to the din. Persian brokers shout exchange rates in silver; Nestorian clerks tally spice bales off the Gulf; Genoese factors pinch raw silk and weigh pearls against Venetian ducats. Whatever a traveller thinks of the locals’ manners, Tauris is where the commerce of half a continent changes hands beneath one roof.

Look closer at those pearls and rubies changing hands across the wooden counters. A single pearl merchant arriving from the Gulf carries his whole season’s profit knotted inside a linen handkerchief tucked into his sash. Beside him sits a silk factor with twenty mule-loads of raw thread stacked in the caravanserai courtyard, praying his beasts do not go lame on the climb toward the Black Sea. Under the vaulted brick roof of Tauris, ocean trade and overland caravan trade shake hands every morning over small scales and silver coins.

From Tauris, the road into Persia stretches twelve days [6]. Before riding out, look down at a humbler craft than gold brocade: the monks of St Barsamo (Barsauma), dressed in habits like Carmelites, spend their days knitting woollen belts, lay them on the altar during Mass, and give them away on their rounds to soothe aching backs and joints, so that neighbours beg for one [7]. The Book places this house on the borders of Tauris, though the famous monastery of St Barsauma stood far to the west near Malatia [8]. Still, anyone riding weeks through mountain cold with stiff knees and bruised ribs was glad enough to tuck a blessed woollen belt inside his tunic.

---

Persia opens with a grim reminder of what the Mongol campaigns left behind: once-splendid cities laid waste across the plateau [9]. Who remains to answer a stranger’s questions? At Saba (Saveh), stand beside Marco as he peers at three tombs standing side by side beneath a square pavilion, their dead supposedly still keeping hair and beard. The Book names them Jaspar, Melchior, and Balthasar; the townsfolk he questions shrug and say only that three kings were buried there long ago [10]. It is a maddeningly thin answer when you rode in with the entire Christmas story in your head.

Three days down the road, he has much better luck at Cala Ataperistan—the Castle of the Fire-worshippers, a fortress whose Persian name translates cleanly enough even if its ruins remain unlocated [11].

![At Saba, travellers ask about three tombs that local tradition in the Book associates with the Magi. *(Modern illustration in the manner of Ilkhanid Persian manuscript painting. Not a historical artifact. This edition.)*](../media/plates/I-6-saba-magi-tombs.png){#fig-i-6-saba-magi-tombs}

Here the people tell a tale worth every mile of the ride. Long ago, three kings set out to see a newborn child, carrying gold for a king, incense for God, and myrrh for a physician—each gift meant to test who the infant truly was. Before laying down their offerings, each king enters the chamber alone: the youngest sees a youth of his own age, the middle-aged king sees a man in his prime, and the eldest sees an old man. Puzzled, they compare notes outside, march in together, and find a thirteen-day-old babe [12].

The child accepts all three gifts and hands them a closed box in return. Days later, curiosity wins out and they pry the lid open. Inside lies a stone—meant to sign them to keep their new faith as firm as rock. Missing the point entirely, they toss it down a well, and straightway fire drops from heaven into the water after it [13]. Picture three crowned kings peering over a well-lip: no retrieving the stone, yet they have been handed a living flame to carry home, provided they never let it die.

It is a dazzling piece of storytelling—half Christian Epiphany, half Persian fire-temple legend. Where the townsfolk of Saba gave Marco three silent graves and a shrug, the keepers down the road handed him a complete origin story for why their altars never went cold. They bore that fire home and enshrined it in a rich church; if it ever goes out, riders travel up to ten days to fetch a fresh spark from co-religionists [14]. The castle claims one king’s grave, Saba a second, and Avah (Āvah) the third [15]—even though all three bodies had just been tucked under one roof at Saba. Listen from the priest tending the altar and you hear where his flame came from; listen from the Feast of the Epiphany and you recognize the gifts, two traditions braided together along the caravan road [16].

---

A merchant setting out again needs to know who holds the road. The Book counts eight kingdoms in Persia—a loose label spanning cities, peoples, and local principalities where real local rulers like the two atabegs of Luristan rose or fell at the Ilkhan’s nod [17]. From north to south, the roll runs through Casvin (Qazvin), Curdistan (Kurdistan), the twin principalities of Lor (Luristan), Suolstan (Shūlistān), Istanit—probably a copyist’s slip for Isfahan [18]—Serazy (Shiraz), Soncara (Shabānkāra), and Tonocain (Tun and Qāʾen) out on the eastern desert rim.

Think of that eight-kingdom roll as a caravan master’s mental map rather than a tidy royal atlas. On parchment, a scribe lists eight names in a row; in the saddle, each name means a different dialect at the gate, a different silver coin at the moneychanger’s bench, and a different garrison captain asking whose seal is stamped on your safe-conduct. A caravan master needed to know which lord still held his citadel before trusting last season’s pass.

What you earn on the road depends as much on the cargo as the route. Try pricing a fine Persian warhorse still on its hooves in the yard: the Book quotes a sum a French listener knew well—about two hundred livres tournois, the price of a prime charger in Europe [19]. A large, fast-ambling ass fetches thirty marks of silver [20], because a beast that glides without jarring your spine over rough gravel is worth a small fortune to a rider facing weeks in the saddle.

Those horses march south in strings to Kisi (Kish) and Hormos (Old Hormuz, called Curmosa here), where dealers meet sea-captains bound for India [21]. Picture what it takes to move a string of high-bred horses across barren ridges to the coast. Every halt means bargaining for barley and sweet water; every rocky ravine risks a sprained fetlock that turns a two-hundred-livre charger into dead weight. A groom has to watch every hoof at dawn, check every saddle-sore at dusk, and sleep beside the picket line with one ear cocked for horse-thieves. By the time a horse steps onto a ship at Hormos, its price already carries the sweat of the road.

Merchants here ride armed. Despite Mongol rule, homicides happen daily along the highways and unguarded caravans are cut to pieces [22]. Yet step inside the walled towns along that same road and you find weavers turning out silk and cloth of gold beside abundant cotton, grain, fruit, and wine [23]. Someone sleeping beside cargo bales with one hand on his sword sees a harsher Persia than the factor dining inside the bazaar. That double vision—wealth inside the city gate, sudden danger outside it—is the rhythm of every Persian stage in the Book, where a caravan master counts his guards as carefully as his silver coins.

Even the wine comes with a dinner-table riddle. How do Muslim hosts drink it when their law forbids it? Boil the grape juice down, locals explain, until it thickens, changes taste, and earns a new name [24]. Imagine a Venetian used to wine at every meal staring into a dark, sweet cup beside his bread and asking how his host squared it with the law—only to hear the kind of practical workaround Marco loved to jot down. A host may well pour you a cup of boiled must in a shaded courtyard, even if taking one city’s custom for all of Persia steps ahead of the evidence.

---

At Yasdi (Yazd), ask for a silk that carries the city’s own name—*Yasdi*—which merchants pack onto camels and ship far abroad [25]. Beyond the walls, seven days across a level plain offer only three roofed halts for the night. There is sport along the way: picture a rider slipping the hood off his hawk for a quick flight after partridges and quail while wild asses bound through the scrub [26] and dates hang from the palms [27]. A caravan master riding out of Yasdi learns to time every day’s march between those three lonely caravanserais, keeping his hawks fed and his camel-strings tight before dusk settles over the gravel.

Before leaving the plain of Yasdi, look back once at those three lonely halts. Seven days across open gravel teaches a caravan master to ride with a hawk on his wrist for sport and a blade at his hip, because the finest brocade in Persia still has to cross empty scrub between one walled market and the next. Every mile of that road reminds a merchant that luxury cloth only reaches a European hall when armed riders bring it safely across the dust.

At the end of the seventh day, carry that Yasdi silk through the gates of Kerman (Kermān), where women and their daughters are waiting with gold thread and needles to stitch birds and beasts across it.

[1] ✓ Silk and gold cloth, precious stones and overseas trade are central to Polo’s market. Yule compares Odoric, who also describes the city’s trade, and explains Cremesor as Garmsīr [@yc, 1.11 nn. 2–3].

[2] ~ The date 1280 comes from a historian’s account of Genoese trade. It gives evidence of the commercial setting, while the precise date and occasion of Marco’s visit remain open [@bernardini2000genoa; @yc, 1.11 n. 3].

[3] ✗ The city is in Azerbaijan. Yule uses the title for the Ilkhan in Ibn Baṭṭūṭa to suggest how the geography might have been confused [@yc, 1.11 n. 1].

[4] ✓ The gardens and fruit appear in Polo’s description. Yule compares other visitors’ accounts of the cultivated surroundings [@yc, 1.11 n. 5].

[5] ~ Polo records different communities in the city. The condemnation of its native townsmen and the parallel Yule draws from Mustawfi belong to the writers who make those judgements [@yc, 1.11 n. 4].

[6] ~ Shared by the V witness and Ramusio. Ramusio supplies the stage Yule places in brackets, and the Venetian witness also has twelve days [@yc, 1.11; @simion2019devisement, 16.9].

[7] ~ The Book describes the knitting, the blessing and the use of the girdles for pain. The comparison with the Carmelite habit is for the benefit of its Latin audience [@yc, 1.12].

[8] ? Yule favours the celebrated Jacobite house near Malatia. The distance from Tauris and the number of dedications to Barsauma leave the question of the actual house open [@yc, 1.12 n. 1].

[9] ~ The damage of the Mongol campaigns belongs to the historical setting. Polo opens his account with a verdict on the whole country and goes on to describe substantial communities and trade [@yc, 1.13].

[10] ~ The Book gives the traditional Latin names and reports the state of the bodies. It distinguishes that account from the much more limited answer Polo says he got in Saba [@yc, 1.13; @yc, 1.14 n. 3].

[11] ? The Persian name is glossed as the Castle of the Fire-worshippers. The distance belongs to Polo’s reckoning, and even the place’s location on the outward or homeward route is uncertain [@yc, 1.13 n. 1; @yc, 1.14 n. 3].

[12] ~ Gold, incense and myrrh signify king, God and physician in the story. Yule notes a different traditional explanation of the myrrh and a separate tradition that assigned distinct ages to the Magi [@yc, 1.13 n. 2; @yc, 1.14 n. 1].

[13] ~ The stone and the fire are episodes of the story Polo reports. The stone’s meaning is given to the Book’s reader while the kings fail to understand it [@yc, 1.14].

[14] ~ The Book describes how people keep and renew the fire. Its description of worship in the story is distinct from what the comparisons establish about Zoroastrian practice [@yc, 1.14 n. 2].

[15] ~ The local version assigns each king to a place. Abbott’s later report places Avah about sixteen miles south-south-east of Saba, though he took a bearing without visiting it [@yc, 1.14 n. 3].

[16] ? Yule compares the treatment of the gifts in the Latin tradition and traditions of a sacred fire, including the account in Ammianus. Their recurrence gives scope for comparison and leaves the sources of the tale Polo tells open [@yc, 1.13 n. 2; @yc, 1.14 nn. 1–3].

[17] ✓ Yule identifies the eight names and gives their extent where possible. Spuler’s account of Great and Little Lur gives examples of Ilkhanid confirmation and deposition of local atabegs [@yc, 1.15 n. 1; @spuler1987atabakan].

[18] ~ Ramusio has *Spaan*. Yule suggests a handwriting confusion between *sf* and *st* and expressly leaves the ending of *Istanit* unexplained [@yc, 1.15 n. 1].

[19] ~ Polo allows the horse price to vary with quality. Yule calculates the two hundred livres as about £193 on the gold basis he uses, a comparison in his own historical terms [@yc, 1.15 n. 2].

[20] ~ The thirty silver marks are Polo’s estimate for an exceptionally fine ass. His notice singles out the amble and speed [@yc, 1.15].

[21] ✓ The Gulf trade in horses bound for India recurs in the Book. Polo names the two entrepôts and describes the change of hands there [@yc, 1.15 n. 2; @yc, 3.17].

[22] ~ The Book distinguishes government protection from the insecurity a merchant could still encounter. The claim of a homicide every day is its generalisation [@yc, 1.15 n. 3].

[23] ~ The list of crafts and produce is Polo’s account of the towns. It puts a trading and manufacturing population alongside his account of danger on the road [@yc, 1.15].

[24] ~ Shared by Zelada and Ramusio. The objection about the prohibition and the response about boiling occur in both. Yule compares later accounts of wine and boiled drinks [@moule1938description, ch. 33, pp. 117–118; @yc, 1.15 n. 4].

[25] ✓ Polo gives the fabric the name of the city. Yule finds the name used in Persian for a cloth from Yasdi [@yc, 1.16 n. 1].

[26] ~ The stages, shelter and game are given as Polo records them. Yule considers more than one line for the road [@yc, 1.16 n. 2].

[27] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule prints the production of dates in brackets; it is a detail of his reading of the woods, while their planting and the road’s precise line remain uncertain [@yc, 1.16 n. 2].
