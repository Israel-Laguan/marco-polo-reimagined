# Chapter I-9
## Balc, Badashan, Pashai, Keshimur: Rubies, Azure, and High Valleys

> *“It is in this province that those fine and valuable gems the Balas Rubies are found… There is also in the same country another mountain, in which azure is found; ’tis the finest in the world.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 29 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Troops that have spent months starving out a mountain fortress ride into a very different world on the road to Sapurgan (Shibarghan). Thick grass spreads beneath the horses’ hooves, while orchards and grainfields keep riders fed. Yet the Book counts six days across towns and villages interrupted by fifty- or sixty-mile dry stretches where every man must lash water-skins to his saddle [1]. Look at the march through a quartermaster’s eyes first: halt where grazing is lush rather than drive gaunt beasts forward on empty bellies. Then look through a merchant’s: every water-skin strapped to a pack-saddle steals space from silk or spice, forcing you to trade payload for survival. Both pasture and thirst belong in the same march, even if squeezing them neatly into six days takes some doing.

In Sapurgan, watch what cooks do with a melon. A knife circles the rind, peeling off a long spiral ribbon to lay in the sun until the moisture dries out. Fresh melon spoils in a day; cured into golden strips, it travels to distant bazaars and arrives *"sweeter than honey"* [2]. A pack-mule that cannot drink until tomorrow’s well can still carry a taste of last summer into the next kingdom, and a trader can sell it long after the harvest is over.

Beyond Sapurgan rises Balc (Balkh), its shattered marble palaces still hinting at the splendour Mongol armies broke when they swept through [3]. Generations later, travellers walking among those fallen mosques and colleges would still trace bright paint clinging to the broken plaster [4]. Local storytellers pointed out a prouder boast: here, they swore, Alexander married Darius’s daughter [5]. A royal wedding in a tale keeps a city’s name shining long after its bazaar roofs have caved in and its streets have emptied.

Here the Book draws the frontier between Persia and the Tartars of the Levant. Eastward it drops a single place-name, Dogana—likely a copyist’s tangle from the next town [6]—before warning riders to pack twelve days of rations across country emptied by raiders into the hills [7], with lions prowling the river-scrub along the approach [8]. Between Sapurgan’s dried melons, Balc’s broken marble, and twelve days of packed bread, the road teaches you in three stages how swiftly a landscape shifts from feast to ruin.

At Taican (Taloqan), a hungry rider heads straight for the grain stalls, though the real wonder lies south in the hills. There the rock salt is so dense it takes an iron pick to break it loose. Caravans ride in from thirty days away to load it, and the Book boasts the mountain could *"supply the whole world to the end of time"* [9]. Meanwhile, neighbouring hills yield almonds and pistachios cheap enough for anyone [10]. Knowing salt sits in a cliff is one thing; turning a profit on it after a thirty-day ride—quarrying blocks, lashing them onto mules, and choosing whether salt or nuts pay better at the next market—is pure merchant calculation. Even a humble salt-block carries the arithmetic of pack-animals and road-days in its weight.

Three days farther bring vineyards, orchards, and settled villages where folk drink boiled wine, wind cords round their heads instead of hats, and stitch coats and shoes from trapped pelts [11]. At Casem (Kishm), a river cuts past hillside dwellings hollowed straight out of the earth [12]. If you need a cobbler to mend a torn boot here, his front door may be a hole cut in a riverbank, with cattle grazing above his roof. Out in the brush, cornered porcupines supposedly huddle together and fire their quills at the hunting hounds [13], though a dog trotting back with a muzzle full of spines never proves the porcupine actually shot them. Three foodless, waterless days beyond Casem bring the caravan at last to Badashan (Badakhshan), where the valleys narrow and the mountain air turns sharp.

---

The kings of Badashan also claim Alexander’s bloodline, tracing their house to his wedding with Darius’s daughter and styling themselves Zulcarnain—the Two-Horned [14]. In Balc, that marriage was civic nostalgia; up here, it serves as a crown’s title deed, dusted off whenever a new prince mounts the throne and demands the loyalty of mountain clans.

Look up into the cliffs of Mount Syghinan, where miners tunnel for red stones the way other men dig for silver: balas rubies, known to modern mineralogists as spinels [15]. In the Book, the king guards the entire output under strict monopoly, issuing gems only as royal gifts, tribute, or controlled sales; any subject who digs for himself forfeits life and goods. The explanation sounds straight out of a Venetian counting-house: flood the market, and the price collapses [16]. Picture a king weighing what gift to send a dangerous neighbour. Bales of silk eat up pack-mules and can be bought in any bazaar; a single red stone travels in one courier’s palm and comes from nowhere else on earth.

Pause beside that mine mouth for a moment, because it is one of the sharpest economic lessons in the Book. Most medieval chroniclers treat precious gems as gifts of fortune or magic talismans. A Venetian merchant looks straight past the sparkle at the supply chain: restrict the digging, control the exports, and a mountain of red spinel keeps its value across every bazaar from Badashan to Venice.

A second mountain yields veins of blue like ore—the finest lapis lazuli in the world [17]. A painter grinding ultramarine in a European workshop rarely pictures the pickaxe work in these freezing cliffs, where miners chip blue stone by lamplight beside silver veins and carry it down narrow tracks while winter cold waits on the pass. Think of the blue robes of the Virgin in an Italian fresco: the pigment that lit up a church wall in Padua or Florence began in a freezing Badashan gallery half a world away.

Unshod horses gallop across these rocky tracks without splitting a hoof, tearing downhill at a pace no lowland charger could survive [18]. Local lore tells of a royal breed marked on the forehead and descended from Bucephalus. When the king coveted the stud and killed their owner for refusing to sell, the widow destroyed every horse in the stable [19]. The finest bloodline in the kingdom became priceless the moment nobody was left to breed or sell it—a grim reminder that even a king’s greed cannot command what a defiant household chooses to erase.

Climb for a full day above the gorges, and the trail opens onto a high plateau of lush grass, trees, and cold springs where trout dart and wild sheep graze in flocks of hundreds [20]. Sick travellers climb up from the stifling lowlands and recover in two or three days, as if the thin mountain air washed the fever straight out of their bones. Here Marco himself lingered after a stubborn fever had dogged him for nearly a year, finding his strength again beside the cold springs [21]. Picture the contrast between those two climbs. Down in the Syghinan galleries, guards search every miner’s pockets for a hidden spinel; up on the grass plateau, a feverish merchant sits by a trout stream with nothing to buy and nothing to guard, waiting simply to breathe.

Sakers and lanners fly above these ridges for noble sport, while clothing prices reveal the gap between rich and poor. Most folk wear animal skins, yet a wealthy woman wraps sixty, eighty, or even a hundred ells of fine cotton into a single pair of pleated trousers [22]. Every bolt of that cotton must be hauled through the same tight mountain defiles the Book trusts to stop invading armies [23]. Where the ground flattens, farmers raise wheat and huskless barley, pressing sesame and walnuts for lamp and cooking oil [24]. Once a traveller reaches the settlements, the country feeds him well—provided he survives the approaches and carries enough silver to buy what the passes let through.

Pause to picture those hundred-ell trousers beside a shepherd’s sheepskin cloak. In a high mountain kingdom with no olive groves and narrow, snow-choked passes, imported cotton cloth becomes a walking bank account: the richer the household, the more yards of woven fabric gather in pleats around the waist, announcing to the whole bazaar that this family can afford what pack-mules hauled up from the plains.

---

Ten days south of Badashan lies the warm country of Pashai (the Pashai country, with its extent disputed), where men wear gold and silver studded with pearls, dine on meat and rice, and carry a fearsome name for sorcery [25]. The jewellery is easier to credit than the spells; neither fixes the province’s borders on a map, yet both show how lowland travellers pictured the southern valleys.

Seven days farther toward India rises Keshimur (Kashmir), a temperate, forested realm guarded by narrow passes under its own independent ruler [26]. Its masters, rumour claims, can make temple idols speak and call down storms at will, leading the Book to call the valley the fountainhead of all idolatry [27]. Such was the reputation that travelled ahead of the valley long before any European walked its streets or heard a local monk explain his faith. Behind those tales of storm-calling priests stood a real Himalayan crossroads of monasteries, manuscripts, and mountain trade routes linking India to the Tibetan plateau.

Monasteries and hermits dot the Kashmiri hills, keeping strict vows of abstinence [28]—though devout locals who refuse to slaughter an animal still eat meat if a Muslim butcher wields the knife [29], the kind of practical market workaround a curious visitor spots at once. Better still for a Mediterranean merchant is one golden ledger fact: Keshimur pays handsomely for red coral [30]. Give a trader that single tip, and he remembers the name long after the talking idols fade.

Think of what coral meant to a Venetian merchant listening to this chapter. Mediterranean divers hauled red coral branches out of warm shallow seas off Naples, Sicily, and North Africa. A trader could pack a whole fortune of polished coral beads into a single leather saddle-pouch, carry it safely across Persia and the Afghan passes, and trade it in Keshimur or Tebet (Tibet) for silver, musk, or spices. Two distant highlands suddenly belong in the same account book, united by lightweight red beads that cross half a continent without spoiling. Notice how the Book’s eye shifts scale across these valleys: from a royal monopoly over one ruby cliff in Badashan to a handful of red beads traded across two mountain kingdoms.

Pack that coral away for the road toward India. Now the Book turns back to Badashan, where a rushing mountain river cuts the only path higher into the range. From here, we follow the clear water upstream toward the roof of the world, leaving the warm orchards and shady gardens behind. Every mile climbs closer to the snowline and the high passes beyond, where villages thin out, winter winds sharpen, and every ration must be weighed.

## Notes

[1] ~ Polo describes fertile stages interrupted by waterless stretches. Yule finds the six days difficult as a continuous itinerary from the preceding episode and discusses the possibility of a gap in the route [@yc, 1.26; @yc, 1.27].

[2] ✓ Yule assembles parallels for the fame of the melons and for drying strips of fruit in the region. The sweetness belongs to Polo’s own estimate. The procedure is given without a cultivar [@yc, 1.26 n. 1].

[3] ✓ Polo gives ruined marble buildings as signs of Balc’s former wealth. The Mongol destruction is documented and later travellers found a city still bearing its effects [@yc, 1.27 n. 1].

[4] ✓ Yule cites Ibn Baṭṭūṭa on ruined buildings of Balc, including traces of former decoration. He found the city still largely laid waste in the fourteenth century [@yc, 1.27 n. 1].

[5] ✗ The legend puts Alexander’s marriage here and calls his wife Darius’s daughter. Roxana was the daughter of Oxyartes of Bactria, and the story joins different episodes of Alexander’s life [@yc, 1.27 n. 1].

[6] ✗ Some texts give a form such as *Dogaua*, with no description of the place. Pelliot follows Benedetto in treating it as a spurious name, probably produced from the phrase naming Taican [@yc, 1.27 n. 2; @benedetto1928milione; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Dogava”].

[7] ~ The Book puts the flight from the low country down to raids and armies. Twelve days without supplies is its estimate of the exposed stage [@yc, 1.27 n. 3].

[8] ? Yule compares several accounts of lions in the country towards the Oxus, including reports relayed by Moorcroft and Lieutenant John Wood. The word and the route have both drawn scrutiny in attempts to identify what travellers encountered [@yc, 1.27 n. 3].

[9] ✓ Taican is usually identified with Taloqan, and rock salt is worked in the region. The picture of traders coming from very far away is Polo’s measure of an important market, while the claim to supply the world is his flourish. Yule records works east and south-east of the town [@yc, 1.28 nn. 1–2].

[10] ~ Ramusio-only. The bracketed addition describes plentiful almonds and pistachios in neighbouring hills. The grain market and the salt are already in the earlier account [@yc, 1.28 n. 2].

[11] ~ Polo disparages the people in this stretch of the narrative. The cord head-dress, skins for clothing and boiled wine are the portable particulars, for which Yule cites later regional parallels [@yc, 1.28 n. 3].

[12] ~ Yule identifies Casem with Kishm and compares cave dwellings observed in other districts. The comparison gives an idea of the dwellings Polo is describing, with the province’s size and exact government open [@yc, 1.28 nn. 4, 6].

[13] ✗ The claim that the porcupines discharge their quills is an old legend. A dog close enough to be pricked has all the evidence of the danger that the Book’s dogs have [@yc, 1.28 n. 5].

[14] ✗ Alexander descent is a royal claim of prestige in this report. The title of the Two-Horned is connected with Alexander, while the descent and its marriage belong to local tradition [@yc, 1.29 n. 1].

[15] ✓ The balas ruby is red spinel, and Kuh-i-Lal is a major source in the historic region. The modern survey identifies the gem and its source without establishing how the medieval workings were organised [@yc, 1.29 n. 2; @vertriest2019field].

[16] ~ The monopoly in Polo’s account keeps the rights to mine and export with the ruler. The penalty and the deliberate restriction of supply are the Book’s account of how he keeps the price up [@yc, 1.29 n. 2].

[17] ✓ Lapis lazuli comes from the Sar-e-Sang deposits. Wood visited the workings in 1838, and the modern geological study describes the mineral deposit [@yc, 1.29 n. 3; @wood1841personal; @wyart1981lapis].

[18] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The F text already gives fast, unshod mountain horses. The special speed down steep slopes belongs to the added detail [@yc, 1.29 n. 4; @eusebi2018devisement, ch. 46 § 13].

[19] ? Ramusio-only. Yule compares other tales of unusual breeds associated with the region. The marked horses and their descent from Bucephalus belong to a legend told about the royal stud [@yc, 1.29 n. 4].

[20] ~ Ramusio-only. Great flocks, streams and high plains appear in the bracketed account. Yule compares the sheep with species known in the mountains [@yc, 1.29 n. 6].

[21] ? Ramusio-only. The recovery after about a year of illness is attributed to Marco in the added account. Yule suggests a site for the ascent, with the identification still open [@yc, 1.29 n. 7].

[22] ~ The cost of cloth and the division between its buyers and wearers of skins belong to the Book. The figures for the cloth in the trousers express the effect of the ample cut [@yc, 1.29 n. 9].

[23] ~ Polo describes fortified settlements and narrow approaches. Yule compares the history of invasions, which qualifies the confidence Polo ascribes to the people who hold those passes [@yc, 1.29 n. 8].

[24] ~ Wheat, huskless barley, and oils pressed from sesame and walnuts belong to the Book’s account of local food. The oils are one of the things the traveller notices in the absence of olives [@yc, 1.29 n. 5].

[25] ? Yule reads Pashai as reported geography and compares the account with stories of Udyāna and with the distribution of the Pashai name. The tale’s magic and its material details cannot establish a single route or population [@yc, 1.30 n. 1].

[26] ~ Keshimur is Kashmir in the Book. The passes and productive valleys fit the broad geography, while political conditions in the period were more complicated than the Book’s account of independence [@yc, 1.31 n. 4].

[27] ~ Kashmir had a significant place in the transmission of Buddhism, especially towards Tibet. The Book goes further in making it the religion’s origin and gives it the travellers’ repertoire of wonders [@yc, 1.31 nn. 2–3].

[28] ~ The ascetics, abbeys and monasteries are the Book’s account of local religion. Yule compares evidence for the region’s religious life at different periods [@yc, 1.31 n. 5].

[29] ~ Zelada and Ramusio. The account of avoiding slaughter and relying on Muslim butchers is shared by the two witnesses. Its explanation belongs to their account of religious practice [@yc, 1.31 n. 6; @moule1938description, ch. 49, p. 140].

[30] ~ Polo gives a merchant’s estimate of demand for coral brought from the west. It is evidence of what a trader might be told about the market, with the extent of firsthand travel in this stretch still open [@yc, 1.31 n. 7].
