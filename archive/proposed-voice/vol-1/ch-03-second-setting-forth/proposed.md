# Chapter I-3
## The Second Setting-Forth: Pope, Oil, and the Boy Marco

> *“…’tis my son and your liegeman.”*
> — Marco Polo, Prologue, ch. 14 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Behind locked doors at Viterbo, the cardinals were still stalling over who would wear the papal tiara. Back on the Venice lagoon, Niccolò and Maffeo Polo had waited two years with the Great Kaan’s golden tablet in their chest, and their patience finally snapped. In 1271 they packed for the East again—only this time, a third shadow fell across the dock beside them. Niccolò was bringing Marco, the boy he had found at home at fifteen, now roughly seventeen and old enough to sit a horse across Asia [1].

First their ship bore them to Acre (Akko), where they sought out Tedaldo Visconti, Archdeacon of Liège—the wise churchman who had urged them to wait for a new Pope two years before. Still no Pontiff sat in Rome, yet Tedaldo was willing to help them discharge at least half of the Great Kaan’s commission. With his leave, the three Polos rode up to Jerusalem to draw oil from the lamp burning at the Holy Sepulchre [2].

Pause for a second over that small glass flask, because it is the most fragile piece of baggage in the entire book. Pilgrims often carried blessed oil home from Jerusalem, where it was worth a journey in its own right. Here, though, the oil had a far stranger destination: Cublay (Kublai) had asked for it, and nobody at the shrine needed to know that the flask was bound for the far side of the world [3].

Back at Acre, the Polos asked Tedaldo for one more favour before they vanished into the interior. They needed parchment with teeth—letters under his seal explaining to Cublay why they had come empty-handed of the hundred Christian scholars he had requested [4].

Think about what a hand-carried letter and a glass flask actually endure on a five-thousand-mile caravan trail. Every time a mule stumbles on a wet slate pass, every time a saddlebag is unstrapped in a muddy caravanserai courtyard, that parchment takes the weather and that flask risks shattering against a cooking pot. Neither item buys a single loaf of bread on the road. Both become priceless only the moment they are laid intact in the hands of the man who asked for them.

They boarded ship and reached Ayas (Yumurtalık) on the Cilician coast—only for a fast galley to come racing after them with astonishing news. On 1 September 1271, the deadlocked cardinals had finally elected a Pope, and they had chosen their very own archdeacon at Acre, who now took the name Gregory X [5]. Worse—or better—the new Pope wanted the Polos back in Acre at once. The ruler of Lesser Hermenia (Cilician Armenia) supplied a royal galley for the turnabout, and the three Venetians found themselves rowing back to the quay they had just left [6].

On that busy stone mole at Ayas, a Venetian traveller was hardly a ghost from another planet; a resident Venetian bailie kept an office right on the docks to mind his countrymen’s cargo and disputes [7]. Yet even that familiar port official must have stared to see Niccolò, Maffeo, and young Marco turn their prows right back toward Syria.

This time, the audience in Acre had the weight of Christendom behind it. Gregory X received the brothers warmly, heard their errand, and drafted his formal reply to the Great Kaan. Instead of the hundred learned doctors Cublay had requested, the Pope appointed two Dominican friars: Niccolò (Nicolas) of Vicenza and William of Tripoli, a scholar who had already written a treatise on Islam and dedicated it to Tedaldo himself [8]. Into their packs went delicate crystal vessels as papal gifts for the emperor, along with extraordinary powers to ordain bishops and grant absolution in the Pope’s stead [9].

All that ecclesiastical pomp lasted barely as far as Ayas. Just as the party stepped ashore, word swept the port that Bundukdar (Baibars), the Mamluk sultan of Egypt, was marching north against Hermenia [10]. The two Dominicans took one look at the panicked harbour, handed their papal credentials and crystal gifts over to the Polos, and hurried straight back aboard ship under the escort of the Master of the Temple [11]. In truth, the great Mamluk sack of Ayas and Sis lay four years in the future, though frontier alarms along the Syrian border were real enough that autumn to send churchmen looking for a safe hull home [12].

Stand on the quay with seventeen-year-old Marco for a moment and watch the friars’ sails shrink toward Cyprus. A churchman turning back can hand his commission cleanly to a superior and sleep in a stone cloister. A merchant going forward has to hire pack-mules from a local broker, bargain in silver or credit for a water-guide, and stop inbound camel-drivers at the city gate to ask whether snow still chokes the mountain passes or raiders are watching the river crossings. In a single afternoon, young Marco had watched an imperial papal embassy shrink back down to three Venetians and their baggage mules.

Between that quay and the Great Kaan’s court stretched three and a half years of snowdrifts, spring torrents, and swollen rivers [13]. The Book deliberately holds the cities and wonders along that track in reserve for the chapters that follow, sweeping the family across Asia in a single breathless stride.

If you were riding beside them, a week of bad weather cost far more than patience. It meant extra barley for the mules, extra dried meat for the riders, and another seven days of interest ticking on your capital before a single bolt of silk could be sold. On a map, a mountain shortcut looks tempting; in the saddle, staying within reach of a walled town with clean wells is worth three days’ detour.

Trace the Book’s later descriptions on parchment and two plausible caravan lines emerge from Ayas: one swings south-east through Savast (Sivas), Mardin, Mausul (Mosul), and Baudas (Baghdad) down to Hormos (Old Hormuz) before turning north through Kerman (Kermān), Badashan (Badakhshan), and Pamier (Pamir Plateau) to the desert; the other strikes east through Tauris (Tabriz). Yet even the tidiest line drawn on a map runs through cities a traveller could just as easily have known from bazaar gossip [14].

Counting from that second departure out of Acre in late 1271, the long march brought the three Polos into northern China around 1274 or May 1275—assuming, of course, that young Marco made the whole journey as the Prologue tells it, a question that will have its full courtroom hearing at the end of Volume III [15].

Forty days before they ever sighted the summer palace, imperial couriers rode out to meet them on the highway, clearing quarters and laying on fresh horses and provisions at every post-house [16]. Niccolò and Maffeo already knew how the Kaan’s post-roads spoiled an ambassador; now they had a twenty-one-year-old Venetian riding at their stirrup to see what imperial hospitality looked like.

At the summer capital of Chandu (Shangdu or Kemenfu), the doors of the painted hall swung open. Cublay sat enthroned amid his barons while the three dusty Venetians dropped to their knees before him. The emperor bade them rise, welcomed them with warm courtesy, and asked how they had fared on the road, to which they answered that finding him safe and in good health made every hardship worth the ride [17].

First they laid the Pope’s letters and sealed credentials before the throne, and then—intact after thousands of miles of mountain passes—the small glass flask of oil from the Holy Sepulchre. Cublay received both with visible delight. Then his gaze shifted to the young stranger kneeling beside the brothers, for whom no letter had spoken. Who was this? Niccolò stepped forward and spoke the line that launched a legend: *“’tis my son and your liegeman.”* Cublay welcomed the youth into his own household [18].

Hold that frozen moment inside the summer hall before the court clerks rustle back to business. In one breath, a Venetian merchant has discharged a nine-year imperial promise and bound his own son into the personal service of the richest sovereign on earth.

After that, the pace shifts from caravan survival to court schooling. Young Marco learned the manners of the Mongol court, picked up its spoken tongues, and—by the Book’s proud boast—mastered four distinct scripts [19]. Which four alphabets he actually read or wrote is never named, and in a chancellery where imperial decrees and stone gates spoke in ’Phags-pa Mongol, Uighur, Arabic, Persian, Chinese, and Tibetan, there is a vast gulf between conversing at table, recognizing an edict’s seal, and keeping a bilingual clerk at your elbow [20].

Soon the emperor tested his new retainer on a six-month ride to a distant province—Carajan (Yunnan), in the wild south-west [21]. Six months out and six months back gave a sharp-eyed twenty-something plenty of time to discover what separates an unforgettable envoy from a dull one.

Picture Cublay sitting through audience after audience as provincial commissioners march in, stare at their boots, recite tax tallies or garrison headcounts, and bow out without a single memorable word about the country they crossed. It bored the Great Kaan half to death. What Cublay craved were the things a traveller notices while waiting for a governor’s seal—what fruits grow in the orchards, how locals bury their dead, what beasts roam the hills, and what tricks merchants play in the bazaar. Marco caught the hint. Wherever he rode on imperial business, he kept one eye on his ledger and the other on the strange customs of the road [22].

When he returned to court, he gave his official report cleanly and in order, then entertained the hall with everything wondrous he had seen and heard. The barons marvelled, the emperor smiled, and the youth earned the dignity of *Messer Marco Polo*. Seventeen years of imperial service followed—along with plenty of envious whispers behind silk sleeves [23]—and even occasional journeys on his own private business with the Kaan’s leave [24].

Yet a golden cage is still a cage. By the time the three Polos had grown rich in jewels and gold, the emperor was ageing, and the Venetians began to dread what would become of them—and their fortune—if their protector died while they were still trapped far from the sea. They begged leave to go home. Cublay flatly refused. Only an imperial wedding embassy bound for Persia would ever pry open the palace gates [25].

Leave the three Venetians standing in that painted summer hall at Chandu, with that small flask of Jerusalem oil resting near the throne. Until now, this Book has followed one family’s diplomatic thriller from Venice to Cathay. Now the curtain drops on the prologue: on the very next page we rewind to the crowded quay at Ayas, trade the family diary for boots on the gravel, and read the continent the way a merchant reads an overland atlas—province by province, pass by pass, and ware by ware.

## Notes

[1] ~ The Prologue's ages and its two-year wait give the conventional departure in 1271 and birth c. 1254 [@yc, Prol. 9–10]. Both dates rest on counting forward from the 1269 return to Venice.

[2] ? The Book calls the archdeacon a legate. Yule and Cordier give conflicting outside accounts of his formal position [@yc, Prol. 9, Prol. 11 n. 1].

[3] ~ Earlier accounts attest the lamps at the Holy Sepulchre and the pilgrim practice of obtaining blessed oil there [@yc, Prol. 10 n. 1]. Cublay's specific request and the flask carried east belong to the Prologue narrative [@yc, Prol. 7].

[4] ~ The request and the letters of excuse are in the Prologue. The text of the letters is unavailable [@yc, Prol. 10].

[5] ✓ Clement IV died on 29 November 1268. The subsequent election was on 1 September 1271 [@yc, Prol. 11 n. 1].

[6] ~ The recall from Ayas and the royal galley lent by the Armenian king belong to the Prologue narrative [@yc, Prol. 11]. Cilician Armenia regularly cooperated with papal legates and Western envoys travelling toward the Mongol courts.

[7] ✓ Yule gives the resident bailie. The Genoese deeds give independent evidence of Italian commerce in the 1270s [@yc, Prol. 8 n. 2; @balletto1989laiazzo; @lin2019cilician].

[8] ~ William of Tripoli and his treatise on Islam dedicated to Tedaldo are independently attested in the material cited by Yule [@yc, Prol. 12 n. 1]. Nicolas of Vicenza and the joint papal commission to the Great Kaan come from the Book's account.

[9] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule suspects exaggeration of the faculties and cites analogous bulls [@yc, Prol. 12 n. 2].

[10] ~ Bundukdar is Baibars, the Mamluk sultan (Yule: reigned 1259–1276). The chronology of the attack as told is disputed [@yc, Prol. 12 n. 3].

[11] ~ The friars' departure with the Master of the Temple is in the Book. Yule gives Thomas Bérard as the master then in office [@yc, Prol. 12 n. 4].

[12] ~ The Mamluk burning of Ayas and Sis in 1275 cannot explain the friars' turnabout in late 1271 [@yc, Prol. 12 n. 3]. Yule instead points to Makrizi's report of serious Mamluk frontier alarms along the Syrian border in the autumn of 1271.

[13] ~ Three and a half years on the outbound road is the Prologue's figure, with bad weather, snow, and swollen rivers given as the explanation [@yc, Prol. 13]. The narrative defers the stops along the route to the descriptive chapters of Book I.

[14] ? Yule constructs the route from the descriptions in Book I. The routes through Tauris and through Mausul and Baudas are reconstructions [@yc, Introduction, § 11; @yc, bk. 1].

[15] ? Yule calculates c. May 1275 at Kaipingfu. Haw's range is 1274 or 1275, inside that window. Wood doubts the second journey [@yc, Prol. 13 n. 1; @haw2006marco, chs. 3 and 5 (pp. 50, 68 in the 2009 paperback); @wood1995did, conclusion].

[16] ~ Sending an escort forty days out to meet the returning party and supply its needs comes from the Prologue [@yc, Prol. 13]. Such advance reception parties matched Mongol diplomatic hospitality along the post roads.

[17] ✓ Yule identifies the Prologue's Kemenfu with Kaipingfu, the summer court later styled Shangdu [@yc, Prol. 13 n. 1]. The formal welcome inside the crowded palace hall follows the Prologue's court scene [@yc, Prol. 14].

[18] ~ The Book presents the papal letters, the credentials, and the Jerusalem oil in this sequence before introducing young Marco as the emperor's *“liegeman”* (French *vostre homme*) [@yc, Prol. 14 n. 2]. That feudal phrasing casts the Venetian youth as a personal retainer of the throne.

[19] ? The Prologue asserts that Marco learned the Tartar customs, languages, and four distinct scripts without naming which alphabets they were [@yc, Prol. 15]. That omission has invited centuries of scholarly conjecture over his actual linguistic command.

[20] ? Pauthier, Yule, and Cordier debate candidate scripts including 'Phags-pa Mongol, Uighur, Arabic or Persian, Tibetan, and Chinese [@yc, Prol. 15 n. 1]. They compare the six-language Yuan inscription at the Juyong Pass and the chancellery practice of issuing orders in several scripts, though none of these comparisons settles which four the Prologue meant.

[21] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The six months are the Prologue's estimate, while the name Carajan appears in Ramusio's version [@yc, Prol. 15 n. 2].

[22] ~ The envoy's education in observing local customs and novelties to satisfy the Kaan's curiosity comes from the Prologue [@yc, Prol. 15]. It supplies the Book's own explanation for why a young commissioner took note of strange manners and cities along his roads.

[23] ~ The return, title, length of service, and court envy are the Book's account, while Pauthier's identification of a court official called *Po-lo* in 1277 with Marco is unproved and refers instead to Bolad [@yc, Prol. 15 n. 1; @yc, Prol. 16].

[24] ~ Ramusio-only. The journeys on private business appear in the bracketed passage [@yc, Prol. 16].

[25] ~ Ramusio-only in part. The requests and refusal are in the Prologue, while the wealth in jewels and gold and the apprehension about the emperor's death appear in Ramusio's bracketed passages [@yc, Prol. 17 n. 1; @yc, Prol. 18].
