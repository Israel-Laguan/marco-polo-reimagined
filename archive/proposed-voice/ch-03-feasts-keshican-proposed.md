# Chapter II-3
## Feasts, Robes, and the Twelve Thousand Keshican

> *“You must know that the Great Kaan, to maintain his state, hath a guard of twelve thousand horsemen, who are styled Keshican, which is as much as to say ‘Knights devoted to their Lord.’”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 2, ch. 12 (Yule–Cordier)

**[V]**

---

We have seen the outer walls, the chess-board avenues, and the midnight curfew bell. Now the doors swing open and the Book walks us straight into the greatest dining hall on earth. Nothing Marco Polo has described so far—not the jewelled markets of Hormos (Hormuz), not the jade-green hill planted with uprooted forests—prepares the reader for what happens inside the palace at Cambaluc (Beijing) when the Great Kaan sits down to eat. Twelve thousand horsemen guard the hall. Five thousand elephants parade his tableware. Gold cisterns the size of wine casks stand between the tables. And if any guest so much as brushes the wooden threshold with his foot, two towering wardens will beat him senseless or strip the clothes from his back. This is court life under Cublay (Kublai) Kaan, and every detail in it is designed to make you feel very, very small.

The guard comes first. Polo calls them Keshican [1]—*“Knights devoted to their Lord”*—and the name is no invention. It is the Mongol *keshikten*, the personal bodyguard created by Chinghis (Chinggis Khan) himself to keep watch over the imperial tent. But Rustichello, writing in his Genoese cell with a mind trained on King Arthur's romances, cannot resist presenting them as a chivalric Round Table magnified beyond all earthly measure. Four captains divide twelve thousand horsemen into companies of three thousand [2]. Each company serves three full days and three nights inside the palace walls, dining from the imperial larders and sleeping under the palace roofs, before being relieved by the next company in an unbroken annual rotation. One body is always awake, and the calendar runs without a hitch. It is not an army camped around a frightened tyrant; it is a pageant on a timetable, the kind of military calendar that runs like clockwork precisely because nobody expects it to fight an invader inside the palace gates. Chinese court records put the actual active watch at around four hundred picked men. The difference between four hundred guards and twelve thousand knights is the difference between an imperial payroll and a romance of chivalry.

Walk through the double gates into the banquet hall and you see political hierarchy laid out as physical architecture *(see Plate 5)*. The Great Kaan sits on an elevated northern dais [3], raised well above the floor and facing south toward the midday sun, exactly as Chinese emperors had done for two thousand years before nomad horsemen ever set foot across the Great Wall. His chief empress sits beside him on the left. The sons, nephews, and imperial princes take their meals at lower tables to his right, but their seats are carefully sunken so that no royal head, no matter how ambitious, can rise above the level of the Sovereign's boots. Below the princes sit the great barons, and below the barons—in a vivid scene preserved in sixteenth-century Italian texts—the ordinary soldiers and court officers sit cross-legged on vast woolen carpets across the stone floor of the hall. Beyond the carved doorways, out in the open courtyards where the winter winds blow, more than forty thousand visitors, foreign ambassadors, and tribute-bearers gather on felt mats. The banquet is not merely dinner; it is an imperial census made edible, a diagram of world dominion enacted three times a day.

At the center of the hall, near the Emperor’s dais, stands an astonishing piece of furniture: a massive square buffet chest of carved and gilded wood [4], three paces on each side, decorated with dragons and beasts. Inside sits a colossal golden cistern holding enough wine or fermented mare's milk to fill a tavern cask, flanked at each corner by four smaller golden vessels the size of firkins. Between every pair of guests along the long tables stands a heavy gold pitcher called a *vernique*—a mysterious word that has teased scholars ever since, likely drawn from the Persian *bārnīya* or the Venetian *vernegal*, meaning a glazed or varnished flagon. Each pitcher holds enough spiced wine to satisfy eight or ten men, flanked by two deep golden drinking cups with handles. Marco, the Venetian merchant's son, watches the servants carry the flagons back and forth, mentally weighing the bullion on display, and confesses that the sheer tonnage of gold and silver beggars his arithmetic. Barons acting as stewards move gracefully among the tables, ensuring that foreign envoys are placed according to rank and that no cup ever runs dry.

At every door stand two colossal wardens armed with heavy staves. If an entering guest so much as clips the wooden threshold with the toe of his shoe, the staves come down [5]: the guards will strip the robes from his back on the spot, or beat him bloody unless he pays an immediate fine. This is not arbitrary cruelty; it is sacred Mongol custom. To nomadic tent-dwellers, the threshold of a dwelling is sacred to the hearth-spirits, and treading upon it brings disaster to the master of the house. Friars Carpini and Rubruck both recorded the taboo when they rode across the steppes, and Odoric found it unchanged a generation after Polo. Ushers stationed outside patiently explain the rule to nervous foreign envoys—though enforcement is politely relaxed on the way out, when courtiers who have drunk too much spiced wine can hardly be expected to mind their footing.

The servers who wait upon the Emperor are themselves high-ranking barons. Before approaching the royal table, they tie cloths of fine silk and beaten gold thread over their mouths and nostrils [6], ensuring that neither human breath nor odor can drift across the imperial food. Whenever the Great Kaan lifts his golden cup to drink, a fanfare of horns and pipes blares through the room. At the very first note, the entire hall—princes, barons, stewards, and the forty thousand guests outside—drops in unison to its knees, prostrating itself while the Sovereign sips. When the cup touches the table, the assembly rises. This miniature earthquake repeats itself at every swallow. Once the feast is cleared, the tables are pushed aside and a troupe of jugglers, acrobats, and court players floods the central hall. Rustichello passes over the food itself—the chivalric writer always preferred pageantry to menus—but he lingers on the explosion of laughter that follows the acrobats' leaps. The Great Kaan looks on, amused by the show, until the final drum beat sounds and the company retires into the night.

---

The calendar of Cambaluc revolves around two immense state gatherings. The first is the Emperor’s birthday feast, which Polo fixes on the twenty-eighth day of the September moon [7]. It is the grandest gathering of the autumn. Cublay appears in a robe of beaten gold leaf that gleams across the hall like sunlight on water. Beside him, twelve thousand barons and knights sit clad in silk-and-gold robes of the exact same color and pattern, each wearing a heavy golden belt presented by the Sovereign's own hand. The entire court forms a living mirror of its ruler, with only the finer cut and precious jewels of the Kaan’s personal robe distinguishing master from servant. Some of these festive suits, Polo swears without blinking, are encrusted with enough pearls and rubies to be worth ten thousand gold bezants apiece.

The birthday is also an empire-wide harvest of gifts. Every subject province sends tribute suited to its wealth, while ambitious provincial clerks flock to the capital bearing rare jewels and furs, hoping a well-timed gift will catch the eye of the court. A council of twelve senior barons sits throughout the day to hear their petitions [8] and award government postings—an administrative civil-service exam disguised as an imperial birthday party. Outside the walls, the empire’s religions pray simultaneously for the Emperor’s long life. Nestorian Christians in their black cowls, Muslim imams in turbans, Buddhist lamas with prayer wheels, and Daoist priests at their incense altars all chant their distinctive liturgies, united under a single royal decree. The Yuan court does not care which god its subjects worship, provided every altar includes an invocation for the throne *(see Appendix C, §2 for Yuan religious policy)*.

Polo links these birthday robes to an astonishing annual cycle: thirteen major festival distributions every year, one for each moon of the calendar, with the Kaan and his twelve thousand guardsmen appearing in thirteen entirely different colors. Thirteen distributions multiplied by twelve thousand men yields a wardrobe of one hundred and fifty-six thousand tailor-made silk suits every single year [9]. The arithmetic is almost certainly an exaggeration born of campfire retellings, though friars Rubruck and Carpini both confirm that nomad khans loved to dress their entire entourages in identical festival colors. The waste is the point: these are not uniforms handed out for military duty; they are royal gifts that wrap the bodies of the warrior nobility in the emperor's own colors. Even the boots match: each man receives a pair of high riding boots of *Camut* or *Borgal*—Russian shagreen horsehide, tanned with bark and embroidered with fine silver thread—locking the imperial retinue into a single aesthetic machine from scalp to spur.

---

The second great festival begins in February: the White Feast, the Mongol New Year [10]. On this morning the entire city sheds its autumn gold and puts on white. Every person who enters the palace gates—from the princes of the blood down to the grooms in the stables—wears plain white silk or sheepskin. To the Chinese, white was the dreaded color of funerals and mourning, but to the Mongols it was the hue of pure milk, the sacred color of beginning and good fortune. Subjects exchange white gifts, embrace in the snow-dusted avenues, and shower the throne with silver ingots, pearls, and white cloths. The crowning tribute is livestock: Polo claims that more than one hundred thousand milk-white horses, without a single dark blemish on their hides, are driven past the palace gates as a New Year offering to ensure that the emperor’s herds never lack abundance.

The tribute of the White Feast is governed by the sacred arithmetic of the steppes: everything must be given in multiples of nine. Where a province can afford it, gifts arrive in sets of nine times nine [11]: eighty-one white stallions, eighty-one bolts of gold brocade, eighty-one chests of silver coin. The mystic reverence for the number nine echoes through nomad law, shamanic rites, and diplomatic gifts, and at the White Feast it sounds in every tribute coffer opened before the throne.

Before the banquet tables are laid, a deafening parade shakes the earth outside the hall: five thousand elephants [12], imported from the jungles of Mien (Burma) and Carajan (Yunnan), march past the Emperor in single file. Each beast is draped in silk blankets patterned with lions and dragons, and each carries upon its back two massive wooden coffers bound in brass, packed with the gold cups, flagons, and ceremonial plate needed to feed forty thousand courtiers. Lines of pack camels follow behind, laden with tents, wine skins, and festive meats. The parade turns the domestic drudgery of catering into high drama: the nation’s pantry walks past the throne on the backs of five thousand armored pachyderms. Five thousand is an impossible stable for northern China, but the spectacle was real enough: Cublay kept captive war elephants as walking symbols of his conquest of the tropical south.

When the parade concludes, the dignitaries take their places inside the great hall: kings, princes, astrologers, physicians, and royal falconers, all arrayed strictly by rank. At a signal from a high prelate, the herald calls out, and the entire gathering prostrates itself four times [13], forehead to floor, worshipping the Kaan as if he were an earthly divinity. The dignitaries then file past a small altar bearing a vermilion lacquer tablet inscribed with the Emperor’s sacred name, each man burning a pinch of fragrant incense in a gold brazier before returning to his station. The gifts are formally unveiled; the tables are laid; the flagons are tipped; and the music begins anew.

One final image remains, and it is Rustichello's strangest. At the height of the feast, an unchained lion is led into the banquet hall and released before the dais [14]. Without cage or rope, the beast pads across the carpet, looks up at the elevated throne, and lies quietly down at the Emperor’s feet like a faithful hound, acknowledging its master without a growl. Medieval travellers routinely used the word "lion" for the striped tigers of Chorcha (Manchuria) and Bangala (Bengal); whatever the cat was, the show was a triumph of imperial animal training. The great predator sleeps upon the floor, enormous, relaxed, and utterly subdued, while the court watches in breathless silence. The court's feasts turn guard duty, rank, tribute, and dress into grand public theatre; the next scene follows the Kaan from the banquet hall into the wilder spectacle of the imperial hunt [15].

## Notes

[1] ✓ The name *Keshican* reflects the historical Mongol institution of the *keshikten* ("the favored ones"), established by Chinggis Khan to guard the imperial yurt and royal family. Persian historian Rashid al-Din calls them the *Kishīkān*, and Friar Odoric recorded them as *Cuthé*. *(See Back Matter II-2, Glossary: "Keshican"; Yule 2.12).*

[2] ~ The four-corps rotation across three-day watches is authentic steppe practice, but 12,000 is an epic chivalric total; Chinese Yuan records list an active daily watch of around four hundred picked men. The guard existed as ceremonial theatre rather than defensive garrison. *(See Back Matter II-1, Trivia: "Robes of one colour"; Palladius in Yule 2.12).*

[3] ✓ The Kaan facing south from an elevated dais while kinsmen sit below his foot-level follows ancient Chinese cosmological court etiquette. The detail that soldiers dined cross-legged on carpets survives primarily in Ramusio’s 1559 text, but matches Friar Rubruck’s eyewitness reports. *(See Map A9 for Dadu palace plan; Rubruck in Yule 2.13).*

[4] ✓ Nomad emperors loved automated drinking furniture. In Karakorum, William of Rubruck saw a colossal silver tree crafted by a captured Parisian goldsmith that pumped wine and fermented mare's milk (*kumis*) into silver basins. Marco’s *vernique* flagons echo the Venetian *vernegal* (a flagon) and Persian *bārnīya* (a glazed jar). *(See Plate 5; Bretschneider in Yule 2.13).*

[5] ✓ In steppe culture, the wooden threshold of a tent was sacred to the domestic hearth-spirits; stepping upon it brought misfortune upon the clan and was punished by beatings or execution. European friars Carpini, Rubruck, and Odoric all noted club-bearing door-wardens (*yundu-chi*), whom Chinese court annals call the "big men" (*dahan*). *(See Carpini, ch. 3; Rubruck, ch. 28; Tao Zongyi, juan 1).*

[6] ✓ Covering the mouth and nostrils with silk-and-gold veils prevented human breath from contaminating imperial dishes—a custom later recorded by European embassies to Ming and Persian courts. Post-dinner acrobats and players (*jongleurs*) were staple entertainment across Eurasian courts. *(See Shah Rukh embassy in Yule 2.13; Chaucer, The Squire's Tale).*

[7] ✓ Cublay’s birthday on the 28th of the September moon was celebrated with distributions of matching robes of beaten gold (*chisun*), a steppe practice binding vassals to their lord. Marco's claim of robes worth 10,000 bezants reflects merchant awe rather than treasury audits. *(See Back Matter II-1, Trivia: "Two capitals, one gravity"; Palladius in Yule 2.14).*

[8] ✓ Yuan state policy required all subject religions—Christians, Muslims, Buddhists, and Daoists—to pray for the Emperor’s health in exchange for tax exemption. The twelve petition-hearing barons functioned as an imperial civil-service admissions board. *(See Appendix C, §2 for Yuan multi-faith edicts; Yule 2.14).*

[9] ? Marco’s claim of thirteen annual robe issues (156,000 suits) exceeds the three seasonal issues documented in Chinese records. The silver-embroidered boots of *Camut* or *Borgal* refer to Russian shagreen horsehide traded through the Volga. *(See Back Matter II-2, Glossary: "Rosia", "Bolghar"; Back Matter II-1, Trivia: "Robes of one colour"; Yule 2.16).*

[10] ✓ To the Mongols, white was the auspicious color of mare's milk and celestial fortune (*Chaghan Sara*); to their Chinese subjects, white was strictly the color of funeral mourning. The sight of the palace swathed in white was simultaneously a triumph of steppe tradition and a cultural affront to Confucian courtiers. *(See Back Matter II-1, Trivia: "Cathay and Manzi are foreigner's halves"; Shah Rukh embassy in Yule 2.13).*

[11] ✓ Steppe diplomacy revered the number nine (the nine heavens, nine-fold tribute). Central Asian sources confirm that royal tribute was systematically presented in sets of nine times nine: eighty-one white stallions, eighty-one bolts of silk, eighty-one cups. *(See Hayton, Intro. §10; Hammer in Yule 2.14).*

[12] ? Five thousand war elephants paraded through Beijing is an impossible stable for northern China, but captive show elephants were brought from tropical campaigns in Mien (Burma) and Carajan (Yunnan) to haul banquet plate. *(See Back Matter II-2, Glossary: "Mien", "Carajan"; Yule 2.15).*

[13] ✓ The four-fold prostration and incense-burning before a vermilion tablet inscribed with the Khan's name was adapted by Cublay in 1263 from Chinese ancestral rites, fusing nomad supremacy with Confucian dynastic worship. *(See Gaubil in Yule 2.15).*

[14] ~ Medieval travellers routinely used the word "lion" for the striped tigers of Chorcha (Manchuria) and Bangala (Bengal). Mongol khans maintained massive menageries of hunting leopards, caracals, and trained tigers taught to prostrate themselves before the throne. *(See Back Matter II-2, Glossary: "Chorcha", "Bangala"; Allsen [-@allsen2006royal]; Yule 2.16).*

[15] ✓ Independent accounts by Rubruck, Carpini, Odoric, and Persian vizier Rashid al-Din corroborate the court customs Marco witnessed, even where numerical totals are romanticized. For an overview of court service claims and veracity debates, see the volume companion. *(See Back Matter II-1, "Trivia and Disputes: Disputed claims"; Reliability Ledger L-18 to L-24).*
