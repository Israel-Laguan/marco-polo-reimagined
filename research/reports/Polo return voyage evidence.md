# Separating the embassy from the Venetians aboard

**Bottom line.** The independent records confirm that the bridal embassy happened. They do not confirm that the Polos sailed with it. Several sentences in the manuscript go further than the evidence. The Chinese notice in the *Yongle dadian* is a 1290 memorial about **rations** for three envoys ordered to Arghun "by way of Ma'bar". It does not record a departure. Yang Zhijiu inferred from it a sailing at the end of 1290 or the start of 1291. Yule, working before the notice was known, reconstructed a sailing in January 1292. Arghun's death on **7 Rabīʿ I 690 AH = 10 March 1291 (Julian)** therefore falls *during* the voyage on Yang's chronology and *before* the sailing on Yule's. III-9 currently asserts Yule's ordering as fact. On Samara, the forts of timber are already in F. The larger camp of about 2,000 men, ditches and five towers is in Z, which Ramusio follows. So the III-3 and III-9 labels "Ramusio-only" and "in every version" are wrong. Joining the Prologue's island to the Samara stay is Yule's editorial linkage, not a statement in any witness. On the route, no Polo witness names **Zayton** or **Hormuz** for this journey. Hormuz first appears as an explicit *conjecture* in Ramusio's own 1559 preface, and Yule then follows it. The Xi'an stele "famous silence" in II-6 describes a scholarly debate the notes could not find. Jacopo d'Acqui's Ayas notice is not "impossible" because of its date: 1296 falls after the return. It becomes impossible only if it is identified with the dated battle of 28 May 1294. All findings below keep the access status recorded in the five notes. Where the notes disagree with each other, this report says so.

Base-text convention: "Yule" means Yule's translation as revised by Cordier (London, 1903), in the repo's Gutenberg copies `book/refs/yule-vol1.txt` and `yule-vol2.txt`. Ramusio means *Navigationi et viaggi* II. Note which printing each citation uses: the q2 note read the **1574 reprint**, while the q4 and q5 notes cite the **1559 preface through Moule–Pelliot's transcription**.

Access labels used throughout (normalised from the notes, never upgraded):

## 2. Detailed findings

### 2.1 A ration memorial of 1290, not a departure record

**What the primary text says.** The notice comes from the *Jingshi dadian* section 站赤 (*Zhanchi*, postal relays), as preserved in *Yongle dadian* juan 19418. On day 17 the officials of the Department of State Affairs relay a memorial from the *pingzhang* Shabuding (沙不丁). It recalls an imperial order of "this year, 3rd month" (今年三月) to send Uladai (兀魯䚟), Abishqa (阿必失呵) and Khoja (火者) "by way of Ma'bar" (取道馬八兒) to Prince Arghun. The party numbered 160. Of these, 90 had already drawn rations, and Shabuding asks that the remaining 70 be refused. The text names no princess and no Polo, and gives no sailing date (q1 lines 16–22). The access is weaker than this summary suggests. The fullest wording comes from a **2025 popular article in The Paper** [inspected, tertiary] ([The Paper 2025](https://m.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_30552365)), and a traditional-character fragment with the juan number comes from an NYU student research site [inspected, tertiary] ([NYU page](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/d2-did-marco-polo-go-to-china/the-return-journey-of-marco-polo)). **The month 八月 (8th) does not appear in the quoted excerpt.** It is reported from the year/month heading of the 站赤 entries by Ma Xiaolin and by Chinese press accounts of Yang (q1 lines 20, 24). The imperial reply has not been seen. Name spellings vary between transmissions and should be treated as variants.

**Conflict between notes.** q1 records that the Wikisource text of juan 19418 "was not found (FINAL-ACCESS-1)" and that an exact-phrase search for "取道馬八兒" returned nothing (q1 line 39). q4 says it read a Wikisource transcription of 永樂大典/卷19418 containing 取道馬八兒 and the 160 persons (q4 lines 20, 56). The two notes cannot both be right about access in the same sense. Until someone re-opens that URL, this report treats the Chinese text as **seen only in secondary quotation and a disputed transcription**. Neither note saw a facsimile.

**Calendar conversion (researcher's computation, unchecked).** Zhiyuan 27 = 1290. The q1 researcher computed new moons by Meeus's algorithm. On that computation, the **3rd month ≈ 11 April–9 May 1290 (Julian)** is the date of the *order*, and **8th month, day 17 ≈ 21 September 1290 (Julian), ±1 day** is the date of the *provisioning memorial* (q1 lines 27–32). The note says explicitly that these conversions **have not been checked** against Cleaves or a concordance such as 《二十史朔闰表》 or the Academia Sinica converter, and that the script lived in a non-durable scratchpad. The month-3 result agrees with Jackson's independently reported "April–May 1290" for the order [earlier pass] ([Jackson PDF](https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/Jackson%20Marco%20Polo.pdf)). One consequence: the apparent "8th month vs April–May" conflict in older project notes disappears, because these are two different events.

**Yang Zhijiu's sailing date and grounds.** Yang found the entry in 1941. Every account reached agrees that he placed the sailing **at the very end of 1290 or the first days of 1291**. Ma's wording reaches us [earlier pass, `ma2025chinese`]. The 2018 Tianjin roundtable, transcribed in The Paper (2019), says Yang dated the departure to 1291, "not 1292" [inspected; the quoted words are the roundtable's, not Yang's] ([The Paper 2019](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_2816319)). The grounds attributed to him are monsoon logic: a party being provisioned in the 8th month would sail with the next north-east monsoon. Yang also argued that the ration context explains the silence about the princess and the Polos [inspected, tertiary, via the NYU page]. **Yang's own 1941 article, his 1944/45 English note and his 1999 book were not inspected.** His reasoning is therefore a report.

**Modern positions.** The positions agree on one point and split on the other. All of them hold that the Chinese and Persian sources corroborate the *embassy* and do not name the Polos. They differ on dates.
- **Jackson (1998)** gives the April–May 1290 order and an arrival of about 1292–93, "clearly before winter 1293–94". He also says the Europeans "are not mentioned in either of these eastern accounts" (p. 98 n. 87) [earlier pass].
- **Cleaves (1976)**: only p. 181 has been seen [earlier pass, JSTOR preview] ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2718743)). His own departure date and his treatment of the Chinese and Persian texts are **unseen**. He did not discover the notice; Yang did [report via Ma].
- **Ma (2025)** calls the notice direct confirmation. That is her interpretation, since the notice does not identify the travellers [earlier pass].
- **Haw (2023)** wrote a dedicated article on the departure. Only the abstract has been read [earlier pass] ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.13173/zdmg.173.1.145)), so **his dates are unknown**.
- **Pelliot** reached 1291 from d'Ohsson and Persian sources [report via the 2018 roundtable]. His *Notes* entry "Cocachin" is in vol. 1, which was not available.
- **de Rachewiltz** estimates arrival in early 1293, and **Vogel** about September–October 1293 [both reported via the NYU page].
- **Yule** gives "the beginning of 1292" from Zayton, then Hormuz in the winter and Ghazan's camp in March 1294 [inspected: `yule-vol1.txt` 6002–04, 6254–84].

No modern author apart from Yule was found arguing for a 1292 departure (q1 line 87). Yule's reconstruction predates the Chinese notice.

**Arghun's death and the ordering.** Rashid al-Din (Thackston p. 575) gives the date as **7 Rabīʿ I 690 AH = 10 March 1291 (Julian)** [earlier pass, OCR only; the Hijri wording on that page is not quoted in project notes]. Yule's Prologue XVIII note gives the same day [inspected, `yule-vol1.txt` 14135–37]. But Yule's own Introduction footnote [21] says "12th March, 1291" (6287), a variant inside Yule. The researcher's tabular Hijri conversion gives Saturday 10 March 1291 Julian, noting that tabular dates can differ by 1–2 days from observed ones (q1 line 106). Gaikhatu's accession is **23 July 1291** (Rashid via Thackston pp. 580–581 [earlier pass, OCR]; Yule [inspected]). The death date is firm. **Its ordering against the sailing is not.**
- On Yang's window, Arghun was alive when the fleet sailed and died about 2–3 months into the voyage.
- On Yule's January 1292, he had been dead about ten months before the sailing.

Yule's "Arghún Khan too had been dead even before they quitted China" (`yule-vol1.txt` 6016–17) is his inference from his own 1292 date. The Book says only "when they got thither they found that Argon was dead" (14013–14) [inspected].

**Do Chinese or Persian records name the Polos or a sea voyage?** No. Rashid (Thackston Part 3 p. 606; Persian in Jahn p. 40) has the khwāja and envoys whom **Arghun** sent to the Qa'an arrive at Abhar with Kökächin, and Ghazan marries her there. The passage gives no date, no route, no ship or sea, and names no Frank or Polo [earlier pass, page scans, English and Persian]. Two readings of this passage are inferences, not text:
- **Boyle (1970, p. 66)** identifies the khwāja with Polo's Coja and suggests that the unnamed companions were the Polos [earlier pass]. That is his inference; Thackston prints *khwāja* as a title.
- **Yule's Hammer quotation** has Ghazan meet "the ambassador whom *he* had sent". That contradicts Rashid, where Arghun sends the envoys (q1 line 157).

Wassaf has no Kököchin passage in the local Elliot–Dowson extract (negative grep only). In the Chinese notice, "by way of Ma'bar" is the only route indicator. It implies a maritime route but describes no voyage and names no port. Placing the provisioning at Quanzhou, because Shabuding was the maritime-trade *pingzhang*, is popular inference (q1 line 36).

**Wording that asserts no ordering** (from q1 lines 139–142, lightly trimmed):
- Body text: "When they came to Persia they found Argon dead. He had died on 10 March 1291, and his brother Kiacatu had taken the throne that July. Whether the fleet had sailed before or after his death depends on the date of the sailing, which is disputed."
- Note: "Arghun died 7 Rabīʿ I 690 / 10 March 1291 (Rashid al-Din, tr. Thackston p. 575; Yule, Prol. XVIII n.). A Chinese notice preserved in the *Yongle dadian* shows that the envoys were ordered west in the 3rd month of 1290 and were being provisioned for the Ma'bar route in the 8th month. Yang Zhijiu inferred a sailing at the end of 1290 or the first days of 1291, which would put Arghun's death during the voyage. Yule's reconstruction (January 1292) would put it before. The Book says only that the travellers found him dead on arrival."

### 2.2 Samara: F already has the timber forts

**What the witnesses say.**

*The Prologue.* F, Ramusio and Yule all give "about three months" to an island called **Java**, then "eighteen months" more on the Sea of India before reaching Argon's land. The readings are:
- F: "najerent bien trois mois … une ysle … qui a non Java … najerent por la mer de Indie bien xviii mois" (Benedetto ch. XIX) [inspected, OCR] ([Benedetto 1928](https://archive.org/download/marcopolomilionebenedetto/marcopolomilionebenedetto_djvu.txt)).
- Ramusio: "circa tre mesi … Iaua … mesi disdotto" (1574 reprint) [inspected, OCR] ([Ramusio II 1574](https://archive.org/download/secundovolumedel00ramu/secundovolumedel00ramu_djvu.txt)).
- Variants: Moule–Pelliot's apparatus records V "nuoue" (apparently nine) and VB "…disenoue" (nineteen, unit illegible) against "eighteen". This is known only through damaged OCR of the apparatus [report] ([Moule–Pelliot I p. 91](https://archive.org/download/descriptionofwor01polo/descriptionofwor01polo_djvu.txt)).
- The "two years" in the witnesses refers to provisions, not voyage length. F's manuscript reads "x anz" there, which the editors emend (q2 lines 33–34, 40–41).

*The Book III Samara chapter.* The five-month stay is **not in the Prologue**. It appears only in this chapter:
- F: "je meisme, Marc Pol, hi demorai por v mois" [inspected, OCR].
- Z: ".v. mensibus" [report, via Benedetto's apparatus].
- Ramusio: "cinque mesi per il tempo contrario" [inspected, 1574 OCR].
- VB reads **six** months [report, Moule–Pelliot apparatus].

**Is identifying the Prologue's island with Samara explicit or reconstructed?** It is **reconstructed**. No witness read links the two. The Prologue names "Java" with no stop or delay, and the Samara chapter mentions no Cocachin, embassy or fleet. Yule makes the link in two places:
- Prologue n. 4: "_I.e._ in this case Sumatra, as will appear hereafter" (`yule-vol1.txt` 14094).
- Intro §21 n. 19: three months to Sumatra, five months detained, eighteen more, "twenty-six months in all" (6254–58) [inspected].

Pelliot's position was not reached (q2 lines 48–61).

**Yule's reckoning, included or additional.** Yule's main reckoning is **additive**: 3 + 5 + 18 = 26 months, sailing January 1292 and reaching Ghazan's camp in March 1294. He then offers an alternative: if the arrival in Persia came earlier, "the eighteen months … must _include_ the five months' stoppage", with Hormuz about November 1293 (6275–82) [inspected]. He does not choose between them, but the inclusive reading is his fallback. Registry row S18-F3 ("the 18 months probably include it") therefore **misreports** him. Read literally, F counts the eighteen months from leaving "that island", which supports the additive sense. The inclusive reading is a harmonisation driven by the arrival date, not by the text (q2 lines 66–75).

**Which Samara details belong to which witness.** The camp is largely **not** Ramusio-only:
- **F** has the landing and the forts: "Nos desendimes des nes et feimes en terre chastiaus de fust et de busches", built for fear of the man-eaters. F also has the tree wine "de blance e de vermoille" and coconuts "mout groses et bonnes" [inspected, Benedetto ch. CLXVIII pp. 172–173].
- **Z**, as quoted in full in Benedetto's apparatus, has: about 2,000 men; great ditches ending on the shore; five *pergama vel trebeschas*; plentiful timber; islanders trading victuals "nam fiebat fiducia"; and the wine curing dropsy, phthisis and spleen. Benedetto marks the passage "riprodotto in R" [report, apparatus].
- **V and L** also carry the dropsy cure, with "R uguale a V" [report].
- **Ramusio-specific, as far as checked:** the coconut "grosse come è il capo dell'huomo" and its liquid "di sapor miglior, et più delicato, che il vino". Even the head-size may not be R-only, because Moule–Pelliot's margin assigns it to other sigla that the OCR does not make legible (q2 lines 83–100).

Yule brackets the whole camp as Ramusio because his base is Pauthier. He did not credit F's forts as the older witness (`yule-vol2.txt` 15995–99). The "toddy from the gomuti palm" in III-3 is Yule's modern identification (his note 3), not the text.

**Named vs inferred ports.** No Prologue witness read names a departure port. **Zayton is Yule's inference** ("They appear to have sailed from the port of Zayton", 5998–6000). The Prologue's only named stop is "Java". Ferlec, Basman, Samara, Dagroian, Lambri and Fansur are named as kingdoms in the Java the Less description, not as an itinerary (q2 lines 104–114).

**Conflict between notes.** q4 describes the Samara stockades as a "bracketed R addition" (q4 line 90), following Yule. q2 shows the forts themselves are in F, and that only the enlarged camp is Z and Ramusio. q4 acknowledges the gap (line 99). q2's reading, based on the text of the edition, should govern.

**Formulations for III-3 and III-9** are in §4, rows III-3-a and III-9-c.

### 2.3 The Xi'an stele: no "famous silence" was found

**Stele history.** The **erection in 781** is documented by the inscription itself. The Chinese text gives Jianzhong year 2, which Gaubil reckoned as 4 February 781. The Syriac gives 1092 of the Greeks (Seleucid era) = 781 [inspected, Yule vol. 2 Bk II ch. XLI n. 2 fn. 4, `yule-vol2.txt` 2737–70; page range ~27–29 inferred from cross-references]. Nothing documents the stone's whereabouts between 781 and the 1620s.

*Burial* is inferred from two things: every discovery account says it was dug out of the ground, and it was found in good condition. Saeki (1916, pp. 17, 21–22) concludes it "had been buried, no doubt, for a long, long time" [inspected] ([Saeki 1916](https://archive.org/details/nestorianmove00saekuoft)). The date of burial rests on conjecture:
- Yule credits **Pauthier** (1857/58) with the supposition that it "may have been buried in 845", the year of Wuzong's Huichang edict (`yule-vol2.txt` 2823–30) [inspected; Pauthier not inspected, so whether he was first is unverified].
- Saeki's claim that Christians buried it to protect it is asserted without evidence (pp. 89–90).
- A 1990 *JRAS Hong Kong Branch* article reportedly allows "as early as 845, possibly as late as the 980s" [snippet; page returned 403; author and title unverified].

**Discovery date: 1625 vs c. 1623–25.** 1625 is not certain. The witnesses are these, all [inspected via Saeki's translation or report]:
- **Li Zhizao's colophon**, dated 12 June 1625 (later report), says the stone was dug out "recently".
- **Trigault**, at second hand: "This year, 1625". He saw the stone in October 1625.
- **Emmanuel Diaz** (1644, later report) dates the discovery to Tianqi year 3 = 1623.
- **Moule** concludes "Early in the year 1625, perhaps about the beginning of March".
- **Saeki** says the date "so far no one has ascertained … exactly". He brackets it between 1620, from Aleni's ignorance of the stone, and March 1625.

Modern authorities split:
- GEDSH: "1623 or 1625" [snippet] ([GEDSH](https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/Xian)).
- Lieu (2025): 1623 [inspected] ([Lieu 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/97ED83314CFBD8DFFA438F3CE7DDE415/core-reader)).
- Wikipedia: "between 1621 and 1625", citing Ma Tianji 2025 [snippet] ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi%27an_Stele)).

The place is also disputed between Xi'an's western suburb, Zhouzhi and a point between them. The notes do not state the calendar behind the 1620s Western dates. They are given here as reported. Yule's "accidentally found in 1625 by some workmen … in the Chang-ngan suburb" (`yule-vol2.txt` 2702–06) is the 1903 consensus, not a settled fact.

**History of the argument.** The notes found **no scholar who uses the stele's absence from Polo as evidence for or against his presence in China.** Yule makes no inference from it; he treats the controversies as being about authenticity (2793–95). The nearest arguments run the other way:
- **Rockhill**, via Cordier, notes that Markos and Rabban Sauma (c. 1276) also never mention Chang'an. He uses this to question Xi'an as a Nestorian see, not to judge Polo (2776–87) [inspected].
- **The 1990 JRASHKB article** reportedly concludes that the tablet "had been long underground" when Polo arrived [snippet].

The Western reception of the stele concerned whether it was a Jesuit forgery, not Polo [snippet/metadata, Lieu; Keevak review]. The project's own audit had already removed the stele from III-12 as a false "miss" (`book/plans/critique/vol-3.md` 94–96; facts.md F-32).

**Independent evidence for Christians at Xi'an c. 1275–90.** The notes found it **weak or absent**:
- Polo's Kenjanfu chapter twice calls the people Idolaters (`yule-vol2.txt` 2347, 2362) [inspected; only Yule's composite was checked, not F, Z or R].
- Odoric's Kansan mentions no Christians [inspected, `odoric-yule-cordier-1913.txt` 13930–80].
- Markos and Sauma pass Chang'an over.
- English and Chinese searches found no Yuan source placing *yelikewen* at Fengyuan/Xi'an [negative search].

Yuan Christianity is well attested elsewhere: Tangut, Tenduc, Zhenjiang, Dadu and Quanzhou. Prince Ananda of Anxi is associated with Islam, not Christianity [snippet]. The 1289 Chongfu si was recalled from general knowledge and is **unverified**.

**Recommendation: omit** the aside at Kenjanfu. **Second choice: reception history only.** Do not frame it as an open question, because the notes found no scholarly debate to describe. The wording is in §4, row II-6.

### 2.4 The route: five named stages, many editorial dots

The ledger is in §3.3. In summary, the Prologue return passage of every witness checked names "Java" after about three months, the land of Argon (no city), Trebizond, Constantinople, Negropont, and Venice in 1295 (q4 lines 10, 50) [inspected, Moule–Pelliot I pp. 90–93 and `yule-vol1.txt` 13976–14058].

**Zayton is not explicit.** Ramusio's own preface says nothing can be said about the port "perche non lo nominano" ("because they do not name it") [inspected, via Moule–Pelliot's reprint p. 582–583, OCR]. Yule writes "seems" and "appear to have sailed" (`yule-vol2.txt` 13262–64; `yule-vol1.txt` 6002–04). Moule–Pelliot I p. 27 n. states it flatly, as editorial comment. Bk III ch. V's "on leaving the port of Zayton you sail west-south-west" is a sailing direction, not a dated departure (`yule-vol2.txt` 14510).

**Hormuz is not explicit in any travel text.** Ramusio's 1559 preface conjectures it: "non si può se non per conietture pensare", by sea "all'isola di Ormus", then Carmania, Persia, Trebizond, Constantinople and Negroponte (Moule–Pelliot I p. 583) [inspected via reprint; 1559 folio unverified]. Yule follows with "probably did on landing at Hormuz" (`yule-vol1.txt` 14121–22).

**Conflict between notes.** q2 attributes Hormuz as landfall to Yule. q4 traces it earlier, to Ramusio's preface. The two are compatible, but the manuscript's "Ramusio names no port" is only half right: true of Ramusio's travel text, false of his preface.

**Tabriz is not named on the return.** Yule writes "turned back to Kiacatu (*probably* at Tabriz)" [inspected].

**Abhar** comes from Rashid only, through Hammer into Yule's note.

**Ma'bar** is the strongest Indian point, and it has two separate kinds of support:
- A Z/R passage (Moule–Pelliot I p. 389, ch. 174; bracketed in Yule) has Marco there "on his way home" [inspected, OCR]. F lacks it.
- The Chinese notice gives Ma'bar as the *embassy's ordered route* (subject to the access conflict in §2.1).

**Ceylon, Kayal and Tana** are Yule's "perhaps" stops (6262–64). **Quilon and Cambay** are claimed by no one. **Trebizond** is explicit in the Book. Maffeo Polo's will of 6 February 1309 *more veneto* (= 1310 modern; Venetian year beginning 1 March) records a loss "by the lord Comnenus of Trebizond" [inspected via Moule–Pelliot p. 28 translation and appendix Latin; original and Orlandini 1926 not seen]. The will gives no date, no mechanism and no "robbery". Tying the loss to 1295 is Moule–Pelliot's inference. Registry S18-F8 adds a further point from an earlier pass: Yule reports a related act under 1308 Venetian style, and that discrepancy remains open.

**Embassy evidence vs. evidence that the Polos accompanied it.** The independent sources establish:
- the envoys' names (Chinese notice, Rashid's khwāja);
- the party size of 160 (Chinese notice);
- the planned Ma'bar route (Chinese notice);
- delivery to Ghazan at Abhar (Rashid).

Neither independent source names the Polos. The Polos' presence rests on the Book alone. The Book's match with the envoy names and with Ghazan as husband is consistent with insider knowledge, but it corroborates no route point except, loosely, Ma'bar (q4 lines 58–60; Jackson pp. 98–99 [earlier pass]).

### 2.5 Jacopo d'Acqui, Ayas and Curzola

**Verified Latin excerpt.** This comes from the Turin redaction, checked on the page image:

> "Inter quos ducitur quidam venetus qui diu fuit cum Tartaris et dicebatur filius Milionis."

Source: Iacobus ab Aquis, *Chronicon Imaginis Mundi*, ed. Avogadro, *Historiae Patriae Monumenta*, Scriptores III (Turin, 1848), col. 1582 [inspected, page image] ([HPM III, n990](https://archive.org/details/HistoriaePatriaeMonumenta5/page/n990/mode/1up)). The Turin notice is **undated**. It places the defeat "ad locum ubi dicitur la Glaza in littore maris", and "la Glaza" is Jacopo's own name for Ayas elsewhere in the same text (c. col. 1607–08) [inspected, OCR].

The **Milan redaction** survives in Ambrosiana D 526 inf. fol. 77v and Trivulziana 704. Under "MCCLXXXXVI" (1296, the chronicle's year; occasion: battle and capture) it reads "in mari de Arminia aput dicitur Layaç": 15 Genoese merchant galleys against 25 Venetian. "dominus Marchus venetus … dictus est Millonus" is taken to prison in Genoa [inspected via Bolognari's diplomatic edition, pp. 36–37; manuscript images not seen] ([Bolognari 2024](https://edizionicafoscari.it/media/pdf/article/transcript/2024/1/art-10.30687-TranScript-2785-5708-2024-01-002_iW61McJ.pdf)). Neither redaction names Curzola or a command.

The Polo chapters are found only in the Milan manuscripts, and their authenticity has been doubted. Moule says "it is not certain that they formed part of the original work"; Bolognari argues they are Jacopo's own.

**Conflict between notes.** q5 saw col. 1582 on the image. It also reports Bolognari citing Avogadro "coll. 1557–1559" for the Turin text. The two locators may refer to different sections, but the discrepancy is unresolved. Moule also pairs the shelfmark G II 34 with the date 1428 inconsistently (q5 line 32).

**The 1294 battle.** Ayas was fought on **28 May 1294 (Julian; battle)**, the feast of St Germanus [inspected, Yule Intro §32 pp. 41–43]. The sources:
- **Iacopo da Varazze**, writing in 1297, gives "MCCLXXXXIIII": 18 Genoese merchant galleys plus 2 *ligna* "in portu Layacii" took 25 Venetian galleys [inspected via Bolognari's quotation, p. 41 n. 45; Monleone edition reported].
- **Hayton** puts the battle under 1293 [inspected, via Yule].
- **Moule's** figures of 22 Genoese and 28 Venetian do not match Varazze. He may be counting the *ligna* (18 + 2 = 20), or there may be a typo.

**Is 1296 itself impossible?** No. Marco was in Venice by 1295 (Prologue), so a 1296 capture at sea is not chronologically excluded. The conflict arises only if Jacopo's battle is **identified** with the dated 1294 engagement: same place, the same 25 Venetian galleys, merchant galleys on both sides. No 1296 Genoese–Venetian engagement at Ayas is attested. The recorded 1296 actions were Venetian offensives at Pera, Phocaea and Caffa (Yule §32 p. 43 [inspected]; [Wikipedia, War of Curzola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Curzola) [inspected, tertiary]). "Tempore Bonifacii" is a dating anomaly for 1294, because **Boniface VIII was elected only on 24 December 1294**.

**Readings.** Each of the following is an editor's interpretation:
- **Yule (1903, Intro §37 pp. 53–55)** [inspected]: the Ayas statement "cannot be true", and "LXXXXVI is as certainly a mistake for LXXXXIV". He still takes Jacopo as evidence of capture "in some sea-fight" and as corroboration of Ramusio. He concedes that "no contemporary authority" names Curzola.
- **Benedetto, followed by Moule (Moule–Pelliot I pp. 33–35)** [Moule inspected; Benedetto p. cxciv not seen]: Marco was taken "at some obscure and otherwise unrecorded engagement of armed merchantmen in 1296", and Ramusio mistakenly made it Curzola. Moule argues that Ramusio's own "Non molti mesi" suits 1296 better than late 1298.
- **Bolognari (2024, pp. 41–42, c. 64)** [inspected]: 1296 is a scribal slip for 1294, parallel to "VI" for Boniface VIII. Jacopo conflated the 1294 battle with Marco's captivity, which makes his account unreliable on how Marco was captured. Bolognari does not affirm Curzola.

**What Ramusio says.** The 1559 preface, fol. 6v–7r, is quoted here from Moule–Pelliot's transcription, document d.95, pp. 585–586 [inspected via transcription; no scan of 1559 seen]. "Non molti mesi" after the return, Lampa Doria came with seventy galleys "fino all'isola di Curzola". Marco was made "sopracomito d'una" under Andrea Dandolo and fought "il giorno di nostra Donna di Settembre", the feast of 8 September. He was wounded, captured, put in irons and sent to Genoa. Ramusio gives **no year**; 1298 comes from the known date of the battle. Moule's apparatus records the variants "governatore" for "sopracomito" and "90. galee" for "molte galee". Ramusio's "non molti anni" for the length of the imprisonment conflicts with the documented release in 1299. The date of the Curzola battle varies by source: **7 September 1298** (Yule) or **8 September 1298** (Moule; Ramusio's feast), both Julian. The peace was signed on **25 May 1299** and ratified by Venice on **1 July 1299**.

**Wording that keeps the layers apart** is in §4, rows I-1-a to I-1-d.

## Conclusion

The five questions share one editorial pattern. In each case, a reconstruction by a named editor has hardened into narrative fact in the manuscript:
- Yule's 1292 sailing has become "dead before sailing".
- Yule's brackets have become "Ramusio-only".
- Ramusio's conjectural Hormuz and Yule's "probably" Tabriz have become a solid route line.
- A passing curiosity about the stele has become a "famous silence".
- The editors' identification of Jacopo's battle has become a notice "dated impossibly".

The fix is mostly attribution, not new research: say whose inference each link is. The evidence that most needs new access is the Chinese notice itself. Its month heading is reported, the two notes disagree about whether a Wikisource text exists, and every Julian conversion is the researcher's own and unchecked. Until a facsimile and a concordance are consulted, the manuscript should print Chinese months, not Julian days. It should also present the sailing as a span from the end of 1290 to early 1292, not as a point.
