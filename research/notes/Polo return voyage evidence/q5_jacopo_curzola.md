# Jacopo d'Acqui's notice of Marco Polo's capture versus the later Curzola tradition

Access labels used below:
- **SEEN-IMAGE**: I inspected the printed page image directly.
- **SEEN-TEXT**: I read the full text of the passage (OCR or publisher PDF), but not a page image.
- **REPORTED**: I rely on another scholar's report and did not see the source.

All dates are Julian, as in the sources. Each date is tagged as a battle, capture, imprisonment, or later report.

---

## 1. What Jacopo d'Acqui's *Imago mundi* actually says (texts, manuscripts, variants)

### Takeaway
Jacopo's chronicle survives in two redactions that tell the story differently.

- **Longer Milan redaction.** This is the only one with the Polo chapters: Ambrosiana D 526 inf. (14th c.), fol. 77v (cols. c–d), and Trivulziana 704 (15th c.). Under the year **"MCCLXXXXVI"** (1296) it reports a sea battle "in mari de Arminia" at a place called **Layaç**: 15 Genoese merchant galleys against 25 Venetian. "Dominus Marchus venetus", called *Milonus*, was captured there and taken to prison in Genoa, where he stayed "per tempora multa".
- **Turin redaction.** This is the one printed by Avogadro in 1848. It has a short, undated notice. The Venetians come "ad locum ubi dicitur la Glaza in littore maris", are defeated, and many are taken to prison in Genoa, among them "quidam venetus qui diu fuit cum Tartaris et dicebatur filius Milionis". He made a book there. Jacopo uses "la Glaza" elsewhere in the same chronicle for the port where the Polo brothers landed on their way to Acre, so it means Ayas/Laiazzo.

Neither redaction names Curzola. Neither says that Marco commanded a galley.

### Cited Findings

**Turin redaction (SEEN-IMAGE).** *Historiae Patriae Monumenta*, Scriptorum tomus III (Turin, 1848), "Chronicon Imaginis Mundi fr. Iacobi ab Aquis", **col. 1582, sections b–c**, under the heading "De magna discordia que oritur inter Ianuenses et Venetos". I verified it against the page image (archive.org leaf n990). The verbatim excerpt:

> "Veniunt Veneti contra Ianuam ad locum ubi dicitur la Glaza in littore maris et ibi forte committunt bellum in mari. Et debellantur Veneti a Ianuensibus et multi ducuntur Ianuam ad carcerem. Inter quos ducitur quidam venetus qui diu fuit cum Tartaris et dicebatur filius Milionis. Et ibi in carcere fecit librum pulcherrimum de mirabilibus mundi."

— [HPM Scriptores III, page image n990](https://archive.org/details/HistoriaePatriaeMonumenta5/page/n990/mode/1up); [OCR text](https://archive.org/download/HistoriaePatriaeMonumenta5/Historiae_patriae_monumenta_5_djvu.txt)

- **The notice has no date, and its placement is anachronistic.** It sits after notices on Frederick II besieging Faenza and on a legate besieging Ferrara (1240s). It comes before "Veneti contra Ianuenses" (the Acre war of St Sabas, 1256–58) and the Pisan–Genoese battle with Pisan prisoners held "fere xxx" years (Meloria, 1284). The chronicle's own sequence therefore gives no reliable date for the Glaza notice. — same page, SEEN-IMAGE
- **"la Glaza" is Jacopo's own name for Ayas.** Later in the same Turin text, the Tartar envoys travel "usque ad civitatem christianorum que dicitur la Glaza. et de la Giaza veniunt ad civitatem Achon vel Acris". This matches Marco's book, where the Polos land at Layas before going to Acre. — HPM Scriptores III, c. col. 1607–08 (SEEN-TEXT, OCR only; column number taken from the nearest running head "1608")
- **The Turin witness behind Avogadro's edition is uncertain.** The HPM contents list describes it as "Ex Codice Cartaceo Saeculi XV in Regia Bibliotheca Athenaei Taurinensis" (SEEN-TEXT, OCR). Bolognari 2024, p. 28 n. 14, says Avogadro, coll. 1557–1559, "si basa sulla lezione del codice trecentesco Torino, BNU, G.II.34". Moule (1938, I, p. 32 n. 1) calls a Turin manuscript G II 34 "dated 1428", while his Appendix I note gives 1428 as the date of Trivulziano 704. Moule is therefore internally inconsistent, and the shelfmark-to-date pairing is unresolved. — [Bolognari 2024 PDF](https://edizionicafoscari.it/media/pdf/article/transcript/2024/1/art-10.30687-TranScript-2785-5708-2024-01-002_iW61McJ.pdf); [Moule–Pelliot 1938 vol. 1 text](https://archive.org/download/descriptionofwor01polo/descriptionofwor01polo_djvu.txt)

**Milan redaction (SEEN-TEXT, Bolognari's diplomatic edition).** Bolognari, "La *Chronica* di Iacopo d'Acqui e il *Devisement dou Monde* di Marco Polo: una prima ricognizione", *TranScript* 3 (2024), 21–68, at **pp. 36–37**, edited from Milano, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, D 526 inf., c. 77v. A short excerpt:

> "Anno Yhesu Christi .MCCLXXXXVI., tempore Bonifacii pape .VI. … factum est prelium in mari de Arminia aput dicitur Layaç de .XV. galeis mercatorum Ianuensium, .XXV. Venatorum … Inter quos capitur dominus Marchus venetus, qui erat cum mercathoribus illis, qui dictus est Millonus … ducuntur in carcerem Ianue et ibi sunt per tempora multa."

— [Bolognari 2024, PDF](https://edizionicafoscari.it/media/pdf/article/transcript/2024/1/art-10.30687-TranScript-2785-5708-2024-01-002_iW61McJ.pdf); DOI [10.30687/TranScript/2785-5708/2024/01/002](http://doi.org/10.30687/TranScript/2785-5708/2024/01/002)

- **Bolognari's apparatus notes three scribal slips:** "Pro. VIII" for the pope's numeral VI, "Pro Venetorum" for "Venatorum", and "Pro medietatem" for "mediantem". The text continues: Marco wrote the book in the Genoa prison, "Liber Milionis de mirabilibus mundi". On his deathbed he replied "Non scripsi medietatem de hiis que vidi". — Bolognari 2024, pp. 36–37 nn. 29–33 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Moule's earlier translation of the same passage** reads: "In the year of Jesus Christ 1296, in the time of Pope Boniface VI, a battle took place in the sea of Armenia at the place called Layas between 15 ships of Genoese merchants and 25 of Venetians". Moule says it was "Quoted by B[enedetto], p. cxciv, from the Ambrosian MS. D.526, fol. 77c, d" and notes "Twice in seven lines the Pope is called Boniface VI instead of VIII." — Moule & Pelliot, *The Description of the World* (London, 1938), I, **pp. 31–32 and n. 1** (SEEN-TEXT, OCR); [archive.org item](https://archive.org/details/descriptionofwor01polo)
- **Yule's English rendering** says it follows an Ambrosian manuscript as extracted by Baldelli Boni. It reads "a battle was fought in Arminia, at the place called Layaz, between xv. galleys of Genoese merchants and xxv. of Venetian merchants". — Yule–Cordier, *Book of Ser Marco Polo* (1903), I, Introduction § 37, p. 54 (SEEN-TEXT: local `book/refs/yule-vol1.txt`, c. lines 7411–7443)
- **Yule on the Turin text.** It "differs widely in wording, is much shorter, and contains no date", and places the capture at "Là Glazà", which he identifies with Ayas ("sometimes called Giàzza"). — Yule–Cordier I, p. 55 n. 30 (SEEN-TEXT, local)
- **Authenticity of the Polo chapters.** These chapters survive only in the two Milan manuscripts and "sono stati sospettati di essere spuri". Bolognari argues they are Jacopo's own (pp. 25–26). Moule (Appendix I, p. 491 n.) says they are absent from two Turin manuscripts and "it is not certain that they formed part of the original work". Chiesa 2004 (quoted by Bolognari, p. 25 n. 9) is likewise cautious. — SEEN-TEXT
- **Jacopo's dates.** He wrote before 1334 (Bolognari, abstract). Yule, I, p. 54 n. 29, traces him alive in 1289, 1320 and 1334, citing Avogadro's introduction. — SEEN-TEXT

### Inferences
- Jacopo, the only near-contemporary narrative of the capture, ties it to Ayas in both redactions: explicitly as "Layaç" in Milan and as "la Glaza" in Turin.
- The year 1296 appears only in the Milan redaction, as the heading of a separately prefaced Polo section ("accessus"). Bolognari (p. 41 n. 44) notes that the heading formula marks a return to the main line of the chronicle. So "1296" may be Jacopo's chronological slot for the section, not a battle date taken from a source.
- **Translation uncertainties:**
  - "in mari de Arminia" means the sea off Cilician (Lesser) Armenia, not Greater Armenia.
  - "galeis mercatorum" means merchant galleys. Moule's "ships" is looser.
  - "dicitur Millonus … quod est idem quod divicie mille milia librarum" glosses the nickname as wealth. It is not Ramusio's later "talked in millions" explanation.
  - In the Turin redaction "filius Milionis" ("son of Milion") makes the nickname a patronymic.

### Gaps
- I did not see a page image of Ambrosiana D 526 inf. or Trivulziana 704.
- I did not see Benedetto 1928, p. cxciv, or Baldelli Boni's extract directly. The Milan Latin above is quoted from Bolognari's edition, not checked against the manuscript.
- I did not check the four other witnesses: Parma ASPr b. 39, Pavia, Turin I.II.22, and Turin misc. 126.I.

## 2. The 1294 Ayas (Laiazzo) battle and the 1296 question

### Takeaway
The Ayas battle securely dates to **28 May 1294** (battle). Iacopo da Varazze's Genoese chronicle, written in 1297, gives the year 1294 and puts the Genoese in "portu Layacii". If Jacopo's notice describes that battle, Marco cannot have been there, because the Polos came home via Trebizond and Constantinople and reached Venice in 1295.

The year 1296 is not impossible in itself. The problem only arises if the notice is identified with the 1294 battle. I found no independently attested 1296 Genoese–Venetian engagement at Ayas.

The 1296 actions that are recorded were Venetian offensives: Pera fired, Phocaea's alum works wasted, and Caffa taken, plus Genoese reprisals at Constantinople. In those actions Venetians were the attackers, not merchant galleys defeated off Cilicia.

### Cited Findings
- **Iacopo da Varazze, *Cronaca di Genova*.** He begins "Anno quidem Domini .MCCLXXXXIIII.": the Venetian fleet of ".XXVIII." galleys and ".IIII. ligna" against Genoese merchant galleys numbering ".XVIII." plus two ligna, under admiral Nicolino Spinola. The Genoese took refuge "in portu Layacii", then fought and took ".XXV." Venetian galleys loaded with goods. — quoted in Bolognari 2024, p. 41 n. 45, citing Monleone (ed.), *Iacopo da Varagine e la sua Cronaca di Genova* (1941), pp. 97–99 (SEEN-TEXT of Bolognari's quotation; Monleone REPORTED)
- **Yule on the battle.** Spring 1294, "off Ayas in the Gulf of Scanderoon". The Genoese were "inferior in strength by one-third" and captured all but three Venetian galleys. The Venetian commander was Marco Basilio (Basegio). Genoa decreed an annual pall for St Germanus, "on whose feast (28th May) it had been won". A Genoese ballad dates it "mille duxenti … novanta e quatro", while Hayton puts it under 1293. — Yule–Cordier I, Intro. § 32, pp. 41–43, and p. 55 n. 31 (SEEN-TEXT, local)
- **Moule's figures** are "twenty-two Genoese and twenty-eight Venetian warships in 1294". — Moule–Pelliot I, p. 33 (SEEN-TEXT, OCR). This is inconsistent with Varazze's 18 + 2 ligna. The 22 may count the ligna (18 + 2 = 20), or it may be a typo.
- **The 25 Venetian galleys match.** Bolognari notes that Varazze's 25 captured Venetian galleys and the "merchant" quality of the ships match Jacopo's figure of 25. He reads "1296" as a scribal error for MCCLXXXXIV, supported by the parallel slip "VI" for "VIII". He adds that the episode heads the section opened by Boniface VIII's election in 1294. — Bolognari 2024, p. 41 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Bolognari on the return route.** A capture in 1294 is "in patente contrasto" both with the Polos' return route (Black Sea, Trebizond, Constantinople; F ch. 18) and with the documented loss the Polos suffered at Trebizond (Matteo's 1310 will). — Bolognari 2024, p. 42 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Pope Boniface.** Jacopo says "tempore Bonifacii", but Boniface VIII was elected only on 24 Dec 1294. Moule uses this point, together with the different ship counts, to argue that Jacopo's battle is distinct from the 1294 battle. — Moule–Pelliot I, p. 33 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Events of 1296.** In Yule's words: "Further outrages on both sides occurred in 1296. The Genoese residences at Pera were fired, their great alum works on the coast of Anatolia were devastated, and Caffa was stormed and sacked", and Venetians at Constantinople were massacred and bailo Marco Bembo killed. — Yule–Cordier I, Intro. § 32, p. 43 (SEEN-TEXT, local). Wikipedia's "War of Curzola" (citing Madden, *Venice: A New History*, 2012, p. 181) says Ruggero Morosini took 40 galleys to Constantinople and Giovanni Soranzo took Caffa. The article does not mention any Cilician engagement. — [Wikipedia, War of Curzola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Curzola) (SEEN-TEXT; tertiary source)
- **Ca' Polo galley levy, July 1294.** A Council of Thirty decree of July 1294 required the Ca' Polo to equip a galley. Yule notes this was "before the return of the travellers". — Yule–Cordier I, p. 52 n. 16, citing Romanin II, 332 (SEEN-TEXT of Yule; Romanin REPORTED)

### Inferences
- **The year 1296 alone does not create the conflict.** Marco was back in Venice by 1295, so he could have sailed east again in 1296. The conflict comes from equating Jacopo's Layaç battle with the dated 1294 battle. That equation rests on the same place, the same 25 Venetian galleys, and "merchant" galleys on both sides.
- **The 15 Genoese galleys do not match** Varazze's 18 (+2 ligna). Moule takes this as a distinguishing detail. Bolognari treats Jacopo as conflating.
- **Status of the 1296 date.** A separate merchant-galley fight at Ayas in 1296 is not excluded. However, no source I found attests one. Moule calls it "some obscure and otherwise unrecorded engagement". That is a hypothesis, not evidence.

### Gaps
- I did not inspect the *Annales Ianuenses* (Jacopo Doria's continuation, MGH SS 18) for 1294–1296. I believe the continuation stops c. 1293–94, but I did not verify this.
- I did not inspect any Venetian chronicles (Dandolo, Navagero) for 1294–1296, or the Genoese ballad text beyond Yule's quotations.
- I did not inspect Madden 2012 or the primary sources for Morosini's 1296 expedition.

## 3. Editors and interpreters: whose interpretation is whose

### Takeaway
There are three distinct scholarly readings:

| Scholar | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Yule** (1871/1903) | Jacopo's Ayas statement "cannot be true". 1296 is a slip for 1294. Jacopo still corroborates that Marco was taken "in some sea-fight". Ramusio's Curzola then remains "beyond reasonable doubt", although Yule concedes "no contemporary authority" names Curzola. |
| **Benedetto (1928) and Moule (1938)** | Jacopo has been under-weighted. Marco was taken in an obscure 1296 merchant-galley engagement. Ramusio wrongly identified it with Curzola. |
| **Bolognari (2024)** | 1296 is a copying error for 1294. Jacopo conflated the 1294 Laiazzo battle with Marco's captivity, so the account is unreliable on how Marco was captured. Bolognari does not affirm Curzola. |

The scholar the brief calls "Bolognari" is **Marcello Bolognari** (Ca' Foscari / Ministero della cultura). The relevant work is the 2024 *TranScript* article. His 2021 *Studi Medievali* article on Agnese is unrelated to this question.

### Cited Findings
- **Yule's case for Curzola:**
  - He admits "certainly no contemporary authority has yet been found for the capture of our Traveller at Curzola. Still I think that the fact is beyond reasonable doubt". He quotes Paulin Paris: Marco was imprisoned "from motives equally unknown".
  - He sets aside the Ayas date because "The date of the Battle of Ayas is not more doubtful than that of the Battle of the Nile", and the "LXXXXVI is as certainly a mistake for LXXXXIV".
  - He then accepts Jacopo "as evidence from a contemporary source that he was taken prisoner in some sea-fight with the Genoese, and thus admit it in corroboration of the Ramusian Tradition".
  - He concedes that if 1296 were accepted, the imprisonment would fit Ramusio's chronology "better … than the capture of Curzola".
  - — Yule–Cordier I, Intro. § 37, pp. 53–55 (SEEN-TEXT, local `book/refs/yule-vol1.txt` c. lines 7368–7471)
- **Moule (author of the introduction in Moule–Pelliot vol. 1):**
  - "there is small doubt that Benedetto is right in thinking that too little weight has been given to this story".
  - Ramusio's "Non molti mesi" "will suit a battle in 1296 much better than one late in 1298". It also leaves time to write the book, which is hard to do if Marco only reached Genoa about 16 October 1298 and the prisoners were released in summer 1299.
  - Conclusion: "Marco was taken prisoner at some obscure and otherwise unrecorded engagement of armed merchantmen in 1296, and that Ramusio was mistaken in identifying this battle with the famous battle of Curzola in 1298."
  - — Moule–Pelliot I, pp. 33–35 (SEEN-TEXT, OCR). This is Moule's argument. I found no separate statement by Pelliot here.
- **Moule on Bratianu.** Moule notes that G. I. Bratianu, *Recherches sur le commerce génois* (1929), pp. 262–274, accepts Curzola "without discussion". — Moule–Pelliot I, p. 34 n. (SEEN-TEXT; Bratianu REPORTED)
- **Bolognari's position.** "una confusione tra scontri marittimi e un sospetto errore paleografico ne compromettono l'attendibilità". In his conclusion, "la battaglia del 1294 di Laiazzo … diventa pretesto per parlare di Marco Polo e della sua cattura". — Bolognari 2024, pp. 41 and c. 64 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Rustichello's prologue.** Marco, "demorant en le charthre de Jene", had everything set down by Rustichello "au tens qu'il avoit 1298 anz que Jezu eut vesqui" (imprisonment and writing, 1298). It names neither the battle nor the place. — Yule–Cordier I, p. 53 (SEEN-TEXT, local)
- **Ramusio's version (R).** Moule says R adds that Marco was in prison "because of the war". — Moule–Pelliot I, p. 33 (SEEN-TEXT)

### Inferences
- **Yule's "corroboration" is his own inference.** Jacopo's notice supports a sea-fight capture, but in both redactions it names Ayas, not Curzola.
- **Moule's 1296 engagement is also an inference.** It is built on Jacopo's date and has no independent record.
- **Bolognari's reading removes the 1296 alternative,** but it also makes Jacopo unusable as evidence for how Marco was captured.

### Gaps
- I did not see Benedetto 1928, pp. cxciii–cxcviii, directly.
- I did not see Larner, *Marco Polo and the Discovery of the World* (1999). Prior repo research reports that Larner treats the place of capture as uncertain (`book/plans/research/results/CHR-people-2-raw.md`, item 6b; REPORTED).

## 4. What Ramusio (1559) says about Curzola, and whether anything earlier places Marco there

### Takeaway
Ramusio's preface to *Navigationi et viaggi* II (Venice, 1559), fol. 6v–7r, is the earliest source I found that puts Marco at Curzola. It says:
- "not many months" after the Polos returned, news came that "Lampa Doria" had reached the island of Curzola with seventy galleys;
- Marco was made *sopracomito* of one of the Venetian galleys under Andrea Dandolo;
- the battle was fought on "the September feast of Our Lady" (8 September);
- Marco, pressing forward in the first line, was wounded, captured, put in irons and sent to Genoa.

The passage does **not** state the year 1298. That year comes from the known date of Curzola (battle, 7 or 8 Sept 1298). Yule and Moule both found no earlier evidence placing Marco at Curzola.

### Cited Findings
- **The 1559 Italian, verbatim (as transcribed in Moule–Pelliot):**

  > "Non molti mesi dapoi che furono giunti a Venetia, sendo venuta nuoua, come Lampa Doria Capitano dell'armata de Genouesi era venuto con settanta galee fino all'isola di Curzola … fu fatto per il suo valore sopracomito d'una messer Marco Polo; … essendo il Capitano generale messer Andrea Dandolo … combattendo il giorno di nostra Donna di Settembre … fu preso … rimase ferito & prigione … posto in ferri, fu mandato a Genoua."

  — Moule–Pelliot I, Appendix document **d.95**, pp. 585–586, transcribing *Navigationi et viaggi*, vol. 2 (Venice, 1559), Preface fol. 6v–7r (SEEN-TEXT, OCR; Moule gives "Pref. fol. 6vo" at I, p. 34 n.)
- **Apparatus variants.** Moule's apparatus for d.95 records "furono armate 90. galee" against the base text's "molte galee", and "gouernatore d'una" against "sopracomito d'una". These suggest a variant witness, possibly a later printing. I did not identify the source of the variants. Yule's translation ("the arming of 90 galleys") follows the 90 reading. — Moule–Pelliot I, p. 585 nn.; Yule–Cordier I, Intro. § 6, p. 6 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Release.** Ramusio says Marco was freed "non molti anni dapoi", which Moule reads as at least four years. That contradicts the documented 1299 release of the Curzola prisoners. — Moule–Pelliot I, p. 34 n. (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Ramusio's errors.** Yule acknowledges that Ramusio's biographical notes "contain many errors of detail", including the long interval he puts between the return and Curzola. Yule thinks the Curzola command was "derived from a genuine tradition, if not from documents". — Yule–Cordier I, p. 53 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **Curzola battle date.** Yule: the fleets sighted each other on Saturday 6 Sept and fought on Sunday 7 Sept 1298. Moule says 8 Sept 1298. Ramusio's "giorno di nostra Donna di Settembre" is the Nativity of the Virgin, 8 Sept. Prisoners reached Genoa on 16 Oct (Yule) or 6 Oct (Bratianu's reading of "seze di"). The peace was signed on 25 May 1299, the Venetian ratification was dated 1 July 1299, and release followed in summer 1299. — Yule–Cordier I, §§ 34–36 (SEEN-TEXT, local); Moule–Pelliot I, pp. 33–34 (SEEN-TEXT)
- **No earlier source for Curzola.** Moule says Ramusio's and Barbaro's accounts "do not seem to be supported by any early authority" (Moule–Pelliot I, p. 33). Yule says "no contemporary authority" (p. 53). — SEEN-TEXT
- **Bolognari on Ramusio's nickname story.** He suggests Ramusio's "Millioni" explanation may come from the Ghisi codex, probably a Z-type copy. This concerns the nickname, not Curzola. — Bolognari 2024, p. 40 n. 42 (SEEN-TEXT)

### Inferences
- **Curzola is a sixteenth-century identification,** made more than 250 years after the battle.
- **The *sopracomito* detail exists only in Ramusio,** and one variant reads "governatore" instead.
- **Ramusio's own "not many months" sits awkwardly with a battle in late 1298.** This is the internal tension Moule exploits.

### Gaps
- I did not inspect a scan of the 1559 printing itself (archive.org, Gallica or the Ca' Foscari Simion–Burgio edition). The text above relies on Moule's transcription.
- I did not find Barbaro's genealogy (Vienna MS 6156, fol. 358, cited by Moule) or any Venetian document naming Marco in the 1298 fleet.

## Narrowest safe wording for the retelling

> Marco's own book says only that he was in prison at Genoa in 1298, when Rustichello wrote down his account. How he got there is not recorded in any contemporary document. The Dominican chronicler Jacopo d'Acqui, writing before 1334, says Marco was captured by the Genoese in a sea-fight off Ayas (Laiazzo) in Lesser Armenia. One manuscript dates this to 1296, which may be a copyist's slip for the well-known Genoese victory there in May 1294, before Marco had come home. Another version gives no date at all. The familiar story that he commanded a galley at Curzola in September 1298 first appears in Giovanni Battista Ramusio's preface of 1559. Yule accepted it; Moule and Benedetto preferred an otherwise unrecorded clash in 1296; Bolognari (2024) regards Jacopo's account as a confusion with the 1294 battle. The place and occasion of his capture remain uncertain.

**Shortest fallback:** "Captured in a sea-fight with the Genoese (traditionally at Curzola, 1298; Jacopo d'Acqui says off Ayas), he was in a Genoese prison by 1298."

**Short Latin excerpt suitable for an epigraph (SEEN-IMAGE):** "Inter quos ducitur quidam venetus qui diu fuit cum Tartaris et dicebatur filius Milionis." Cite as: Iacobus ab Aquis, *Chronicon Imaginis Mundi*, ed. Avogadro, HPM Scriptores III (Turin, 1848), col. 1582.
