## Which projects cite specific excavated or dated objects precisely enough that the illustrator can go and look at the original?

### Takeaway
Most of the usable, well-dated evidence comes from excavated and conserved finds, not from reconstructions. The strongest tie to the 1274/1281 Mongol invasions is the Takashima (Kōzaki) underwater site, with its helmet, leather armour scraps and ship timbers. For Mongol and Yuan dress, the strongest evidence is the excavated robes from Mingshui (Inner Mongolia), together with the Met/Cleveland "When Silk Was Gold" catalogue and the Abegg-Stiftung volume. For Mediterranean ships, the evidence is documentary: Pryor's work on Louis IX's 1246–1269 ship contracts. Only one project that sails a replica has a peer-reviewed object source, the Jewel of Muscat (the Belitung wreck), and that source is roughly 450 years too early.

### Cited Findings

**Mongol armour and kit**
- **Takashima Kōzaki underwater site (Matsuura, Nagasaki), the wrecks of the 1281 invasion fleet.** In 2003 KOSUWA (Kyushu Okinawa Society for Underwater Archaeology), led by Kenzo Hayashida, reported finding a Mongol iron helmet upright on the seabed and "scraps of red leather armor" (found in a citation): [Archaeology 56:1 (2003)](https://archive.archaeology.org/0301/etc/kamikaze.html).
  - The project lead you named, "Ikeda Kenzo", appears to merge two people. The 2011 discovery of an intact hull is credited to a University of the Ryukyus team led by Ikeda Yoshifumi (found in a citation; one later source places him at Kokugakuin University instead). For Hayashida, see the Archaeology article above.
  - Japanese government-funded (KAKEN) final research reports exist for project nos. 18102004 (FY2006–2010) and 23222002 (found in a citation): [KAKEN 18102004 report](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/report/KAKENHI-PROJECT-18102004/18102004seika); [KAKEN 23222002 results PDF](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/file/KAKENHI-PROJECT-23222002/23222002seika.pdf).
  - One exhibit listing has an "Iron helmet, Kamakura period" dated to about 720 years ago (found in a citation).
  - Dating basis: historical, from the typhoon that destroyed the fleet in 1281 (from memory).
  - Holder: Matsuura City board of education, through the Takashima history and folk museum (from memory; the search tool itself suggested this museum). I found no inventory numbers.
  - **I found no published reconstruction of the Takashima helmet or armour.**
  - Ship-timber study by Randall Sasaki, *The Origins of the Lost Fleet of the Mongol Empire* (found in a citation: [archive.org PDF](https://archive.org/download/Book_1137/The%20Origins%20of%20the%20Lost%20Fleet%20of%20the%20Mongol%20Empire.pdf)). That this was a Texas A&M MA thesis (2008) and later a book from Texas A&M University Press (2015, NEW) is from memory.
- **M. V. Gorelik (1946–2015)**: Russian armour scholar, described as the founder of the Russian school of historical weapons reconstruction, who made reconstructions for institutions including the Tower of London (found in a citation: [Kulikovo Field museum page](https://en.kulpole.ru/discover/history)).
  - His plates rest on specific finds. Examples: two lamellar plates from Samarkand (13th–14th c.), and a burial near Yablonovsky that he read as a Circassian warrior of the Golden Horde (found in a citation: [Gorelik PDF](https://aheku.net/f1jok/files/4473/Gorelik.pdf)).
  - He argued that many helmets were made in the Ulus of Jochi (the Golden Horde) and reached Europe in the second half of the 13th century. He also discussed a 14th-century falchion from a Golden Horde burial near Kairka in the Sivash area (found in a citation: [swordmaster.org summary](https://swordmaster.org/summary.html)).
  - Later criticism (NEW):
    - Kubik (2020) argues that the Samarkand plates reflect a local tradition from before the Mongols (found in a citation: [Kubik, Historia i Świat 2020](https://czasopisma.uph.edu.pl/historiaswiat/article/download/1768/3116/6721); the page redirected to a login, so I did not read it).
    - Negin (2025) disputes Gorelik's reconstruction of a helmet crest and plume (found in a citation: [Negin 2025](https://nav.jvolsu.com/index.php/en/component/attachments/download/630)).
  - The same museum page notes that no complete original military equipment of the 14th-century Rus' or Horde survives. Reconstructions therefore combine fragments with images (found in a citation).
- **Mongolian museums (archery).** I opened a 2012 photo gallery of Ulaanbaatar and Kharkhorin museum cases. It is a hobbyist site, "Frauenadler", run by "Kazimira", and is a blog-grade source.
  - The Military History Museum in Ulaanbaatar shows a **13th-century bow labelled as a copy**, with no find-site given. Commenters note that it is strung backwards.
  - The National Museum of Mongolian History shows 13th–14th c. arrowheads from Karakorum, **Inv. No. U.63-1-7**. Per the caption they were excavated by a Soviet-Mongolian team in 1949.
  - Iron arrowheads from Ikh Khet sum, Dornogovi aimag, are labelled "Mongolian Empire Period", found 1972.
  - Source: [frauenadler.com gallery](https://www.frauenadler.com/galleries/mongolian-arms-and-armor/archery-artifacts-in-mongolian-museums/) (**opened and confirmed**; it only reports what the museum captions say).
- **The 13th-century Mongol bow find.** A surviving 13th-century Mongol bow is said to come from "Cagaan Chad". The only source for this was a Wikipedia mirror, so it is unverified. I did not open atarn.org (Stephen Selby's Asian Traditional Archery Research Network; that it is his site is from memory), so its bow article is **not opened**.
  - The Buural Uul burials (excavated 1980–84, dated 11th–13th c.) are published in [Munkhtulga 2015, NUM journal](https://journal.num.edu.mn/MJAAE/article/download/1566/1465/4766) (found in a citation; I did not confirm that a bow was among the finds).
  - A 2025 Russian article, "Composite Bows of the High Middle Ages from Rock Burials in Mongolia" (Universum Humanitarium, NEW), may hold the dated bow evidence. I saw only its metadata: [NSU record](https://pure.nsu.ru/portal/en/publications/-------(1b173320-8e73-4c0e-9fb8-73b562d755d4).html).

**Mongol and Yuan dress**
- **Mingshui robe.** A yellow silk robe embroidered with gold thread, from the Onggut tomb site at Dasujixiang, Mingshui, Darhan Muminggan United Banner, Inner Mongolia, excavated in 1978. It is dated "Mongolian, 13th–14th century". The images are credited to the Inner Mongolia Museum, Hohhot. No accession number is given. A footnote cites Kessler, *Empires Beyond the Great Wall*, for a possible Uighur manufacture. Source: [UW Silk Road Seattle, fig. 104](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/exhibit/mongols/fig_104.html) (**opened and confirmed**).
  - A similar robe was found at Yanhu in 1970 (found in a citation).
  - I found **no source tying Zhao Feng or the China National Silk Museum to a reconstruction of the Mingshui robe**. Zhao Feng leads the *Comprehensive Collection of Chinese Silks* publishing project, and the museum's re-creations covered other objects (found in a citation: [China Daily 2026](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202602/07/WS6986eea1a310d6866eb380b8.html)). The museum's restorations covered Ming tomb textiles from Tongxiang and Yingpan mummy garments, not Mingshui ([chinaservicesinfo 2024](https://www.chinaservicesinfo.com/s/202403/29/WS66065d8e498ed2d7b7eaaba4/zhejiang-exhibition-presents-textile-artifact-restoration-achievements.html), found in a citation).
- **Watt & Wardwell, *When Silk Was Gold* (1997).** A catalogue of dated, numbered objects, among them Mongol-period robes and cloths of gold, published by the Met with the Cleveland Museum of Art. A Christie's lot note cites a Mongol robe in it as no. 41, p. 152. Source: [Met publications](https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/when-silk-was-gold-central-asian-and-chinese-textiles) (found in a citation; this edition is free to read on the Met site).
- **Abegg-Stiftung, Riggisberg.** Von Fircks & Schorta (eds.), *Oriental Silks in Medieval Europe*, Riggisberger Berichte 21 (2016, NEW), from a 2011 colloquium at the Abegg-Stiftung. It includes Caroline Vogt, "Mongol splendour: a cloth-of-gold garment in the Abegg-Stiftung collection". A review calls it a sequel to Wardwell's work on "Tartar cloths". Source: [Abstracta Iranica review](https://journals.openedition.org/abstractairanica/43114) (found in a citation).
  - The garment's Abegg inventory number was not found.
  - Abegg also published *Dragons of Silk, Flowers of Gold* (2007), but it covers the Liao dynasty and is too early ([Zenon record](https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/000741493)).

**Western dress, c. 1270–1300**
- **Las Huelgas, Burgos (Patrimonio Nacional, Museo de Telas Medievales).** Royal burials, among them the Infante Fernando de la Cerda (d. 1275), whose garments survive. Gómez-Moreno published them as *El Panteón Real de las Huelgas de Burgos* (1946). All of this is **from memory and was not verified this session.** The date is a perfect match for the period, but the dress is Castilian, not Venetian.
- For Italy I found no reconstruction project with object-level sourcing. Dated frescoes remain the best evidence (gap).

**Mediterranean ships**
- **Pryor, crusader transport ships.** Published in *Mariner's Mirror* 70 (1984), Part I pp. 171–219, Part II pp. 275–92, Part III pp. 363–86, with a follow-up, "The naval architecture of crusader transport ships and horse transports revisited", *Mariner's Mirror* 76 (1990). Found in a citation via [weaponsandwarfare.com](https://weaponsandwarfare.com/?p=3116), a low-grade aggregator, so verify these page ranges.
  - The evidence base is documentary, not archaeological. Specifications for two-masted ships survive from Genoa and Venice for 1246–1269, from Louis IX's contracts. The earliest specifications come from a 1246 contract with Genoa for 12 *taride* carrying 20 horses each (found in a citation).
  - **Pryor's reconstructions are drawings from contracts, not from hulls.** They are excellent for dimensions, but carry no physical object.
- **Boccalama, Venetian lagoon.** A galley and a *rascona* were exposed by CVN/NAUSICAA from June to October 2001 and then resubmerged. The galley was reused as foundation formwork for a monastery and probably sunk in the early 1300s. Medas and D'Agostino published preliminary reports in 2003. Source: [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/secrets-from-venices-lago/) (found in a citation).
  - **I found no radiocarbon or dendrochronology date.** The dating is from historical context.
  - The hull is reburied in situ, so there is no museum object to visit.
- **Contarina I and II (Po delta, found 1898).** That they are dated c. 1300 is from memory, unverified. The excavation records and photos survive, but the hulls are largely lost (from memory).

**Indian Ocean and China Sea ships**
- **Jewel of Muscat (2008–2010), a sewn-plank ship.** Built at Qantab, Oman, starting October 2008 and funded by Oman's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, based on the **Belitung wreck (early 9th c.)**, excavated 1998–99.
  - Only about 20% of the hull was exposed well enough to study. Mike Flecker produced a computerised design, and the result resembles a large Omani *battil*.
  - It reached Singapore on 3 July 2010 (that date comes from a Wikipedia-derived source).
  - Sources (found in a citation): [Aramco World 2012](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/201203/sailing.through.time.jewel.of.muscat.htm); the peer-reviewed paper Vosmer, Belfioretti, Staples & Ghidoni 2011, PSAS 41: 411–425 ([Archaeopress OJS](https://www.archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/PSAS/article/view/1705)); Vosmer, "The Jewel of Muscat: reconstructing a ninth-century sewn-plank boat", in *Shipwrecked: Tang Treasures and Monsoon Winds* (Smithsonian Arthur M. Sackler Gallery, 2010), pp. 121–35 ([Smithsonian PDF](https://asia.si.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/shipwrecked-08-vosmer.pdf)).
  - Fetch status: the Archaeopress page timed out and the Smithsonian PDF returned 403, so **neither was opened**.
  - The Belitung cargo is held by the Asian Civilisations Museum, Singapore, as the Tang Shipwreck Collection (from memory).
- **INSV Kaundinya (Indian Navy, inducted May 2025 at Karwar; NEW).** A stitched ship whose planks are sewn with coir rope and sealed with resin, built by Kerala artisans.
  - It is based on a **5th-century Ajanta Caves painting**. Sanjeev Sanyal says the team made a line drawing of the painting and then compared it with local boatbuilding traditions and with the Jewel of Muscat. One report adds that "ancient seals" were used to work out its proportions.
  - It sailed from Porbandar to Muscat in about 18 days, approaching Oman in early January 2026.
  - All of this is found in a citation, from news coverage only: [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2628486/); [The Week, 21 May 2025](https://www.theweek.in/wire-updates/national/2025/05/21/del64-navy-ld-stitched-ship.html).
- **Samudra Raksa (2003; Borobudur Ship Expedition).** Built from the 8th–9th c. Borobudur ship reliefs with help from Nick Burningham. It sailed from August 2003 to February 2004 to the Seychelles, Madagascar, South Africa and Ghana, and is now in the Samudra Raksa Museum at Borobudur. Found in a citation; the sources were Wikipedia and tourism pages only ([injourney](https://injourneydestination.id/en/experience/Ocean-Raksa-Ship-Museum/)). That the expedition was led by Philip Beale is from memory.
- **Spirit of Majapahit (2016, NEW).** Also based on the Borobudur reliefs. Historians object that the Majapahit ship type was the *jong*, not an outrigger vessel of the relief type (found in a citation; Wikipedia-grade source only).
- **Quanzhou ship (from memory).** A Song-dynasty hull, sunk c. 1270s and excavated 1974. It is displayed in the Quanzhou Maritime Museum's hall at the Kaiyuan Temple and dated by coins and cargo. This is the best physical evidence for a Chinese seagoing ship in Polo's period.
  - I found **no replica that rests on it**. The *Princess Taiping* replica (2009, Taiwan) was, I believe, a Ming-style type, unverified.
- **Shinan wreck (from memory).** Sunk c. 1323, dated by a cargo tag of 1323. It is held by the National Research Institute of Maritime Cultural Heritage, Mokpo, Korea. A generation late, but close.
- **Phanom Surin (Thailand; from memory).** A sewn ship of the 8th–9th c. with a Pahlavi inscription, published by John Guy (JRAS, 2017, NEW; unverified). Too early.
- **Lucy Blue / Tom Vosmer, Oman sewn boats (from memory).** Ethnographic and experimental work on sewn-boat traditions that survive in Oman and India. It is useful for technique, but carries no object dated to 1250–1300.

**Conservation and excavation projects**
- **DAI Karakorum excavation (Mongolian-German, from 2000; from memory).** Ernst Pohl and others. The exhibition catalogue *Dschingis Khan und seine Erben* (Bonn/München 2005) catalogues excavated objects with their find contexts. Not verified this session.
- **Golden Horde textile conservation (Kazan/Tatarstan).** Not found in this session (gap).

### Inferences
- **Highest trust (A or B) for the illustrator:**
  - Takashima finds: dated by the event, but published mostly in Japanese, with no inventory numbers found.
  - Mingshui robe: excavated 1978, held by the Inner Mongolia Museum, no accession number.
  - *When Silk Was Gold* catalogue numbers: object-level, free online.
  - Abegg's "Mongol splendour" garment.
  - Karakorum arrowheads, U.63-1-7.
  - Pryor's contracts: documentary but dated precisely (1246–1269).
- **The Takashima site is the single best-dated (1281) assemblage of Mongol equipment.** No reconstruction exists, so the illustrator has to work from photographs of the conserved finds and from the KAKEN reports.
- **Gorelik's plates are the most-used source for Mongol armour imagery but should be grade B/C.** They cite real finds, yet in places mix 14th-century Golden Horde, Samarkand and Yuan material. Recent critics (Kubik 2020, Negin 2025) dispute specific attributions and reconstructions.
- For Venice and Genoa there is **no physical reconstruction of a dress outfit or a nave, c. 1270–1300, that rests on dated objects**. The illustrator should combine Pryor's contract dimensions, the Boccalama galley, dated frescoes and the Las Huelgas garments (with the Castilian caveat).

- The identity and source of the "Cagaan Chad" 13th-century bow, and the content of the atarn.org article, are unverified.

## Which projects are known to rest on later or doubtful evidence (flag)?

### Takeaway
Every replica ship that has actually sailed in the Indian Ocean or Indonesia rests on evidence centuries before 1290: Belitung (9th c.), Borobudur (8th–9th c.) and Ajanta (5th c.). The Majapahit replica (1290s–1400s) also uses the wrong ship type. Use them for sewn-plank technique only, not as 1290s hull forms.

### Cited Findings
- **INSV Kaundinya: grade C.** Too early and iconographic only. A line drawing of a 5th-century Ajanta painting was extrapolated with the help of later traditions and the Jewel of Muscat. No excavated hull lies behind it ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2628486/), found in a citation).
- **Jewel of Muscat: grade A for its own period, C for 1290.** It is a peer-reviewed reconstruction, but of an early-9th-century wreck of which only about 20% was well exposed. The final form was shaped by the modern Omani *battil* ([Aramco World](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/201203/sailing.through.time.jewel.of.muscat.htm), found in a citation).
- **Samudra Raksa: grade C.** Based on 8th–9th c. reliefs, not on a hull.
- **Spirit of Majapahit: grade D.** It applies the Borobudur relief type to the Majapahit era, and historians say the Majapahit ship type was the *jong* (found in a citation; Wikipedia-grade source only).
- **Gorelik's Samarkand lamellar plates: grade B/C, contested.** Kubik (2020) argues they are a local Central Asian tradition from before the Mongols, not a Mongol type (found in a citation).
- **Gorelik's helmet crest reconstruction: grade B/C, contested** by Negin (2025) (found in a citation).
- **Tana and Azov armour: grade C.** Gorelik and Fomichev's "Mongol" attribution of some 14th-century Azov/Tana plate is uncertain, because Western European parallels date to the 1360s ([oocities Kaganate page](https://www.oocities.org/Kaganate/tana.html), found in a citation; low-grade site).
- **13th-century bow in the Military History Museum, Ulaanbaatar: grade D.** It is a copy with no provenance on the label, and it is reportedly strung backwards ([frauenadler](https://www.frauenadler.com/galleries/mongolian-arms-and-armor/archery-artifacts-in-mongolian-museums/), **opened and confirmed**).
- **"Hanfu" community reconstructions of Yuan dress: grade D** by your brief. I did not research them.
- **Northern cog replicas:** wrong region for Venice and Genoa (your note; not researched).

### Inferences
- **Correct date but the wrong region:** Takashima ships (Chinese and Korean hulls), the Quanzhou ship and Las Huelgas.
- **Correct region but the wrong date:** the Belitung, Borobudur and Ajanta ships, and the Shinan wreck (1323).

### Gaps
- I found no scholarly critique of Samudra Raksa or of INSV Kaundinya. The only commentary found was news and Wikipedia-grade.
- I did not research any Mongol ger-on-wagon reconstruction. Evidence would come from Rubruck's text and Ilkhanid paintings; no project was found.
