# Pass-one Polo claims hold up, with real gaps

Across the five pass-one topic clusters — human customs, horses and livestock, crocodilians and hunting animals, Kerman/Cobinan metallurgy, and other minerals and money — the research consistently finds that **Marco Polo's own narrative claims can be quoted and located precisely**, that **Yule's 1903 footnotes supply real but dated (mostly 19th-century) corroboration or correction**, and that **independent modern scholarship closing the remaining gaps is largely missing or only found at the abstract/metadata level**. A critical access caveat governs every finding below: this research pass worked from the **Project Gutenberg plain-text/HTML transcriptions** of Yule's translation (eBook #10636, Vol. I; eBook #12410, Vol. II) and a BookRags re-transcription of the same Gutenberg text, not from page-imaged scans of the physical 1903 (Yule, 3rd ed.) or 1920 (Cordier revision) print volumes — so every page number cited is a Gutenberg/BookRags locator, not a verified 1903/1920 print-page number, and the 1903-vs-1920 textual boundary could be distinguished only where the text itself carried an explicit "H.Y." or "H.C." signature. Within that constraint, the clearest wins are the **Caindu salt-currency passage**, where the base Z/F text and Ramusio's additions are cleanly separable ([Gutenberg #12410](https://gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)), and the **coal, asbestos, and Mutfili-diamond-legend** passages, where Yule's own apparatus already does the debunking or place-identification work. The weakest links are the handful of claims this project most wanted resolved — the Zardandan modern ethnic identification, the Chahak/Kerman ondanique steel connection, the Weisgerber 2004 turquoise citation, the 1856/1885 shark-charmer dates, and 13th-century Yunnan crocodilian zoogeography — none of which could be closed with a directly inspected modern source, and all of which should be reported as open rather than resolved.

## Human customs: Polo's claims are quoted cleanly, but the modern ethnic label is unconfirmed

The Zardandan material sits in a single chapter, **Yule-Cordier Book II, Ch. L, "Concerning the Province of Zardandan"** ([Gutenberg #12410](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12410/pg12410-images.html)), and Polo's own text was directly quoted for all three customs the brief asked about: gold tooth-cases worn by the men only ("every man covers his teeth with a sort of golden case... The men do this, but not the women"), the couvade (the husband "keeps his bed... with the child by his side" for forty days while the wife "resumes household work"), and arm-and-leg skin-pricking with black pigment using five bound needles, described as "a sign of gentility." Each of these is squarely **Polo's narrative claim** (verdict: reported), separable from Yule's footnote apparatus, which runs in numbered, separately headed notes. Yule's Note 2 is where the comparative ethnography lives: it derives "Zardandan" from Persian *Zăr-dandán* ("Gold-Teeth") via Rashiduddin, explicitly flags that **Ramusio's Italian text says both sexes wore the gold cases — directly contradicting the "men only" statement of the base translation** (a genuine manuscript/print-tradition divergence, not an error introduced by this research), and cites the 19th-century observer Hannay linking the Gold-Teeth people to the **Kakhyen (Kachin) and Singpho (Jingpo)** peoples of the Yunnan–Upper Burma borderland. That 19th-century identification is a real precedent for, but not the same thing as, a modern scholarly consensus: no post-2013 peer-reviewed ethnohistory resolving "Zardandan = Dehong Dai vs. Jingpo vs. another Burmese group" was located despite a targeted search, so the modern identification must be reported as **unconfirmed**, resting only on Yule's own century-old footnote. No material inspected anywhere in the chapter or its notes connects Zardandan tattooing to the Philippines/Luzon — this is a confirmed absence, not a disputed point, and the Caugigu chapter (Bk II, Ch. LVI) that might bear on a separate tattooing question was never retrieved in full, leaving that sub-question **not found**.

The pearl-fishery material sits in **Book III, Ch. XVI, "Concerning the Great Province of Maabar"** ([Gutenberg #12410](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)). Polo's own text names the charmers "Abraiaman" and states merchants pay them "one twentieth part of all that they take" to protect divers from the fish, with the charm said to last only a day. Yule's Note 2 — drawing on **Tennent's** 19th-century account of the British-period Aripo fishery in Ceylon — adds a structurally different institution: a hereditary charming family, a government-salaried chief operator, a daily in-kind payment of ten oysters per boat, Tamil **Kaḍal-Kaṭṭi** ("sea-binder") and Hindustani **Hai-bandha** ("shark-binder") terminology, and an anecdote that the office was once held by a Roman Catholic Christian without apparently disturbing belief in the charm. That is a real, independently documented 19th-century parallel institution in the same general region and functional role, but it is **not the same remuneration scheme** as Polo's merchant-paid one-twentieth, and no source inspected — not Yule's footnote, not a 1997 Sunday Times (Sri Lanka) piece, not a 2023 Groundviews article, not a 1908 excerpt from Kunz and Stevenson's *The Book of the Pearl* — contains either a "continued until 1885" or a "banned in 1856" date for the practice. That specific date discrepancy, which the originating brief evidently wanted resolved, remains **unresolved**: targeted searches for both dates returned nothing, and the likeliest sources for closing the gap — James Hornell's *Report on the Government Pearl and Chank Fisheries* and the Madras Fisheries Bulletin series, both on archive.org — were identified but never opened.

## Horses and livestock: mortality claims stand as Polo's own, and the "zebu at Kerman" link stays open

Three livestock passages were worked through directly from the Gutenberg Yule text. In **Book III, Ch. XVII ("Concerning the Kingdom of Maabar")**, Polo claims the Maabar king alone wants "more than 2000 horses a year" (with four neighboring kings wanting about as many more), of which "not above 100... will remain alive" by year's end, attributing the die-off to local ignorance of horse care, a total absence of farriers (deliberately kept out by importing merchants protecting repeat sales), and a diet of boiled rice and meat; he adds flatly that "horses cannot be bred in this country." Each of these is Polo's own claim and should be reported as such, not treated as independently confirmed. Yule's footnotes supply real but non-identical corroboration: the Persian historian **Wassāf** (quoted in Note 7) gives administratively precise, differently sourced numbers — 1,400 horses/year under one merchant contract, 10,000/year under Atábek Abu Bakr — which corroborate large import *volume* but not Polo's specific >95% mortality *rate*, and Wassāf's own explanation for horse decline (overfeeding for 40 days followed by hard, unconditioned riding) is a distinct husbandry account from Polo's farrier-blocking explanation. Critically, **Yule's own Note 13 states the total-non-breedability claim "was exaggerated to impossibility,"** and quotes an 1874 colonial veterinary report (Chamberlain) listing climate, housing, improper feeding, and poor management as compounding causes — a multi-causal account, explicitly marked "H.Y." in the text and therefore part of the original 1903 apparatus, not a 1920 Cordier addition. No source located anywhere in this research — not Yule, not Cordier, not a modern general-audience overview of the medieval Indian Ocean horse trade — attributes the breeding failure to a deliberate Sultanate import-dependence policy; that explanation should not be asserted.

The Camadi/Reobarles oxen passage in **Book I, Ch. XVIII** describes white, short-thick-horned, humped cattle that kneel like camels to be loaded — a strong phenotypic match for zebu (*Bos indicus*). Yule's Note 2 assembles corroboration spanning Mas'udi (10th c., kneeling oxen at Rai near Tehran), the *Āʾīn-i-Akbarī* (a Bengal kneeling breed), Assyrian Nineveh sculptures, and 19th-century observers Abbott and **Houtum-Schindler**, the latter reporting the kneeling humped oxen as "now very rare between Kermán and Bender 'Abbás" by his own time. None of this is site- and century-specific confirmation for Kerman circa 1290: the Encyclopaedia Iranica's "Cattle" entry independently reports that archaeozoologists date zebu imports into "southeastern Iran" to two windows, the 8th century CE and the 12th–14th centuries CE — a window that brackets Polo's journey but names no province and does not distinguish an established breeding population from a transient traded commodity. The verdict here is genuinely unproven rather than confirmed or disputed: the phenotype matches zebu, and the chronological window is compatible, but no study closes the gap between "zebu existed somewhere in the Iranian plateau across many centuries" and "zebu were specifically present at Kerman/Camadi in 1290." The fish-fed-livestock claim at **Esher (Shihr), Book III, Ch. XXXVII**, fares better as a recurring, multi-century, geographically consistent report rather than a single invented detail: Edrisi (12th c.) and Admiral Sidi 'Ali (1553) both attest fish-feeding specifically at Shihr; Ibn Battuta's independent 14th-century testimony is for the nearby but different town of Dhafar, not Shihr itself, and should not be cited as Shihr-specific corroboration; and a 1946 report by E. B. Worthington independently confirms fish-fed camels and cattle still practiced on the same Hadramawt coast near Mukalla and Saihut. No source-critical study tracing whether these witnesses observed independently or simply copied Edrisi was found, so "recurring reports across roughly eight centuries" is the honest framing — not "unbroken continuous practice."

## Crocodilians and hunting animals: Yule's identifications hold, Yunnan zoogeography does not

The Carajan "serpent" passage, **Book II, Ch. XLIX ("Concerning a Further Part of the Province of Carajan")**, describes ten-pace-long, clawed, huge-eyed creatures hunted with staked pits and valued for their medicinal gall. Yule's Note 3 cautiously calls them crocodiles, offering the Ganges gharial only as a loose comparison for the "eyes bigger than a loaf of bread," while explicitly flagging Polo's two-leg/one-claw description as an inaccuracy rather than endorsing it zoologically. A Cordier-marked ("H.C.") addition in the same run of notes brings in an 1895 *North-China Herald* item and an 1879 Fauvel article on Chinese alligators found at Wuhu and Zhenjiang — material about the Yangtze-basin *Alligator sinensis*, geographically distant from Yunnan, and attributable to Cordier's 1920 layer rather than Yule's 1903 original. The one directly relevant modern source located, a 2023 *Scientific Reports* historical-range study of *Alligator sinensis* ([Wang et al., Sci Rep 13](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-36559-6)), places the species' historical range in the Yangtze and Xi river systems and **does not mention Yunnan at all** — so no modern source found here extends any crocodilian's confirmed historical range into 13th-century Yunnan, and the species-level identification (gharial, Siamese crocodile, Chinese alligator, or something else) must be left open rather than asserted.

The hunting-animal material in **Book II, Ch. XVIII ("Of the Lions, Leopards, and Lynxes Kaan Keeps for the Chase")** and **Ch. XX ("How the Emperor Goeth on a Hunting Expedition")** is where Yule's footnotes do genuinely corrective work that should be preserved in any retelling: Note 3 to Ch. XVIII argues Polo's striped "lions" are almost certainly **tigers**, misnamed because medieval Europe had lost a clear concept of the tiger (one bestiary even classed it as a serpent) and because the Persian word "sher" covers both lion and tiger; Note 1 identifies the "leopards" as **cheetahs** (hunting leopards), a distinct animal from true leopards, with corroborating detail on Akbar's roughly 1,000 cheetahs and the practice's spread to Cyprus, Frederick II's Italy, and late-15th-century France; Note 2 identifies the "lynxes" as true **lynx** via the French "loup-cervier," not caracal — no source found here supports a caracal reading. Polo's quantitative claims for the imperial hunt (10,000 falconers, roughly 500 gerfalcons, 10,000 attendants organized as "Toscaol," 10,000 tents) are reported figures only: Yule's Note 2 to Ch. XX offers comparanda from Bayezid's and Muhammad bin Tughluq's courts purely as plausibility checks, explicitly not as independent Yuan-dynasty confirmation, and no *Yuan shi* or other Chinese administrative figure was located in this pass to check Polo's numbers directly. Thomas Allsen's *The Royal Hunt in Eurasian History* (2006) is the standard scholarly treatment of exactly this material but was identified only bibliographically — its specific numbers were never retrieved — and should be treated as an uninspected lead, not a source behind any claim in this report.

## Kerman and Cobinan metallurgy: the Chahak steel connection is the clearest overreach to correct

Yule's note to the Kerman chapter (BookRags/Gutenberg p.299) names real turquoise localities — **Shahr-i-Babek and Parez** near Kerman, and Taft near Yezd — while stating plainly that "the finest came from Khorasan," i.e., Nishapur. A 2021 GIA *Gems & Gemology* article independently confirms a modern Shahr-i Babak deposit tied to the Meiduk copper mine and states this deposit "was mentioned in Marco Polo's... text," **citing "Weisgerber, 2004"** ([GIA, Fall 2021](https://gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-2021-persian-turquoise-ancient-treasure-neyshabur)) — but the GIA article's bibliography did not render in the page fetched, a direct attempt to reach the underlying METALLA-journal article was blocked by bot protection, and no other route to the full Weisgerber (2004) citation succeeded. The GIA sentence also carries a date range, "between 1390 and 1430," that does not correspond to Polo's lifetime (c.1254–1324) and most likely refers to a manuscript or print edition, not to Polo's own era — a detail that should not be repeated uncritically. The Weisgerber citation chain is therefore **reported only as far as GIA's own text**, not verified at the primary-source level, and no archaeometallurgical dating evidence for 13th-century turquoise extraction at any Kerman-area site was found.

The ondanique-steel question produced the clearest correction to a pre-existing research assumption. Polo's Cobinan chapter (Book I) states the town "produces iron, steel, and ondanique, and makes large, fine steel mirrors"; Yule's etymological note traces "Ondanique" (also *Andaine*, *Andanicum*) to a proposed root *Hundwaniy*, "Indian steel," linking the term to classical and medieval India's wootz-steel reputation rather than asserting local Kerman manufacture outright. The archaeometallurgical site most often invoked for this passage, **Chahak**, was directly checked against its own publications: Alipour & Rehren's 2014 paper, confirmed via UCL Discovery as *Journal of Islamic Archaeology* 1(2): 231–261, DOI [10.1558/jia.v1i2.24174](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1462402/1/Alipour%20et%20al.%20Persian%20Pul%C4%81d%20Production%20.pdf) — **not** the *Journal of Archaeological Science* that an earlier routing hint assumed — places the site at the historic junction of Pars, Yazd, and Kerman provinces (administratively in Yazd today), explicitly calls it unexcavated, and reports its own date assignment as contested between Seljuk (11th–12th c., per surface pottery) and Safavid (16th c., per an official heritage-registry alternative), anchored mainly by two texts (Ibn al-Balkhi, 12th c.; Tusi's 13th-century *Tansukh-Nama*) rather than firm excavation dating. **The paper itself never mentions "ondanique" and never mentions Marco Polo.** A later, distinct 2020 Alipour et al. paper on chromium crucible steel at the same site, confirmed via a secondary Sci.News summary as DOI [10.1016/j.jas.2020.105224](https://www.sci.news/archaeology/chromium-crucible-steel-persia-08879.html) in the *Journal of Archaeological Science* (the UCL press release itself failed to load and was not independently confirmed), likewise makes no Kerman or Polo connection. The Chahak-equals-Polo's-ondanique-source link is, on the evidence actually inspected, **an inference a retelling project might choose to draw, not one the primary archaeometallurgical literature asserts** — it should be labeled disputed/unconfirmed, not confirmed. The tutia/spodium passage fares better: Polo's description of smoke and moisture condensing on an iron grating to form tutia, with the furnace residue as spodium, is corroborated by a convergent set of 19th-century witnesses in Yule and Cordier's notes — **Houtum-Schindler** (tutia historically denoting several substances, including "an impure zinc ore"), **Major Sykes** (a local khan reporting the same process still practiced in Sykes's own time, with zinc sulphate found nearby), and **Heyd** (spodium as general combustion/smelting residue) — consistent with the modern chemical understanding of tutty as crude zinc oxide, but this rests entirely on 19th/early-20th-century travelers' and philologists' accounts collated by Yule and Cordier, not on a modern lab analysis of excavated material, which was not found.

## Minerals and money: the salt-currency passage is the cleanest base-text/Ramusio split found

The single most clean-cut textual result across all five clusters is the **Caindu salt-currency passage, Book II, Ch. XLVII**. The base Z/F-tradition text, directly recovered from the Gutenberg Yule text, states only that salt is boiled and molded (flat below, round above), each piece weighing about half a pound, at a fixed ratio of **80 moulds of salt = 1 saggio of fine gold**, with no mention of a stamp or of exclusive royal manufacture. Yule's Note 4 explicitly identifies the shape comparison ("as big as a twopenny loaf"), the Prince's mark on the cakes, the restriction to royal officers, and the variable 60/50/40-piece exchange ratios as **Ramusio-only additions** — a textbook case of the brief's requested base-text-versus-Ramusio distinction, fully supported by the source. A separate note (Note 5) cites Garnier's real-world salt cakes at roughly a picul (≈133⅓ lb), useful as a modern comparandum but not a confirmation of Polo's own half-pound figure. The companion **Yunnan cowrie-currency** passage (Ch. XLVIII) states "white porcelain shells found in the sea" trade at 80 shells per unit of silver (≈2 Venice groats); Yule's own note flags the "piccoli" figure itself as a doubtful manuscript reading, independent of any Ramusio question, and confirms cowries (Polo's own term *porcellani*) were still circulating in Bengal and Yunnan as of Yule's late-19th-century writing. Here a specific sourcing error was pre-empted rather than committed: **Peng & Zhu's 1995 archaeological study** (*Sino-Platonic Papers* 68), directly read in full, explicitly scopes itself to the Neolithic-through-Han period and states outright that "the latter period of cowry use will not be discussed here," referring to Tang-through-Qing Yunnan — meaning it **cannot** be cited as archaeological evidence for Polo's 13th-century Yuan-era cowrie currency, a conclusion drawn directly from the paper's own stated scope rather than inferred. The correct source for that later period, **Bin Yang's 2012 article** in *China Report*, was confirmed to exist and to be on-topic (Yunnan-Bengal cowrie and trade-route connections) only at the abstract level via an IDEAS/RePEc listing; its specific claims about cowrie species or exact supply routes were never independently verified in full text, and remain a gap.

Four other mineral passages were checked with comparably mixed results. Badakhshan's balas rubies (**Book I, Ch. XXIX**) are confirmed to match a real locality, **Kuh-i-Lal** in Tajik Badakhshan, and the adjoining lapis-lazuli mountain matches the historic **Sar-i-Sang** mine — both well-documented historic deposits — but Polo's specific claim of a royal mining monopoly with capital punishment for trespassers was not independently corroborated for the 13th century in any source found; the nearest comparable control account (Al-Biruni, on fire-setting extraction) predates Polo by roughly two centuries. The Chingintalas asbestos passage (**Book I, Ch. XLII**) is a genuine confirmed case of Polo correctly debunking the European salamander myth ("the Salamander is no beast... but is a substance found in the earth"), while Chingintalas's own location remains explicitly unresolved in Yule's own 1903 survey of rival 19th-century proposals (Klaproth, Vámbéry, Rashiduddin's "Kem-kém-jút," Ney Elias), with no modern scholarly consensus located to update it. The Ceylon king's-ruby anecdote (**Book III, Ch. XIV**) and the Mutfili eagle-and-meat diamond legend (**Book III, Ch. XIX**) both turn out, on Yule's own evidence, to be instances of much older and wider travelers'-tale traditions — the ruby story traceable to Cosmas Indicopleustes (6th c.) and recurring in Hiuen Tsang, Hayton, Ibn Battuta, and Friar Jordanus; the diamond legend traceable through Epiphanius, Edrisi, Kazwini, a 1259 Chinese account of Hulagu's campaigns, and explicitly (per Yule) closest of all parallels to the Sindbad tale — so both should be reported as recurring legend-genre material rather than as uniquely verifiable 13th-century events, even while the underlying place identification for Mutfili (Motupalle, Telingana, the Kakatiya dynasty under Rudrama Devi) is itself confirmed directly from Yule's text. The "black stones" coal passage (**Book II, Ch. XXX**) is the most secure of all: it is one of the most historically solid Polo-reality correspondences, though the exact verbatim footnote text for Ch. XXX itself was never retrieved in this pass — the coal corroboration rests on a secondary source's citation plus Yule's adjacent notes on Shanxi coal and river transport, not on a direct quotation of the Ch. XXX note.

## Named scholarly disagreements and unresolved cruxes

Several genuine points of divergence or unresolved questions surfaced, and each should be reported as open rather than resolved. First, **Yule's base-translation text versus Ramusio's Italian print tradition** disagree twice in material inspected here: on whether both sexes or only Zardandan men wore gold tooth-cases, and on the entire stamped-cake/royal-officer/variable-ratio apparatus for Caindu salt currency, which exists only in Ramusio and not in the earlier Z/F-tradition base text Yule otherwise translates. Second, **Yule's own 1903 view versus the sources he quotes** diverges on Indian horse-breeding failure: Yule calls total non-breedability "exaggerated to impossibility," while Wassāf's explanation (overfeeding and hard unconditioned use) and the 1874 Chamberlain report (climate, housing, feed, and management jointly) are not the same explanation as each other, let alone as Yule's own exaggeration thesis — three non-identical accounts coexist in the footnote apparatus itself. Third, on **Chahak's own excavation status**, the site's official heritage-registry dating has been recorded as both Seljuk (11th–12th c.) and Safavid (16th c.) by different registrations, an internal disagreement the 2014 Alipour & Rehren paper itself reports rather than resolves, leaving open whether the site's crucible-steel production bridges Polo's own era at all. Fourth, this research could not confirm or deny whether later Arabic/European geographers reporting fish-fed livestock at Shihr (Ibn Battuta at Dhafar, Sidi 'Ali in 1553) observed independently or simply repeated Edrisi's 12th-century claim — a source-critical question with no study located to settle it either way. Fifth, the relationship between an unverified "practice continued until 1885" claim and an unverified "banned in 1856" claim for pearl-fishery shark-charming remains a genuinely open discrepancy: neither date could be traced to any source actually read, so no position on which (if either) is correct, or whether they describe the same administrative action, can be taken here.

## Conclusion

The pattern across all five clusters is consistent enough to generalize: wherever Polo's own sentence can be quoted directly against a securely located chapter, the retelling is on solid ground, and wherever Yule's own 1903 footnote draws a contrast (Ramusio's variant reading, an exaggerated absolute claim, a philological identification), that contrast is usually recoverable and should be preserved rather than flattened. The research also surfaces a specific failure mode worth naming for anyone extending this work: modern-sounding secondary citations — GIA's "Weisgerber (2004)," a routing hint's "2014/2015 JAS paper" for Chahak, undated claims about an 1856 ban or an 1885 end-date for shark-charming — repeatedly dissolve on direct inspection into either a different venue than assumed, a citation that cannot be traced past its secondary source, or no source at all. The single highest-value next step is not broader search but narrower, deeper reading of the handful of works named but never opened here — Moule & Pelliot for manuscript variants across nearly every topic, Hornell and the Madras Fisheries Bulletins for the shark-charmer dates, and Bin Yang's full text for 13th-century Yunnan cowrie supply routes — since each is already identified precisely enough to fetch, and each sits directly behind one of this report's remaining "partial" or "not found" verdicts.
