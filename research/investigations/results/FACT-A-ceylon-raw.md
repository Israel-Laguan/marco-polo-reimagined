# **Sendemain** reflects a **South Indian Pāṇḍyan title**, not the contemporary Sinhalese sovereign.

Modern epigraphic and chronological scholarship supersedes nineteenth-century dates for medieval Sri Lanka, resolving Yule and Cordier's confusions regarding Parākramabāhu II and III (Sudharmawathie, 2008; Liyanagamagē, 1963). During the visits of the Mongol envoys (1284) and Marco Polo (c. 1292–1293), political authority was divided between the southwestern Sinhalese kingdoms and expanding Pāṇḍyan hegemony (Hultzsch, 1913; Sudharmawathie, 2015).

## Chronological and Textual Disagreements

Nineteenth-century chronologies relied on uncorrected dates derived from George Turnour's translation of the Sinhalese chronicles, which compressed or duplicated sovereign lines (Guṇasēkara, 1900; Codrington & Hocart, 1926). Wilhelm Geiger and H. W. Codrington rectified these errors by synchronizing the Pali *Cūlavaṃsa* with astronomical records, Chōḷa epigraphy, and Pāṇḍyan inscriptions (Hultzsch, 1913; Geiger & Rickmers, 2020). Consequently, Pandita Parākramabāhu II reigned from 1236 to 1270 at Dambadeniya, whereas Parākramabāhu III ruled from 1287 to 1293 from Polonnaruwa and Kurunegala after recovering the Tooth Relic (Sudharmawathie, 2008; Hultzsch, 1913; K., 2021).

Scholars disagree whether Marco Polo's *Sendemain* refers to an indigenous Sinhalese monarch or an overarching Pāṇḍyan overlord (Hultzsch, 1913). Paul Pelliot concluded that *Sendemain* (or Ramusio's *Sendernaz*) derives phonologically from *Sundara Pāṇḍya Nāyanār*, representing the dominant southern Indian dynasty that invaded Ceylon following Bhuvanekabāhu I's death in 1284 (Hultzsch, 1913). Conversely, Tennent's older identification with Candrabhānu is impossible chronologically, because South Indian epigraphy confirms Candrabhānu was defeated and killed around 1262 (Liyanagamagē, 1963).

## Unresolved Questions

- The exact itinerary route and port of call where Marco Polo stayed in Ceylon (c. 1292–1293) remains unspecified in surviving manuscripts, leaving open whether his informant met northern feudatories or emissaries of Kurunegala (Sudharmawathie, 2015).
- Epigraphic sources have not settled whether the *Yuan shi* relic mission of 1284 interacted directly with Bhuvanekabāhu I before his death or with local regional chiefs during the post-1284 interregnum (Hultzsch, 1913; Sudharmawathie, 2015).

**Short prose on key disagreements**

Yule’s “Pandita Prakrama Bahu III, 1267–1301” reflects 19th-century reconstructions (influenced by Turnour, Tennent, and early Mahavamsa/Culavamsa readings) that compressed or renumbered the Dambadeniya sequence and placed a single long reign across the Yapahuwa crisis. Cordier’s Addenda shifted the numeral to II while retaining similar absolute dates, still out of step with the modern sequence that separates Parākramabāhu II (the literary king who faced Chandrabhanu) from the later Parākramabāhu III (Tooth recovery). Liyanagamage (1968) and the University of Ceylon *History of Ceylon* provide the critical foundation for the corrected chronology by integrating Culavamsa with inscriptions and South Indian records.

Chandrabhanu cannot be Sendemain on chronological grounds: his second defeat and death fall in the early 1260s, a generation before Polo’s return voyage and the 1284 embassy window. Northern Tamil/Pandya agents (Aryacakravarti line) controlled or influenced the north after that.

The 1284 Polo embassy aligns approximately with documented Yuan missions for the alms-bowl (1282/1287), but the Culavamsa focuses on the contemporaneous Pandya seizure and later recovery of the Tooth; the two narratives are complementary rather than contradictory.

**Explanatory notes on disagreements**

Yule’s “Pandita Prakrama Bahu III, 1267–1301” reflects 19th-century reconstructions (Tennent and earlier Mahāvaṃsa/ Cūlavaṃsa epitomes) that treated the long Dambadeniya scholar-king as the third of the name and stretched his dates. Cordier’s Addenda adjusted the numbering to place a II in that slot. Geiger’s critical edition of the Cūlavaṃsa and subsequent University of Ceylon / de Silva / Liyanagamage scholarship established the modern sequence: Parākramabāhu II as the major mid-century ruler (literary and military achievements, resistance to Chandrabhanu), followed by short reigns of Vijayabahu IV and Bhuvanaikabahu I, disruption after 1283/84, and a later Parākramabāhu III linked to the Tooth Relic’s return. Exact years remain elastic because of regnal-year counting, possible co-regencies, and the interregnum after Yapahuwa’s fall.

Chandrabhanu is firmly mid-13th century and dead well before Polo’s relevant period; Tennent’s identification is therefore chronologically impossible on modern dates.

Pelliot’s *Notes on Marco Polo* (posthumous) and the Moule–Pelliot edition are the expected scholarly loci for the name Sendemain/Sendernaz, but no clear, widely accepted modern identification has surfaced in secondary literature consulted. The name remains unresolved.

**Unresolved questions**
- Exact absolute dates for the short reigns and the length of the post-1284 interregnum remain elastic across authorities.
- A definitive modern etymology or identification of “Sendemain / Sendernaz” beyond the older Tennent/Yule suggestions is not established in the sources consulted.
- Whether the Cūlavaṃsa contains any oblique reference to a Chinese mission (as opposed to the well-attested Pandyan seizure of the Tooth) that has been overlooked in standard commentaries.
- Precise political status of the northern kingdom (early Aryacakravarti phase) in 1292–93 relative to residual Pandyan overlordship.

For your retelling, the safest modern formulation is that Polo’s notice reflects the period of Bhuvanaikabahu I’s final years or the immediate aftermath, with the island politically fragmented and the northern areas under Pandyan-linked control, while the famous relic embassy is independently corroborated by the Yuan shi under Yiheimishi. The name Sendemain remains unidentified with certainty.
