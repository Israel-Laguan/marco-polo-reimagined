Editorial Source Audit for a Historically Responsible Retelling of Marco PoloAcross the six publication-critical dossiers examined for an English retelling of Marco Polo’s book, direct inspection of accessible primary excerpts, bibliographic metadata, and specialist scholarship resolves several source-identification errors while exposing crucial gaps where primary manuscripts could not be verified in full. Verified corrections include separating Darlim and colleagues’ 2023 study of an extinct Quaternary alligator (Alligator munensis) from Thailand from Hansford and colleagues’ 2023 conservation paleobiology abstract on Alligator sinensis; refuting Shirdam and colleagues’ attribution of Marco Polo’s Book of the Wonders of the World reference to 1390–1430 against Polo’s 1298 composition date; distinguishing the floating buoy on the Socotran whaling line from the casks used solely for head oil in accessible English renderings; confirming that Oscar Raphael had already rejected the 1204 Fourth Crusade provenance of San Marco’s porcelain jar as too early in 1932; and showing that the 1856 ban cited in Seeing Like the Sea and the 1885 termination reported by Kunz and Stevenson both concern British colonial Ceylon’s Gulf of Mannar pearl fishery rather than different regions, leaving their conflicting dates unresolved without the underlying colonial records.Equally critical for editorial integrity is marking what remains unverified. Full witness texts of Franco-Italian F, Toledo Latin Z, and Giovanni Battista Ramusio’s 1559 Italian Navigationi et viaggi could not be inspected directly beyond catalog metadata, front matter, and secondary English excerpts. Several key specialist works—including Hans Ulrich Vogel’s chapter on Caindu salt money and the full 35-page PDF of Siriwardana, Dissanayake, and Çakırlar’s 2024 Mannar midden study—were likewise accessible only through abstracts, tables of contents, or brief extracts. Those gaps are explicitly flagged not found so that cautious, witness-specific phrasing replaces unsupported extrapolation.Investigation of Priority Questions1. Socotra: Ambergris and Whale Hunting [III-07]Polo textual witnesses. Full chapter texts of Franco-Italian F, Toledo Latin Z, Ramusio (1559), and Yule–Cordier Volume II, Book III, Chapter XXXII could not be accessed directly; Heidelberg’s digitized Ramusio Volume 2 record yielded only bibliographic metadata, and Project Gutenberg’s Yule–Cordier Volume 2 yielded only the table of contents. Direct comparison of which witnesses include or omit the hunting mechanics is therefore not found. However, accessible English quotations preserve the exact equipment distinction across two related phrasings. A 2015 Indian Journal of History of Science (IJHS) article explicitly cites "Yule (Tr. & Ed.), 1903, pp. 425-426" for Socotra stating that ambergris is "voided from the entrails of whales" and that islanders "extract the ambergris from the bellies and from their head they procure casks of (spermaceti) oil". Cordier’s Notes and Addenda to Yule’s edition quotes the phrasing "great object of trade, the people contrive to take the whales with barbed ... A long cord is attached to this end, to that a small buoy which floats on". Elsewhere in the same 2015 IJHS paper (and in a 1927 The Naturalist extract), a parallel English rendering reads: "barbed iron, which they strike into the whale so firmly that it cannot be drawn out. To the iron a long line is fastened with a buoy at the end", after which they "drag it to the shore, and proceed to extract the ambergris from its belly, whilst from its head they procure several casks of (spermaceti) oil" (echoed in a ProQuest dissertation abstract citing "barbed iron darts attached to a buoy"). An unlocated Italian Il Milione snippet also reads "un palo di legno grosso e forte, e in quel palo è fitto un ferro d...", though its manuscript and chapter context are obscured. Crucially, in every accessed English witness, the floating marker attached to the cord or line is a buoy ("small buoy" / "buoy at the end"), whereas casks appear exclusively for storing oil extracted from the whale’s head.Editorial and scholarly arguments. The introduction to a modern English edition of The Travels observes that the Franco-Italian Paris version behaves like an abridgement of a fuller earlier version whose omitted material sometimes survives in Ramusio and the Toledo Latin manuscript found in 1932. Luigi Foscolo Benedetto (1928) and more recent Italian philological scholarship by Samuela Simion and Burgio (2015, 2017) characterize Ramusio’s 1559 edition as a composite reservoir preserving valuable older textual strata alongside editorial interventions. Without inspecting Ramusio’s 1559 Italian text, the exact Italian noun behind Yule’s "buoy" versus "casks" remains unverified.Independent historical and archaeological evidence. Classical and Late Antique archaeology on Socotra confirms long-distance Indian Ocean trade without mentioning ambergris in the accessed texts: the first-century CE Periplus Maris Erythraei (Casson 1989, pp. 69, 169–170, cited by Jansen van Rensburg 2018) places Socotra under Hadhramaut rule with Arabian, Greek, and Indian traders on its north coast, while Hoq Cave preserves multilingual inscriptions from the first century BCE to sixth century CE, and the opening pages of Hatke’s 2025 historical survey summarize sixth- to sixteenth-century insularity and maritime connectivity. Closer to Polo’s era, Ibn al-Mujāwir (13th century, partially translated by G. Ducatez and discussed in an OpenEdition monograph) explicitly lists frankincense and ambergris among the resources of Ash-Shiḥr on the southern Arabian coast opposite Socotra, where University of Pisa excavations at a related urban site record archaeological occupation lasting until the eleventh–twelfth centuries (a blog assigns Ibn al-Mujāwir to 1232, and a truncated Brill preview notes his coverage of ambergris in Dhofar and Socotra). Earlier Arabic authors quoted secondhand in the 2015 IJHS paper—Abu Zaid (citing 1867–1887, pp. 1–11), Sulaiman, Al-Mas‘udi (c. 950 CE), and Al-Idrisi (1099–1186 CE)—similarly record storm-washed anbar along Yemen’s shores and across the Indian Ocean.Inference and suggested provenance wording. Regional medieval evidence establishes coastal ambergris collection and commerce along the southern Arabian littoral opposite Socotra by the thirteenth century, yet offers no independent confirmation of active Socotran harpoon-whaling or Ramusio’s buoy-and-cask technique. Do not state that a floating barrel or cask was tied to the harpoon line; use the following provenance note:In the text compiled by Giovanni Battista Ramusio (1559) and printed in brackets by Henry Yule, Socotran whalers strike whales with a barbed iron attached to a long cord and a small floating buoy, haul the carcass ashore, remove ambergris from its belly, and take casks of oil from its head. Because the Franco-Italian (F) and Toledo Latin (Z) manuscripts and Ramusio’s original Italian wording were not directly verified here, this whaling procedure should be presented as attested in the Ramusio/Yule tradition rather than confirmed across Polo’s earliest witnesses.2. Mutfili: Diamonds, Meat, and Eagles [III-06]Polo textual witnesses. Secondary scholarship explicitly verifies the diamond-valley episode in Yule–Cordier Volume II, Book III, Chapter XIX (cited as "Yale and Cordier 1993, 2:360–62" in a Yale University Press history of diamonds). A folklore compilation by Cor Hendriks quotes Yule’s Chapter XIX translation, including the detail "And, when the eagles themselves are taken, diamonds are found in their stomachs", along with eagles picking up meat with clinging diamonds and men gathering diamonds from eagle droppings. Direct inspection of F, Z, Ramusio, and full critical variants was not possible, so manuscript differences remain not found.Editorial and scholarly arguments. As quoted in Hendriks’s compilation, Yule describes the episode as "very ancient and widely diffused", noting that "Sindbad the Sailor relates the story, as is well known, and his version is the closest of all to our author's", while Henri Cordier adds a bracketed pointer to Les Merveilles de l'Inde (pp. 128–129, i.e., Buzurg ibn Shahriyar). Neither Yule’s accessible commentary nor any other accessed source—including a January 2026 blog briefly mentioning Laufer on "diamond-points"—proposes a documented historical transmission route linking Epiphanius, Buzurg, or Sindbad to Polo; studies by Laufer and Wittkower were inaccessible.Earlier literary parallels and independent historical evidence. Full primary texts, manuscripts, and scholarly editions of Epiphanius, Buzurg ibn Shahriyar, and Sindbad’s Second Voyage could not be inspected directly (the accessed Project Gutenberg Burton text stops at the introductory Sindbad frame). Only secondhand bibliographic references and summaries appear in the accessed corpus: a footnote in Mapping Frontiers Across Medieval Islam cites Epiphanius’s De XII gemmis in Opera (Leipzig: Weigel, 1862, 4:190–1[...]), while Hendriks quotes Burton (V, 342 n. 1) on Epiphanius’s Scythian valley where condemned watchers recover gems carried upward by eagles on lamb flesh, alongside Benjamin of Tudela (traveling 1160–1173, citing Keller III, 441), an Alexander Romance vulture-and-roasted-sheep version (Leeuwen 1999, 104f), the Genoese manuscript of Usodimare, and Sindbad’s Second Voyage (Chauvin, BOA VII, 10f; placed on the 549th night in a 2026 blog). Independently, South Asian historical scholarship identifies Polo’s Mutfili with the Kakatiya port of Motupalli in Andhra Pradesh (flourishing under a female ruler’s reign in fine textiles and gem trade) and links the diamond reference to Krishna Valley deposits such as the Kollur area. Separating folklore from extraction, the 2026 blog cites Proctor (p. 388) on diamonds adhering naturally to grease, and Hendriks quotes Yule’s reference to Valentine Ball ([1879]) observing sacrificial meat offerings carried off by birds of prey near Indian mines. No thirteenth-century archaeological or documentary evidence of actual Krishna Valley diamond-mining methods was retrieved.Inference. Mutfili’s port commerce under the Kakatiya dynasty belongs to verifiable medieval South Asian trade history, whereas the eagles-and-meat episode reproduces a centuries-old Eurasian and Indian Ocean literary motif rather than documented mining practice. Keep the two clearly separated in the retelling by treating Motupalli’s diamond commerce as historical backdrop and framing the meat-and-eagle method explicitly as a circulating regional legend or traveler’s marvel.3. Crocodilians in Carajan [II-07]Polo textual witnesses. No primary Polo manuscript (F, Z, Ramusio) or Yule–Cordier text for Carajan was accessible. Only a November 2014 Chinese Academy of Sciences (Man and the Biosphere) article paraphrases Polo in Yunnan describing beasts with "a forelimb growing on each side of their head". Full witness verification is not found.Modern paleontological and scholarly distinctions. The two 2023 studies confused in an earlier report are wholly distinct. Darlim, Suraprasit, Chaimanee, Tian, Yamee, Rugbumrung, Kaweera, and Rabi (2023) (Scientific Reports 13:10406, 13 July 2023, DOI 10.1038/s41598-023-36559-6) name an extinct deep-snouted species, Alligator munensis sp. nov. (holotype DMR-BSL-2011–2), from a Quaternary pond deposit at Ban Si Liam, Nakhon Ratchasima Province, Thailand, dated in a Tübingen/IDW press release to "not more than about 200,000 years ago". While the authors suggest A. munensis and A. sinensis shared a common ancestor across the Yangtze–Xi and Mekong–Chao Phraya lowlands, the fossil is Pleistocene Thai material, not medieval Yunnan evidence. Separately, Hansford, Saupe, Turvey, Ma, Tu, Varnham, and Mannion (2023) published a conference abstract, "Using Past Archives to Better Constrain The Future of Alligator Sinensis" (Bulletin of the Florida Museum of Natural History 60(2):85, 16 Feb 2023, DOI 10.58782/flmnh.xwfy9716), outlining ecological niche modeling and past archives to define IUCN Green Status recovery baselines; only the abstract metadata page was accessible, revealing no Yunnan data.Independent zoological, archaeological, and historical evidence. No accessed source supplies thirteenth-century specimens or dated local records confirming a single crocodilian species in Yunnan around 1300. A March 2012 CITES China scientific bulletin includes Yunnan within the historical/regional distribution of the Siamese crocodile (Crocodylus siamensis) alongside Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, Thailand, and Vietnam, and a November 2018 Banyuetan news report documents two small crocodiles of unconfirmed wild provenance recovered along the Nanlei River in Menglian County, Yunnan; both are modern records of moderate to low evidentiary value for the thirteenth century. A February 2025 Chinese Academy of Social Sciences (CASS) PDF fragment asserts that mugger crocodiles (Crocodylus palustris, 泽鳄) once lived in Yunnan’s Red and Lancang rivers in "ancient times", but provides no date, site, or primary citation. In Chinese historical geography, a 2019 Journal of Natural Resources bibliography (DOI 10.31497/zrzyxb.20190717) cites WEN H. R. (1980, Yunnan Forestry Survey and Planning, pp. 1–44) and an East China Normal University study on historical shifts in "Malayan gharial" distribution, neither of which could be accessed in full. Elsewhere in southern China, Iijima, Yoneda, Liu [Jun], et al. (2022) named the extinct Bronze Age gavialoid Hanyusuchus sinensis—linked to Han Yu’s 819 CE crocodile proclamation in the Han River Delta, Guangdong—and Chinese news coverage notes that archaeological crocodilian bones across China were previously over-attributed to A. sinensis; however, those finds concern Bronze Age and Tang-era Guangdong, not Yuan-era Yunnan. Meanwhile, Alligator sinensis is a small (1.5–2 m), relatively docile Yangtze/Huai reptile known from ~7,000-year-old settlement remains, ~3 Ma Late Pliocene Japanese fossils, and Martino Martini’s 1656 Yangtze note, while Crocodylus porosus is recorded for coastal estuaries such as the Pearl River (Zhang Mengwen et al. 1998, cited in 2015 taxonomy).Inference and species-neutral wording. Because neither thirteenth-century Yunnan archaeology nor dated Yuan historical records establish whether Carajan’s beasts were Crocodylus siamensis, another freshwater crocodile, or exaggerated travelers’ lore—and A. sinensis conflicts with both Yunnan’s Mekong/Red River geography and Polo’s reported scale—use strictly species-neutral wording in the retelling:In the rivers and lakes of Carajan, Marco Polo describes formidable, heavily scaled crocodilians with short forelimbs near the head—accounts reflecting large freshwater crocodilians once native to southwestern river systems rather than any single species verifiable from thirteenth-century Yunnan evidence.4. Pearl Fishery and Shark-Charmers [III-05]Polo textual witnesses. Neither F, Z, Ramusio, nor Yule–Cordier’s Maabar pearl-fishery chapter was retrieved in the accessed corpus. Polo’s reported fishery location, diving season, royal share, and shark-charmers’ share are therefore not found for direct witness inspection.Editorial and scholarly arguments (1856 vs. 1885 and Mannar archaeology). Accessible modern scholarship and early twentieth-century compilations reveal a genuine conflict regarding when shark-charmers ceased operating at the same fishery. In Seeing Like the Sea: A Multispecies History of the Ceylon Pearl Fishery, 1800–1925 (Past & Present 254(1):127+, author and publication year not visible in the accessed snippets), the author states that hereditary shark-charmers (kadalkatti in Tamil, hai-banda in Hindi) were "banned by the government in 1856 and did not feature in the twilight decades of the industry in Ceylon" (notes 120–121), adding that "they were not on colonial payrolls". By contrast, George Frederick Kunz (with Stevenson, cited in query as 1908) in The Book of the Pearl states regarding the Ceylon reefs: "Until 1885, one of the most novel features of the fishery was the employment of shark-charmers or 'binders of sharks' {kadal-kotti..." and "finally, in 1885, the shark-charmers were done away with entirely" (repeated without archival sourcing in a 2022 Royal Philatelic Society handout). Both texts clearly describe the same British colonial Ceylon pearl fishery in the Gulf of Mannar and the same ritual specialist practice. (An unrelated appearance of 1856 in The Book of the Pearl and pearl-culture histories refers solely to zoological papers by Küchenmeister and von Hessling.) Separately, Siriwardana, Dissanayake, and Çakırlar (2024) (International Journal of Historical Archaeology 28(4):960–994, DOI 10.1007/s10761-024-00739-5) synthesize Gulf of Mannar shell-midden excavations to demonstrate intensive Pinctada fucata extraction across pre-colonial and colonial eras.Independent historical and archaeological evidence. Neither the archival citations in notes 120–121 of Seeing Like the Sea nor Kunz’s primary source for 1885 could be inspected, leaving unresolved whether an 1856 official ban/payroll removal was followed by informal practice until 1885, or whether one date involves a secondary error or confusion with Tuticorin and the Parava fisheries on the Indian coast (where studies citing James Hornell and 1839 Madras records attest hereditary shark-charmers paid daily wages). For Polo’s own era, no contemporary thirteenth- or fourteenth-century South Asian or Arabic evidence verifying Polo’s diving season, royal share, or charmers’ share was found; accessible regional sources span Strabo’s Roman-era trade (first century BCE–fourth century CE) and nineteenth- to twentieth-century colonial records (Vane 1887; Thurston 1894; Herdman 1905; Hornell 1905, 1909). On the archaeological side, the accessible abstract of Siriwardana, Dissanayake, and Çakırlar (2024) confirms surface surveys and excavated test pits in shell middens around the Gulf of Mannar in Sri Lanka, where ceramic and radiocarbon analyses date peak Pinctada fucata pearl extraction to the tenth through nineteenth centuries, alongside by-catch faunal evidence of changing pearl-bank ecosystems. Because the full 35-page article was inaccessible, individual midden site names and site-by-site dates inside that paper are not found. Those Gulf of Mannar midden results must be kept distinct from published port chronologies at Mantai, dated 2360–2220 cal BCE to 715–967 cal CE in Siriwardana’s December 2024 Ancient Lanka survey (and "about 200BC and 1400AD" in a 2024 ISPRS paper).Inference. Gulf of Mannar shell-midden archaeology confirms sustained, high-impact pearl-oyster exploitation encompassing Polo’s era (10th–19th centuries), yet neither Polo’s exact manuscript shares nor contemporary thirteenth-century corroboration of his royal and charmer percentages is verified here. Moreover, one must not project nineteenth-century British colonial or Parava shark-charmer payroll practices backward across six centuries as proof of thirteenth-century custom, nor assert a single end date between 1856 and 1885 without checking the underlying colonial archives.5. Kerman Turquoise, Steel, and Salt Money [I-07–08; II-07]Shahr-i Babak turquoise and Weisgerber (2004). For Polo’s textual witness, the accessible English edition of The Travels states of Kerman: "in the same mountain there is a vein from which..." (identifying "Turquoises" in Yule–Cordier Book I, Ch. XVII contents), without naming Shahr-i Babak. F, Z, and Ramusio were not directly inspected. In modern scholarship, Shirdam, Shen, Yang, Mokhtari, and Fazliani (2021) (Gems & Gemology, Fall 2021, p. 241) explicitly write: "Other important mines include the Baghu deposit in southern Semnan Province and the Shahr-i Babak deposit in western Kerman Province, the latter of which was mentioned in Marco Polo's Book of the Wonders of the World between 1390 and 1430 (Weisgerber, 2004)". Their bibliography cites Gerd Weisgerber (2004), "The turquoise mines at Nishapur" in Persiens Antike Pracht. Independent chronology refutes the 1390–1430 date for Polo’s text: Polo traveled through Acre and Persia around 1271, composed his book in 1298, and died in 1324. Because Weisgerber (2004) could not be accessed, it remains unknown whether 1390–1430 in Weisgerber referred to a later illuminated manuscript or mining horizon, or reflects a misreading by Shirdam et al. Furthermore, Shirdam et al. explicitly state that "no scientific study has linked" ancient Iranian turquoise artifacts to specific mines. Consequently, the underlying evidence does not establish Shahr-i Babak’s specific exploitation in Polo’s time, and the retelling should speak only of Kerman’s mountains rather than naming Shahr-i Babak as Polo’s deposit.Kerman steel and ondanique. Accessed English translations of Book I, Chapter XVII report "steel and ondanique" / "plenty of veins of steel and Ondanique" in Kerman. Summarizing Yule (I, 93), the 1912 Periplus commentary (p. 80) explains iron and ondanique in the markets of Kerman as Persian andanic—fine steel for swords and mirrors—deriving the term from hundwānīy ("Indian" steel). Independently, an accessible ResearchGate study on Hispano-Arabic and early Castilian metallurgy (covering ca. 950–1400) inspects the contemporaneous thirteenth-century Castilian Lapidary, which reports that iron melted with "medicaments" becomes hard enough to cut all other irons, called alhinde by "the Moors" and "in some lands... named andanico" (also forged as alhynde by captive Domingo Bono at Ceuta in 1285), concluding that alhinde / andanico designates crucible steel and linking it to Polo’s Kerman and Chingintalas accounts. No inspected source mentions Chahak. While Polo’s "veins" implies local Kerman origin, philological and Castilian evidence confirms only the material identity (ondanique = fine Indian-type crucible steel), leaving local Kerman/Chahak smelting versus import unverified without archaeometallurgical evidence.Caindu salt currency. Direct texts of F, Z, Ramusio (1559), critical editions, and Hans Ulrich Vogel’s chapter "Marco Polo on Salt Currencies in Tebet and Caindu (1275–1291)" (Brill ISBN 9789004236981, pp. 285ff) were not accessible. Only two isolated Ramusio notes in Yule–Cordier Book II, Chapter XLVII—"Ramusio alone has 'a great salt lake'" and "Ramusio has '100 bezants daily'"—plus a Reviews in History summary confirming "salt currency and the exchange of salt for gold in Tibet and Caindu" were retrieved. Salt-cake shape, stamping, exclusive manufacture, and exact exchange ratios across witnesses are therefore not found and must be withheld until F, Z, Ramusio, and Vogel’s chapter are directly inspected.6. Porcelain and Indigo [II-14; III-06]Tingui: Dehua versus Changting. Direct witness texts of F, Z, Ramusio, and Yule–Cordier for Tingui were not retrieved. In modern archaeology, Lin Meicun and Ran Zhang (2017) (European Journal of Archaeology 21(1):39–56) cite earlier proposals (Wright 1854: 343–46; Feng 2001: 375–80; Cliff 2015: 221–24) and follow Feng (2001, pp. 378–380) in identifying Zayton with Quanzhou port and Tingui with Dehua (administratively part of Quanzhou). They marshal 1979 Dehua kiln excavations (Dehua Guciyao Kaogu Fajue Gongzuodui, 1979) and extensive twelfth- to fourteenth-century Indian Ocean distributions of Dehua Qingbai stonewares ("Marco Polo wares"), including a thirteenth-century Java shipwreck, Pengkalan Bujang (12th–13th c.), Kota Cina (12th–early 14th c.), Tioman Island, Temasik/Singapore, Manila, southern Indian ports (Periyapattinam, Palaya-Kayal, Motupalli, Kunnattur, Pondicherry, Pattanam), Polonnaruwa in Sri Lanka, the Persian Gulf, and eastern Africa. Conversely, Matilde Crapanzano’s Ca’ Foscari laurea magistrale thesis identifies Tingui with Tingzhou, modern Changting (长汀) ("Il toponimo Tingui75 corrisponde a Tingzhou, l'attuale Changting 长汀"), with adjacent bibliography mentioning Wu, X. and Did Marco Polo go to China?, though footnote 75 and the detailed linguistic or kiln arguments for Changting are truncated in the accessible excerpt. Thus, archaeological export-kiln evidence strongly favors Dehua as a major porcelain supplier, while the Changting counter-identification rests on toponymic arguments not inspectable here; the retelling should present the identification as scholarly debate rather than settled fact.The San Marco porcelain jar. Lin and Zhang (2017) observe that ceramic archaeologists (Whitehouse 1972; Morgan 1991; Horton et al. 1996; Flecker 2003; Kennet 2004) widely attribute the Qingbai jar in the Treasury of San Marco, Venice, to Dehua kilns contemporary with Polo’s late-thirteenth-century return from China (while also noting in their opening excerpt that "no further research has examined the manufacturing and dating evidence for this so-called 'Marco Polo jar'"). Crucially, contemporaneity with Polo’s return does not establish personal ownership or transport by Polo, and no Venetian archival record linking Polo personally to the jar was found. Regarding the older tradition that Doge Enrico Dandolo brought the jar back from Hagia Sophia during the 1204 Fourth Crusade sack of Constantinople, Lin and Zhang explicitly report that Oscar Raphael (1932, p. 14) already rejected the 1204 dating as too early after inspecting the vessel, a dismissal shared by modern ceramic specialists.Coilum (Kollam) indigo production. Direct witness texts (F, Z, Ramusio, Yule–Cordier) for Coilum were not retrieved; only secondary web blogs (Dickinson College’s Mapping the Middle Ages blog, 2018; Malabar Days, 2010/2012) summarize Polo’s account of Quilon (500 miles southwest of Maabar) cultivating pepper, brazilwood, and indigo. (A student study-notes upload erroneously claims Marco Polo wrote letters about a Quilon Latin diocese, conflating him with later Franciscan missionaries such as John of Monte Corvino.) Named Scholarly Disagreements and Their EvidenceTermination of shark-charmers in colonial Ceylon (1856 vs. 1885): Seeing Like the Sea (Past & Present 254(1):127+) reports that the colonial government banned kadalkatti shark-charmers in 1856 and excluded them from colonial payrolls thereafter, whereas Kunz (with Stevenson, cited as 1908) in The Book of the Pearl states that shark-charmers operated at the Ceylon reefs "Until 1885", when they "were done away with entirely". Both refer to the Ceylon Gulf of Mannar fishery; neither accessed excerpt displays the primary colonial decrees, leaving unresolved whether an 1856 payroll ban preceded informal persistence until 1885 or whether one figure stems from error or Indian-coast (Tuticorin) conflation.Identification of Tingui (Dehua vs. Changting): Feng (2001) and Lin Meicun and Ran Zhang (2017) equate Tingui with Dehua (within Quanzhou’s jurisdiction, distinct from the port Zayton), citing 1979 Dehua kiln excavations and widespread twelfth- to fourteenth-century Indian Ocean exports of Dehua Qingbai stonewares. In contrast, Matilde Crapanzano’s thesis identifies Tingui with Tingzhou, modern Changting (长汀), though the philological and kiln evidence in her truncated footnote 75 could not be inspected.Provenance of the San Marco porcelain jar (1204 vs. late thirteenth century): Early Venetian tradition linked the San Marco Qingbai jar to Doge Enrico Dandolo’s 1204 Fourth Crusade loot from Hagia Sophia, whereas Oscar Raphael (1932: 14) already rejected 1204 as too early upon examination, and modern archaeologists including Whitehouse (1972), Flecker (2003), Kennet (2004), and Lin and Zhang (2017) attribute it to late-thirteenth-century Dehua kilns contemporary with Polo’s return.Local Kerman production vs. Indian origin of ondanique: Accessed English Polo texts describe "veins of steel and ondanique" in Kerman’s mountains, whereas Henry Yule (summarized in the 1912 Periplus commentary, p. 80) refers to "markets of Kerman" and derives ondanique (andanic) from hundwānīy ("Indian" steel), supported by the thirteenth-century Castilian Lapidary treating andanico as a synonym for alhinde crucible steel. Whether Kerman smelted crucible steel locally or traded imported ingots remains unproved here.Textual status of Ramusio relative to Franco-Italian F: A modern English edition characterizes the Paris Franco-Italian text as an abridgement of a fuller original partly preserved in Ramusio and the Toledo Latin manuscript, while Luigi Foscolo Benedetto (1928), Samuela Simion, and Burgio (2015, 2017) treat Ramusio as a composite sixteenth-century compilation combining early lost material with later editorial reworking.2.
