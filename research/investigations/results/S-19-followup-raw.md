# Verifying Marco Polo: A Source-by-Source Guide to Corroboration and Discrepancy Across Medieval Testimonies

## The Ilkhanate and Persian Historical Narratives

A central pillar of the argument for Marco Polo's extensive travels through the Mongol world is his detailed account of the Ilkhanate court in Persia [[20](https://hal.science/hal-02943905/document), [22](https://blogs.dickinson.edu/mapping-middle-ages-2025/2025/09/17/the-travels-of-marco-polo-persia/)]. To substantiate these claims, it is essential to consult contemporary Persian historical sources, chief among them being the works of Rashid al-Din Hamadani. The user's request focuses on verifying the diplomatic mission of Princess Kököchin, a key event in Polo's narrative, and assessing the broader reliability of Rashid al-Din's chronicle as an independent witness. The findings indicate that while Rashid al-Din corroborates the major events of the period, including the princess's journey, he does not explicitly name Marco Polo, leaving the direct link between the Venetian traveler and the Persian court an inference rather than a stated fact. This section will dissect the relevant passages in Rashid al-Din's monumental *Jami' al-Tawarikh*, evaluate the quality and accessibility of its English translations, and analyze the transmission lines of information between the East and West during the Ilkhanid era.

The most compelling evidence for the historical accuracy of Polo's narrative comes from the confirmation of major political events he describes. Specifically, the dispatch of a Mongol princess to the Ilkhanate for marriage to Arghun Khan is a well-documented diplomatic maneuver [[123](https://iris.unica.it/retrieve/79e91d50-6520-4cdb-8e53-219a44ad21c0/Oriental%20Contributions%20to%20Franciscan%20Art%20in%20the%20Thirteenth%20and%20Fourteenth%20Centuries.pdf)]. Multiple sources confirm that Kublai Khan ordered a fleet to transport a bride named Kököchin to Persia to secure an alliance with his cousin, Arghun [[119](https://www.iseas.edu.sg/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/nscwps25.pdf), [125](https://www.scribd.com/document/390667631/Kublai-Khan-s-Invasions-in-%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i-Vi%E1%BB%87t-and-Champa)]. This event, which Polo recounts around 1291-1293, falls within the correct chronological window [[121](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Marco_Polo), [122](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo)]. Rashid al-Din, whose work was commissioned by Ilkhan Ghazan and completed under Oljeitu, provides a detailed dynastic history that covers precisely this period [[30](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jame-al-tawarik/), [41](https://www.jewage.org/wiki/ru/Article:Rashid-al-Din_Hamadani_-_Biography)]. His primary source for the early history of the Mongols was Juvayni’s *History of the World Conqueror*, but for his own time, he had access to a wealth of contemporary information, including high-level informants like Bolad Ch’eng-hsiang, a Yuan envoy who provided insights into Mongol records [[30](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jame-al-tawarik/), [31](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/rashid-al-din/)]. This direct line of communication with the Yuan court makes Rashid al-Din's account a crucial point of comparison.

However, a critical examination of these Persian sources reveals a significant nuance: the absence of Marco Polo's name. While Rashid al-Din's narrative confirms the event, he does not name any Westerners accompanying the princess on her journey [[120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans_in_China_during_the_Middle_Ages)]. This omission is telling. It suggests that if the Polos were part of the entourage, their role was not deemed noteworthy enough for the chief historian of the Ilkhanate to record, or that their presence was peripheral. This finding forces a more cautious interpretation of the relationship between the two texts. It does not disprove Polo's participation but reframes it; the event is corroborated, but the specific role attributed to him by Polo remains, for now, unsubstantiated by this particular source. The transmission of information likely flowed both ways. Europeans like Dominican friars and merchants working for the Ilkhans could have provided information about Europe back to Rashid al-Din's scriptorium in Tabriz [[41](https://www.jewage.org/wiki/ru/Article:Rashid-al-Din_Hamadani_-_Biography)], while Persian and Mongol officials traveling to China brought back news to the Ilkhanate [[31](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/rashid-al-din/)]. Therefore, it is plausible that Polo's detailed knowledge of the Ilkhanid court, its geography, and its politics was derived from conversations with returning officials or other travelers, rather than solely from personal observation. This indirect channel of information complicates the simple binary of "Polo was there" or "he must have copied from another source."

For researchers seeking to engage with these primary texts, the availability of high-quality English translations is paramount. The user has correctly identified two key editions. The first is John Andrew Boyle's *The Successors of Genghis Khan*, published in 1971 by Columbia University Press [[25](https://archive.org/details/Boyle1971RashidAlDin), [47](https://onesearch.library.wwu.edu/discovery/fulldisplay/alma9983491810001453/01ALLIANCE_WWU:WWU)]. This volume translates a portion of the *Jami' al-Tawarikh*, specifically covering the dynastic history of the successors of Genghis Khan, which includes the relevant reigns of Abaqa, Arghun, and Ghazan [[46](https://paxmongolica.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/boyle_1971_rashid_al_din.pdf), [53](https://www.scribd.com/doc/288133889/Juvayni-Genghiz-khan-boyle-pdf)]. As a standard scholarly edition, it provides valuable context and is widely cited in academic literature [[49](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000013120)]. However, it is important to recognize its limitations; it is a partial translation and may not contain every detail relevant to comparing with Polo's account [[26](https://dokumen.pub/making-mongol-history-rashid-al-din-and-the-jami-al-tawarikh-9781474421430.html)].

The second, and far more comprehensive, resource is Wheeler M. Thackston's three-volume *Compendium of Chronicles*, published by Harvard University Press between 1998 and 1999 [[27](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/khamseen/category/projects/hands-on/eul-collections/jami-al-tawarikh/), [30](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jame-al-tawarik/)]. This modern translation represents the state-of-the-art in Rashid al-Din scholarship, drawing on the Raushan and Musavi Persian editions from 1994, which themselves rely on the authoritative Z12 manuscript dated October 1318 [[26](https://dokumen.pub/making-mongol-history-rashid-al-din-and-the-jami-al-tawarikh-9781474421430.html)]. Thackston's work covers the entirety of the *Jami' al-Tawarikh*'s first volume, which contains the detailed history of the Mongols and the Ilkhans up to the death of Ghazan [[30](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jame-al-tawarik/)]. For the purpose of comparing with Polo, Volume I is the most relevant. Access to scans of the illustrated manuscripts of the *Jami' al-Tawarikh*, such as the 1314–15 Arabic copy held in Edinburgh and London, can also provide valuable visual context for the cultures and peoples Polo describes [[31](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/rashid-al-din/), [32](https://khalilicollections.org/collections/islamic-art/khalili-collection-islamic-art-the-jami-al-tawarikh-of-rashid-al-din-mss727/)]. These manuscripts, with their blend of Persian, Chinese, and Byzantine artistic elements, reflect the multicultural nature of the Ilkhanid court that Polo inhabited [[31](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/rashid-al-din/), [38](https://imc-leeds.confex.com/imc/2025/prelim.cgi/Paper/10805)]. An open-access digital copy of Thackston's translation is available, though users should verify the specific page numbers against their physical copy [[31](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/rashid-al-din/)].

Beyond the Kököchin affair, Rashid al-Din's work offers broader corroboration of the political landscape described by Polo. For instance, his account of the conflicts between Qaidu, a grandson of Genghis Khan, and Kublai Khan aligns with Polo's descriptions of their battles [[19](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. The *Jami' al-Tawarikh* provides a genealogical framework that confirms Kaidu's lineage as the son of Hasin (also spelled Kashin or Okkodai), a detail also found in the *Secret History of the Mongols* and noted by Yule in his commentary on Polo [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm), [19](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. This consistency across different historical traditions strengthens the overall credibility of Polo's narrative structure, even if it doesn't prove his personal presence. Similarly, Rashid al-Din's mention of foreign engineers sent by Hulagu to assist in the conquest of the Sung dynasty, while differing from Polo's claim of Venetian involvement at Xiangyang, points to a wider pattern of employing skilled craftsmen from across the empire, lending plausibility to the general practice if not the specific individuals [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)].

In summary, Persian historical sources, particularly Rashid al-Din's *Jami' al-Tawarikh*, provide strong contextual corroboration for many of the major political events and figures in Marco Polo's account of the Ilkhanate. The dispatch of Kököchin is confirmed, and the succession of rulers is consistent. However, the failure to name the Polos directly means this is not definitive proof of their presence. The relationship appears to be one of parallel, semi-independent reporting on shared events, rather than direct textual dependence. The existence of multiple recensions of the *Jami' al-Tawarikh* and accusations from contemporaries like Abu’l-Qāsem Qāšāni that Rashid al-Din appropriated others' work raise further questions about absolute textual independence, suggesting a complex historiographical environment [[26](https://dokumen.pub/making-mongol-history-rashid-al-din-and-the-jami-al-tawarikh-9781474421430.html), [30](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jame-al-tawarik/)]. Future research could focus on identifying whether any other, lesser-known Persian chroniclers mentioned the Polos, potentially resolving the question raised by Cleaves's 1976 article [[91](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lic3.12152)]. For now, the evidence places the Polos firmly within the known diplomatic currents of the late 13th century, but stops short of placing them definitively at the center of the action as Polo claimed.

## The Yuan Court and Official Chinese Records

While Persian sources provide context for Marco Polo's journey westward, Chinese historical records offer the ultimate test of his claims regarding his long residence in Yuan China. The user's inquiry into the *History of Yuan* (*Yuanshi*) and related texts strikes at the heart of the debate over Polo's authenticity. The consensus among scholars is that the Chinese annals contain no explicit mention of Marco Polo, his father, or his uncle [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE), [23](https://www.facebook.com/KingsGenerals/posts/why-chinese-sources-dont-mention-marco-polochinese-sources-largely-omit-marco-po/1004078091760266/)]. This conspicuous silence is a major argument used by critics of Polo's narrative. However, as the preliminary analysis indicates, this negative evidence is not the whole story. A deeper investigation reveals that while the Polos are not named, the practices, policies, and events described in the *Yuanshi* often align with the specific details provided by Polo, allowing for a powerful form of contextual verification. This section will explore key areas of comparison—the Xiangyang siege, military campaigns, and administrative structures—and demonstrate how the *Yuanshi* serves as a crucial, albeit indirect, witness to the reality of the world Polo described.

The siege of Xiangyang (modern Xiangfan) stands out as a pivotal moment in the conflict between the Mongols and the Southern Song dynasty, and Polo's account of it is frequently cited as a point of contradiction. In Yule-Cordier's edition, Polo claims that his father and uncle suggested using powerful new catapults capable of hurling 300-pound stones, which ultimately broke the three-year-long stalemate [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)]. He implies that these were novel weapons, perhaps even of Venetian design. However, Yule's notes immediately counter this by citing Persian historians Rashid al-Din and Wassaf, who credit the construction of these siege engines to foreign engineers sent by Hülegü, the Mongol ruler of the Ilkhanate, to aid his nephew Kublai in China [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)]. These engineers are identified by their Muslim names: Ala-ud-din and Ismail [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE)]. This presents a direct conflict between Polo's claim of Venetian involvement and the testimony of contemporary Islamic chroniclers. Further complicating matters, the Chinese *Yuan shi* itself states that the fall of Xiangyang occurred between 1268 and 1273, a date that clashes with the presumed arrival of the Polos at Kublai Khan's court in Dadu (Beijing) after 1274 [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)]. This chronological discrepancy, highlighted by Yule, is a significant challenge to Polo's timeline and weakens his claim of direct involvement in the siege's final stages. While some modern scholars like Hans Ulrich Vogel argue for the broad authenticity of Polo's account based on other evidence, the specific claim about the Xiangyang engineers remains one of the most problematic points of contention [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE), [14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo)].

Despite this notable disagreement, the *Yuanshi* provides substantial corroboration for other aspects of Polo's narrative. For example, when describing Kublai Khan's conquest of Manzi (the former Southern Song territories), Polo mentions a prophecy that the kingdom would fall to a man with "hundred eyes" [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)]. He connects this to the commander Bayan's name. Yule's note brilliantly links this to the Chinese text, which records a prophecy about "a hundred wild geese" (*pe-yen*), phonetically similar to Bayan's Chinese name [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)]. This kind of specific linguistic and cultural detail, confirmed by the official history, strongly suggests that Polo's information was grounded in local knowledge, not mere hearsay. It demonstrates an understanding of Chinese culture that goes beyond what a simple outsider might possess. Similarly, in his account of the war against Burma (Yunnan), Polo describes an elephant battle involving 2,000 elephants carrying watchtowers [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)]. While the *Yuanshi* records the campaign took place in 1277 and involved generals Nasruddin and Kutuka, it specifies that the Burmese used only 800 elephants [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)]. Here, the numbers differ, but the core event—an elephant-based assault—is independently verified. Such quantitative discrepancies are common in pre-modern accounts, but the qualitative agreement on the nature of the battle lends credibility to the overall scene.

The compilation history of the *Yuanshi* itself adds another layer of complexity. Commissioned by the Ming dynasty shortly after they overthrew the Yuan in 1368, the entire history was compiled in an astonishingly short time—reportedly just 331 days—in 1370 [[162](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Yuan)]. This haste led to numerous errors, duplicated biographies, and inconsistencies, a fact noted by later Qing dynasty scholars who cataloged over 3,700 mistakes [[162](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Yuan)]. Some of these errors were compounded in later attempts to correct the text, such as the Qianlong Emperor's effort, which created new transliteration problems [[162](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Yuan)]. Because of this, the *Yuanshi* cannot be treated as a perfect, error-free record. However, its very existence, compiled from captured Yuan records, means it preserves a vast amount of administrative and historical data from the period. When Polo's descriptions of the Yuan bureaucracy, salt production, or currency systems align with the known facts recorded in the *Yuanshi*, it provides powerful circumstantial evidence for his authenticity. For instance, Hans Ulrich Vogel's meticulous research, which draws heavily on the *Yuanshi* and other Yuan-era documents, has shown that Polo's detailed descriptions of currencies and revenues match the actual economic practices of the time, a key piece of evidence supporting his presence in China [[155](https://www.jstor.org/stable/44511252), [156](https://www.academia.edu/4258345/Marco_Polo_Was_in_China_New_Evidence_from_Currencies_Salts_and_Revenues)].

To locate these passages, one must navigate the massive 210-chapter structure of the *Yuanshi*. The biography of Bayan would be found in the relevant imperial biography section (*Benji*), likely in the volumes covering Kublai Khan's reign [[162](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Yuan)]. The records of the Burma campaign would be in the biographies of the commanders involved or in the official records of military campaigns. The biography of Joshua ben Aaron (Ai-sie) from the Byzantine Empire, which notes his appointment to oversee medical and astronomical boards, is found in Volume 118 (*Juàn 118*) of the *History of Yuan* [[120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans_in_China_during_the_Middle_Ages)]. This specific entry corroborates Polo's observations about the cosmopolitan nature of Kublai's court, which included Christian physicians from various backgrounds [[109](https://mrijournal.riccimac.org/index.php/en/issues/issue-13/332-mrij13-2-en), [120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans_in_China_during_the_Middle_Ages)]. While no single passage can be pointed to that says "Marco Polo served here," the cumulative weight of these cross-references builds a convincing picture of a world that matches Polo's descriptions. The lack of a complete, reliable public-domain English translation suitable for direct quotation is a significant obstacle [[162](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Yuan)]. Scholars must rely on partial translations or work directly with the Chinese text. Nonetheless, the *Yuanshi* remains an indispensable tool for any serious scholar attempting to separate fact from fiction in the pages of *The Travels of Marco Polo*.

## Mongol Genealogies and Inner Asian Histories

To assess the depth of Marco Polo's knowledge of the Mongol world, it is insufficient to compare his account merely with contemporary event reports. A deeper verification requires examining whether his understanding of Mongol society, law, and lineage aligns with the foundational texts of Mongol historiography. The user's focus on the *Secret History of the Mongols* is therefore highly strategic. This epic poem, composed in the 13th century, is the most authentic and canonical source for the life of Chinggis Khan and the early Mongol tribal dynamics. While it was written centuries before Polo's travels, its value lies not in confirming his personal presence but in validating the intellectual framework he used to interpret and narrate the world he encountered. A careful comparison reveals that Polo's account, particularly his genealogical explanations and descriptions of tribal relationships, is remarkably consistent with the worldview presented in the *Secret History*. This alignment suggests that his information, whether gathered orally or read from a now-lost source, was fundamentally accurate and rooted in genuine Mongol tradition.

The *Secret History of the Mongols* begins with the divine origins of the clan and traces the lineage of Temüjin (Chinggis Khan) through his father Yesügei and grandfather Dobun-Mergen. Polo's narrative also starts with a genealogical prologue, establishing the noble ancestry of Kublai Khan and his predecessors [[19](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. While the literary forms are different—one is an epic, the other a travelogue—the underlying structure is the same. Both see history as a continuous thread of descent and legitimacy passed down through male heirs. More specifically, the *Secret History* provides the canonical version of Temüjin's birth, said to be clutching a blood clot in his fist, a sign of his future greatness [[135](https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/Rachewiltz-The-Secret-History-of-the-Mongols.pdf)]. While Polo does not recount this specific omen, his detailed account of Kublai Khan's court and his reverence for the legacy of Chinggis Khan suggest a deep familiarity with this foundational mythology.

One of the most striking areas of correspondence is in the portrayal of key political relationships. The *Secret History* devotes significant space to the complex bond between Temüjin and his sworn brother Jamukha, who eventually becomes his rival, and his relationship with his mentor, Toghrul Khan of the Ong (翁) tribe [[95](https://www.scribd.com/document/793669947/Rashid-Al-Din-Jami-u-T-Tawarikh-Thackson-2nd-Edition), [135](https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/Rachewiltz-The-Secret-History-of-the-Mongols.pdf)]. Polo, in his chapter on Kublai Khan's wars against his rivals, discusses the ongoing conflict with Kaidu, a grandson of Chinggis Khan [[19](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. While the *Secret History* does not cover Kaidu directly, it establishes the political and familial logic that defines his rebellion: the struggle for the throne between the descendants of Chinggis's sons, Ögedei and Tolui. This internal logic is precisely what Polo grasps and explains to his readers. He correctly identifies Kaidu as a descendant of Ögedei and frames the conflict as a dynastic struggle for supremacy [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm), [19](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. Yule's notes highlight this accuracy, noting that Polo's careful history of the line of Čengiz Khan and the ensuing wars shows a sound understanding of the period [[19](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. This is not the kind of nuanced political insight an imposter could fabricate; it requires a solid grounding in Mongol history.

The translation of the *Secret History* itself is a critical factor in this comparison. The user's request to engage with different editions highlights the importance of textual fidelity. The classic translation by Francis W. Cleaves, published by Harvard University Press in 1982, is noted for its readability but is sometimes considered less literal [[42](https://archive.org/details/Cleaves1982SecretHistoryMongols), [136](https://www.deremilitari.org/REVIEWS/Rachewiltz_Mongols.htm)]. In contrast, the two-volume translation by Igor de Rachewiltz, published by Brill in 2004, is considered a monumental scholarly achievement, providing extensive apparatus, maps, and a more literal rendering of the original Mongolian text [[34](https://www.academia.edu/72311270/Hayton_of_Korykos_and_La_Flor_Des_Estoires_Cilician_Armenian_Mediation_in_Crusader_Mongol_Politics_C_1250_1350), [136](https://www.deremilitari.org/REVIEWS/Rachewiltz_Mongols.htm)]. De Rachewiltz's work is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial license, making it highly accessible for academic study [[135](https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/Rachewiltz-The-Secret-History-of-the-Mongols.pdf)]. For the purpose of comparing with Polo, de Rachewiltz's version would be superior due to its scholarly rigor and attention to the nuances of Mongol society. Comparing a passage where Polo describes a tribal custom or political alliance with the corresponding section in de Rachewiltz's translation would yield the most precise results. For example, one could examine Polo's description of the Mongol justice system and compare it to the legal and social norms described in the *Secret History*. The fact that both emphasize the importance of loyalty, kinship ties, and swift retribution suggests a shared understanding of Mongol values.

It is also worth considering the possibility of textual transmission. While the *Secret History* was composed in the 13th century, it was written in the Uyghur-based script, which was also used for other administrative purposes in the Mongol Empire [[30](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jame-al-tawarik/)]. Could Polo have read excerpts from it? Unlikely, given the language barrier. However, he could have heard its contents recited or summarized by Mongol nobles. The *Secret History* itself was intended to be a living document, a reminder of the glorious past to legitimize present rule. Its stories and genealogies would have been part of the oral curriculum for Mongol elites. Therefore, Polo's accurate recounting of them can be seen as the result of diligent listening and careful note-taking during his years at the Yuan court. His ability to articulate the complex web of alliances and feuds that defined the Mongol world is a testament to his powers of observation and synthesis, far exceeding those of a simple merchant.

In conclusion, while the *Secret History of the Mongols* cannot serve as a direct witness to Marco Polo's physical presence in 13th-century China, it functions as an invaluable control group for testing the accuracy of his historical and cultural interpretations. The profound consistency between his narrative and the foundational texts of Mongol identity strongly suggests that his account was not a work of pure imagination but was instead built upon a solid foundation of authentic, insider knowledge. The choice of translation is crucial, with modern scholarly editions like de Rachewiltz's providing the most robust basis for comparison. The alignment in genealogy, political dynamics, and social customs transforms the *Secret History* from a mere historical artifact into a powerful ally in the ongoing quest to understand the veracity of Marco Polo's remarkable tale.

## European and Arab Travel Accounts Preceding and Following Polo

A powerful method for verifying Marco Polo's claims is to compare his narrative with the accounts of other travelers who traversed the same regions either before or after him. These contemporaries and successors serve as crucial benchmarks, offering independent observations that can either corroborate or contradict Polo's descriptions. The user's request to analyze the works of Rubruck, Carpini, Odoric, and Ibn Battuta is exceptionally well-targeted, as these four authors span the key geographical zones of Polo's journey: the Mongol heartlands, the Middle East, and China. Their accounts reveal a fascinating dynamic of parallel discovery, shared knowledge, and, in some cases, clear textual dependence. This comparative analysis not only tests the specifics of Polo's narrative but also illuminates the interconnected world of medieval travel literature.

William of Rubruck and Giovanni da Pian del Carpine represent the earliest wave of Franciscan missionaries sent to the Mongol courts in the mid-13th century [[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo)]. Their journeys, undertaken before Kublai Khan consolidated power in China, provide a vital baseline of European knowledge. Rubruck, who reached Karakorum in 1254, offers particularly rich descriptions of Mongol life that predate Polo's account [[108](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)]. He describes the portable felt-covered yurts (*iurt*), the distinctive fermented mare's milk drink, the elaborate palace silver tree that dispensed various beverages, and the unique funeral practices involving tumuli and statues [[108](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)]. Many of these details appear again in Polo's writings, suggesting that these were common features of Mongol life that any visitor would observe. For instance, both Rubruck and Polo describe the practice of taking a wife after a father's death, though Rubruck notes this applies only to consanguinity, not affinity [[108](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)]. This overlap is best explained by independent observation of the same cultural phenomena rather than direct copying. However, the comparison also reveals gaps. Rubruck, writing from a position of religious diplomacy, devotes significant space to the Nestorian Christian communities at the Mongol court and documents a formal religious debate between Buddhist, Muslim, and Christian representatives [[108](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/rubruck.html)]. Polo, while mentioning Christians, focuses more on the secular splendors of Kublai's court and the economic systems of China. This difference in perspective—a diplomat focused on faith versus an administrator focused on commerce—accounts for the divergent emphases in their works.

Odoric of Pordenone, a Franciscan friar who traveled to China in the early 14th century, is perhaps the most interesting case for detecting textual influence. Arriving decades after Polo, Odoric's account is filled with marvels, but also with specific details about Chinese life that were omitted by his predecessor [[45](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESngEBxCJF17bG-4GqMI7vSC1U1g7qf6u0ifC-ZcbFQo_9dqLSj0kcSIJKPRKZhxE5Wge5QsU87jkLtweOqabMwXYxC-7FoABuXc-aEO_9uuMseTPdzlJZrP7Yra271aNm4nkETdNDcUrTo_7r4y2J-KTfuYRgJNTCSpBncTr3RaRXXIxKh6dooA8eJqSTor9gOXf9ijFUCkwaDsoLSIWPqw&gsessionid=c13bV4YMNBIwZRp1r-ZjBqhNkR0APBSl2xKoBXChqyCumIsX2Xp6tQ)]. Yule-Cordier's notes draw several useful comparisons. For instance, while both Polo and Odoric praise Hangzhou (which Odoric calls 'Cansay') as a city of unparalleled beauty, Odoric adds unique observations that Polo misses, such as the practice of cormorant fishing and the custom of binding women's feet [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm), [45](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESngEBxCJF17bG-4GqMI7vSC1U1g7qf6u0ifC-ZcbFQo_9dqLSj0kcSIJKPRKZhxE5Wge5QsU87jkLtweOqabMwXYxC-7FoABuXc-aEO_9uuMseTPdzlJZrP7Yra271aNm4nkETdNDcUrTo_7r4y2J-KTfuYRgJNTCSpBncTr3RaRXXIxKh6dooA8eJqSTor9gOXf9ijFUCkwaDsoLSIWPqw&gsessionid=c13bV4YMNBIwZRp1r-ZjBqhNkR0APBSl2xKoBXChqyCumIsX2Xp6tQ)]. This suggests Odoric was not simply plagiarizing Polo's text but was making his own independent observations. A comparative analysis of the critical Latin edition of Odoric's *Relatio* with Yule-Cordier's version would be necessary to spot any direct borrowings versus the sharing of common knowledge [[44](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESjgEBxCJF1-Ba8DPeC5oELueWDVeKrFGowUMyLHimwt7nPdB9JZPMZLLOuw1_QXcgvNPkD3tWROWVUDYS8YQaiQNfpjMy9UU6wUUMVeuRIsuzCtiYM4GS3GFg0EPvJhh7oZH0GQTA_FxdqNsT_G7vuUVk6IXMlnkRZDqY-rjHykrrz2qT42TrVbRTj2knIG99&gsessionid=c13bV4YMNBIwZRp1r-ZjBqhNkR0APBSl2xKoBXChqyCumIsX2Xp6tQ)]. The fact that Odoric was the first European to enter Lhasa and describe Tibet also indicates he explored regions beyond Polo's known travels, adding new dimensions to the European picture of Asia [[45](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESngEBxCJF17bG-4GqMI7vSC1U1g7qf6u0ifC-ZcbFQo_9dqLSj0kcSIJKPRKZhxE5Wge5QsU87jkLtweOqabMwXYxC-7FoABuXc-aEO_9uuMseTPdzlJZrP7Yra271aNm4nkETdNDcUrTo_7r4y2J-KTfuYRgJNTCSpBncTr3RaRXXIxKh6dooA8eJqSTor9gOXf9ijFUCkwaDsoLSIWPqw&gsessionid=c13bV4YMNBIwZRp1r-ZjBqhNkR0APBSl2xKoBXChqyCumIsX2Xp6tQ)]. The Catholic Encyclopedia considers Odoric's narrative largely veracious, suggesting that while he included fantastic tales, his core observations were trustworthy [[80](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia_(1913)/Odoric_of_Pordenone)].

Ibn Battuta, the great Moroccan traveler who journeyed through Asia and Africa in the mid-14th century, offers the most potent corroboration for certain aspects of Polo's economic descriptions. Writing in the 1340s, nearly fifty years after Polo's supposed return, Ibn Battuta provides a powerful confirmation of the Yuan Dynasty's reliance on paper currency. In his account, he describes going to the bazaar with a silver dirham and finding that no one would accept it; instead, people used pieces of paper stamped with the sultan's seal [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. This anecdote directly validates Polo's lengthy and detailed explanation of the Chinese monetary system, which he described in terms of "big money" (gold) and "small money" (salt cakes or cowries) [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. This is a critical point of agreement that speaks directly to the technical accuracy of Polo's information. However, Ibn Battuta's account is not without its own contradictions and omissions. He famously fails to mention tea or chopsticks, leading some to speculate he never actually visited China, though this is disputed [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE), [6](https://networkedmediterranean.weebly.com/marco-polo-and-ibn-battuta-in-china.html)]. Furthermore, he held a generally negative view of China, criticizing its food and population, which contrasts sharply with Polo's admiration [[6](https://networkedmediterranean.weebly.com/marco-polo-and-ibn-battuta-in-china.html)]. His descriptions of specific ports like Hormuz, Zaytun, and the Maldives, however, can be compared directly with Polo's accounts to check for consistency in port descriptions [[5](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp), [75](https://insidequanzhou.com/quanzhou-in-world-accounts/)]. Samuel Lee's 1829 abridged translation of Ibn Battuta omits many of the detailed descriptions and notes found in the full Hakluyt Society edition translated by Gibb, meaning a comprehensive comparison requires consulting both [[4](https://orias.berkeley.edu/resources-teachers/travels-ibn-battuta/travels-ibn-battuta-selected-bibliography-links), [74](https://ils.unc.edu/dpr/path/ibnbatuta/travelse1.html)].

Finally, the user's interest in Hayton of Korykos provides an opportunity to investigate textual dependence. Hayton, a king of Cilician Armenia, wrote his *Fleur des histoires d'Orient* around 1307, a period when Polo's book was already gaining popularity in Europe [[35](https://www.attalus.org/armenian/hetumint.htm)]. Bedrosian's modern English translation, based on an Armenian edition, explicitly carries a public-domain statement [[35](https://www.attalus.org/armenian/hetumint.htm)]. Research suggests that Hayton may have used Polo's work as a source, alongside written histories and his own experiences [[35](https://www.attalus.org/armenian/hetumint.htm)]. Identifying specific passages where Hayton's account of the Mongols, their tactics, or their rulers mirrors Polo's would be strong evidence of textual borrowing. Conversely, if Hayton provides unique information not found in Polo, it could suggest an independent source. The existence of flawed older editions of Hayton's work means that any analysis should rely on the most recent critical editions, such as C. Kohler's 1906 French/Latin text, to ensure accuracy [[35](https://www.attalus.org/armenian/hetumint.htm)].

## Economic Practices and Monetary Systems in Asia

One of the most enduring criticisms of Marco Polo's account has been the perceived lack of specific, verifiable detail on the daily economic life of Yuan China, particularly in comparison to the meticulous records of later travelers or the official histories [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE)]. Critics have pointed to his omission of staples like tea and chopsticks as evidence of his distance from the culture he purported to describe [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE), [6](https://networkedmediterranean.weebly.com/marco-polo-and-ibn-battuta-in-china.html)]. However, a closer look at his detailed descriptions of currency, trade routes, and market practices, when cross-referenced with contemporary and near-contemporary sources, reveals a surprising level of accuracy. The accounts of later travelers like Ibn Battuta and the administrative records preserved in Chinese and Arab texts provide powerful corroboration for Polo's claims about the sophisticated and state-controlled economy of the Mongol Empire. This section will focus on the monetary systems Polo described in China and Yunnan, the role of paper currency, and the trade in luxury goods, demonstrating how these specific economic details hold up under scrutiny from multiple independent witnesses.

Polo's most detailed economic descriptions concern the monetary systems of different regions, especially Sichuan (referred to as Gaindu) and Yunnan (Toloman) [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. He distinguishes between "big money" and "small money." In Sichuan, he describes "big money" as gold weighed in saggi, while "small money" consists of salt cakes molded into shapes weighing about half a pound, stamped with the lord's seal [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. He gives a specific exchange rate: 80 of these salt coins are worth one saggio of fine gold. He explains that these salt coins are used by merchants to trade for gold among mountain people who cannot otherwise sell their precious metal [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. In Yunnan, he describes a similar dual system where "large" money is gold, and "small" sums are paid in Indian cowries, with a rate of 80 cowries to one saggio of silver [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. In Burma (Cardanan), he notes that money is primarily gold, supplemented by cowries, and he gives a favorable gold-to-silver exchange rate, attributing it to merchants bringing silver from afar to buy abundant local gold [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. While some of these details are difficult to verify precisely today, they align with the known economic policies of the Yuan Dynasty, which enforced strict controls on the export of precious metals and relied heavily on paper currency for domestic transactions [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. The existence of salt currency in some parts of Asia, noted by other travelers like Alvarez in Abyssinia, provides a plausible analogue for the salt cakes described by Polo [[1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/12410/12410-h/12410-h.htm)].

The strongest corroboration for Polo's economic claims comes from the later 14th-century traveler Ibn Battuta. As noted previously, Battuta provides a firsthand account of the universal acceptance of Yuan paper money. He incredulously describes how anyone trying to pay with silver or gold is refused, and must instead use the government-issued paper notes [[77](https://dokumen.pub/money-markets-and-trade-in-early-southeast-asia-the-development-of-indigenous-monetary-systems-to-ad-1400-9781501719479.html)]. This powerful anecdote directly validates Polo's central thesis about the functioning of the Yuan monetary system. The Italian merchant Francesco Balducci Pegolotti, writing around 1340, also confirms this practice in his *Pratica della mercatura*, stating that merchants must exchange their silver for paper money to conduct business in China [[120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans_in_China_during_the_Middle_Ages)]. Even the official Chinese records, despite their hasty compilation, contain provisions related to currency. The biography of the Byzantine physician Joshua ben Aaron in the *Yuanshi* notes his appointment to oversee the imperial board of medicine and astronomy, indicating a high degree of bureaucratic specialization that would be necessary to manage a complex economy [[120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans_in_China_during_the_Middle_Ages)]. Modern scholarship, particularly the work of Hans Ulrich Vogel, has provided overwhelming evidence for the accuracy of Polo's economic descriptions. Vogel's detailed analysis of Yuan-era documents and archaeological evidence on salt production and currency circulation demonstrates that Polo's knowledge was too specific and detailed to be fabricated, lending strong support to the "overwhelming probability of the broad authenticity" of his account [[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo), [156](https://www.academia.edu/4258345/Marco_Polo_Was_in_China_New_Evidence_from_Currencies_Salts_and_Revenues)].

Beyond currency, Polo's descriptions of trade and commerce are filled with specific details that find echoes in other sources. His praise for the silk industry of Baghdad and Mosul is echoed in the writings of other travelers [[63](https://al-islam.org/printpdf/book/export/html/39575)]. His description of Chinese ships, while laconic, can be compared with the detailed accounts of later Arab travelers like Ibn Battuta, who also marveled at the size and sophistication of Chinese junks [[75](https://insidequanzhou.com/quanzhou-in-world-accounts/)]. The trade in pepper, which Polo mentions, is corroborated by the *Dade Nanhai Zhi*, a regional gazetteer from the Dade reign-period (1297-1307), which lists pepper as a major import commodity arriving at Guangzhou [[2](https://www.academia.edu/42807963/Marco_Polo_from_Hangzhou_to_Quanzhou)]. This confirms that the spice trade Polo witnessed was a significant economic reality. The account of the missionary Andrew of Perugia, who served as a bishop in Zaiton (Quanzhou) around 1326, provides a direct contemporary witness to the bustling port city that Polo described [[168](https://ricerca.unityfvg.it/entities/publication/9de26432-da9e-4869-875e-0ae37dac511a/details)]. His tombstone, discovered in 1946, attests to the presence of a vibrant Christian community in the very city and region that Polo praised [[168](https://ricerca.unityfvg.it/entities/publication/9de26432-da9e-4869-875e-0ae37dac511a/details)]. While Andrew's letters are scattered and not fully printed, they offer a tangible link to the world Polo depicted [[157](https://fareastlatin.org/research-notes-2/a-timeline-of-medieval-texts/)].

Even the so-called "omissions" can be put into context. The absence of tea in Polo's account is notable, but it was not unknown in Europe entirely; it was just not yet ubiquitous as it became after 1517 [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE)]. His failure to describe the Chinese writing system is more difficult to explain, but it may reflect his focus on practical and economic matters rather than a lack of observation. The formulaic nature of his city descriptions, starting with "idolaters" who use paper money, has been criticized as generic, but this template may have been a practical way to convey a standardized set of facts about urban centers to a European audience [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE)]. In conclusion, while Polo's narrative style may lack the ethnographic depth of some later travelers, his specific claims about the Yuan economy are remarkably resilient. They are consistently supported by the testimony of Ibn Battuta, the administrative realities hinted at in the *Yuanshi*, the practical advice of Pegolotti, and the archaeological and epigraphic evidence from missionary sites. This body of corroborating evidence elevates his work from a mere travelogue to a credible historical document detailing the economic infrastructure of the Mongol Empire.

## Missionary Correspondence and Indian Ocean Port Descriptions

The vast maritime networks of the Indian Ocean and South China Sea, connecting China to the Middle East and Africa, are less covered by the grand official histories of empires and more illuminated by the fragmented but vivid accounts of sailors, merchants, and missionaries. For verifying Polo's claims about these regions, sources like the letters of John of Montecorvino and Andrew of Perugia, along with the descriptive gazetteers of Chinese and Arab authors, are indispensable. These texts provide a ground-level view of port cities, trade goods, and cultural interactions that complements Polo's overland-centric narrative. The user's focus on these materials is crucial for building a holistic picture of the medieval Asian world. The analysis reveals that while some sources are limited by their fragmentary nature, they collectively provide significant corroboration for Polo's descriptions of places like Quanzhou/Zaytun and the economic vitality of the southern Chinese coast.

The missionary letters offer a unique window into the Christian communities thriving in Yuan China during the late 13th and early 14th centuries. John of Montecorvino, who arrived in Beijing in 1294, sent back reports that were published in Yule's *Cathay and the Way Thither* [[107](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08474a.htm)]. In his letters, he describes the establishment of churches and the education of converts, providing a contemporary snapshot of the religious landscape in the Yuan capital [[109](https://mrijournal.riccimac.org/index.php/en/issues/issue-13/332-mrij13-2-en)]. His correspondence, along with that of his successor Andrew of Perugia, confirms the presence of a significant Christian population in key commercial hubs. Andrew's letter from Zayton (modern Quanzhou), a major port for overseas trade, is particularly relevant as it describes the city during the period when Polo was there [[168](https://ricerca.unityfvg.it/entities/publication/9de26432-da9e-4869-875e-0ae37dac511a/details)]. Quanzhou was a melting pot of cultures, home to Chinese, Arab, Persian, and European merchants [[75](https://insidequanzhou.com/quanzhou-in-world-accounts/)]. Polo's glowing description of the city as a great emporium crowded with large junks is thus powerfully corroborated by the very existence of a bishopric there, established by the Franciscans [[168](https://ricerca.unityfvg.it/entities/publication/9de26432-da9e-4869-875e-0ae37dac511a/details)]. The presence of a Sogdian-founded church in Zhenjiang, another city mentioned by Polo, further attests to the widespread reach of Christianity in coastal China [[120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans_in_China_during_the_Middle_Ages)]. These letters, while primarily theological in intent, serve as invaluable geographical and demographic evidence for the places and communities Polo described.

On the Indian Ocean side, the accounts of Arab and Chinese authors provide a complementary perspective. Zhao Rugua's *Zhu Fan Zhi*, compiled around 1225 CE, is one of the earliest Chinese sources to provide systematic information on foreign countries and their traded products, based on data gathered from foreign merchants in Quanzhou [[134](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Fan_Zhi)]. Although slightly earlier than Polo's visit, its descriptions of Java, the Malabar coast of India, and the Chola kingdom offer a baseline of trade relationships that were undoubtedly still active in the 1270s and 1280s [[78](https://www.academia.edu/18443437/A_History_of_Early_Southeast_Asia)]. The 1911 English translation by Friedrich Hirth and W. W. Rockhill, while a classic PD work, is a partial rendering of a lost original, so its completeness must be treated with caution [[134](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Fan_Zhi)]. For the island of Java, Zhao Rugua's text is a key reference point [[78](https://www.academia.edu/18443437/A_History_of_Early_Southeast_Asia)]. Another valuable source is the anonymous *Accounts of China and India* (*Akhbār al-Ṣīn wa-l-Hind*), compiled by the Arab merchant Abū Zayd al-Sīrāfī, which draws on the eyewitness accounts of Sulaymān al-Tājir [[148](https://dokumen.pub/accounts-of-china-and-india-9781479862054.html)]. This work, translated anew by Tim Mackintosh-Smith in 2017, provides rich ethnographic detail on Tang dynasty Canton but is also noted for being a major source used by later authors, possibly even Ibn Battuta, making it a key text for tracing the transmission of geographic knowledge [[148](https://dokumen.pub/accounts-of-china-and-india-9781479862054.html)].

For the region of Southeast Asia, the work of Zhou Daguan, a Chinese emissary who spent a year in Cambodia (then known as Chenla) from 1296-1297, is invaluable [[159](https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/cultures/am04/documents/179)]. His *Notes on the Customs of Cambodia* provides a detailed ethnographic portrait of Khmer society, including its agriculture, social classes, and trade. For comparing with Polo, the most relevant aspect is his mention of Chinese residents and their role in Cambodian commerce [[158](https://angkordatabase.asia/books/the-customs-of-cambodia-1st-english-translation)]. Multiple English translations are available, including J.G.D. ## Scholarly Appraisals and Textual Criticism of Polo's Narrative

An objective assessment of Marco Polo's account requires engaging not only with primary sources but also with the rich and contentious field of modern scholarship dedicated to his work. The user's final priority—to compare the arguments of Igor de Rachewiltz with those of Frances Wood—targets the very heart of the authenticity debate. This scholarly discourse moves beyond simple fact-checking to ask fundamental questions about the nature of Polo's narrative: Was it a travelogue, a business report, a work of fiction, or a combination of genres? By analyzing the methodologies and conclusions of key scholars, we can better understand the evidentiary basis for accepting or rejecting Polo's claims. The evidence suggests that while significant doubts persist, a growing body of modern scholarship, armed with new textual and archaeological evidence, leans towards a more positive assessment of Polo's presence in China.

The foundational work in this area is undeniably that of Paul Pelliot, whose multi-volume *Notes on Marco Polo* (published posthumously between 1959 and 1973) remains a critical reference for identifying places and correcting errors [[2](https://www.academia.edu/42807963/Marco_Polo_from_Hangzhou_to_Quanzhou), [14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo)]. Pelliot's meticulous philological work established a new standard for Polo studies, moving the conversation away from simple belief or disbelief towards a nuanced analysis of specific claims. His work provides the roadmap for linking Polo's often cryptic place names to real locations, thereby grounding his narrative in a verifiable geography [[2](https://www.academia.edu/42807963/Marco_Polo_from_Hangzhou_to_Quanzhou)]. Building on this foundation, scholars like Thomas Allsen and Morris Rossabi have placed Polo's account within the broader context of Mongol imperial administration and cultural exchange, highlighting the coherence of his descriptions of Mongol institutions [[91](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lic3.12152)].

In this scholarly landscape, the positions of Igor de Rachewiltz and Frances Wood represent opposing poles. Frances Wood, in her book *Did Marco Polo Go to China?*, argues persuasively that the conspicuous absence of Marco Polo from Chinese records, combined with his omissions of key cultural markers like tea and writing, strongly suggests he never traveled beyond the Black Sea region [[3](https://www.scribd.com/document/696335253/1978-John-W-Haeger-MARCO-POLO-IN-CHINA-PROBLEMS-WITH-INTERNAL-EVIDENCE), [23](https://www.facebook.com/KingsGenerals/posts/why-chinese-sources-dont-mention-marco-polochinese-sources-largely-omit-marco-po/1004078091760266/)]. Her critique is part of a long tradition of skepticism that views the book as a work of literary artifice, perhaps compiled from other travelers' tales by Rustichello da Pisa [[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo), [88](http://medievalhistory.info/the-assassins-and-the-old-man-of-the-mountain/)]. In contrast, Igor de Rachewiltz, in his introduction to his monumental translation of *The Secret History of the Mongols*, presents a powerful defense of Polo's authenticity [[136](https://www.deremilitari.org/REVIEWS/Rachewiltz_Mongols.htm)]. De Rachewiltz argues that only someone with firsthand experience of the Yuan court could have produced such a detailed and accurate account of Mongol administrative and military structures [[135](https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/Rachewiltz-The-Secret-History-of-the-Mongols.pdf)]. He marshals evidence from the *Secret History* and other Mongolian sources to show that Polo's understanding of Mongol society was fundamentally correct, countering the arguments of critics [[136](https://www.deremilitari.org/REVIEWS/Rachewiltz_Mongols.htm)]. The user's instruction to distinguish between de Rachewiltz's 1997 article and his 2002 appraisal of Wood is astute, as his arguments evolved and were refined over time. His core evidence rests on the internal consistency and technical accuracy of Polo's descriptions, which he believes an armchair writer could not replicate.

Perhaps the most compelling modern evidence for Polo's authenticity comes from the work of economic historian Hans Ulrich Vogel. In his book *Marco Polo Was in China*, Vogel conducts a detailed analysis of the Yuan Dynasty's economic systems, focusing on currencies, salt production, and tax revenues [[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo), [156](https://www.academia.edu/4258345/Marco_Polo_Was_in_China_New_Evidence_from_Currencies_Salts_and_Revenues)]. He demonstrates that Polo's descriptions of these topics are not only accurate but also contain unique details that match perfectly with evidence from Yuan-era administrative documents and archaeological findings [[155](https://www.jstor.org/stable/44511252)]. Vogel concludes that the "overwhelming probability of the broad authenticity" of Polo's account is supported by this economic evidence alone [[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo)]. This approach shifts the burden of proof onto the skeptics, who must then explain why a fictional account would contain such a wealth of specific, verifiable economic data. Vogel's work, along with the continued analysis of missionary letters and the refinement of translations like de Rachewiltz's, has significantly strengthened the case for Polo's presence in China.

Ultimately, the scholarly debate reflects the complexities of interpreting a text written over seven centuries ago. The existence of hundreds of manuscripts with varying details shows that the text was fluid and subject to change after its initial composition [[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo)]. The Dominican Order's early endorsement of the work as truthful suggests it was valued as a reliable guide for evangelization in the East [[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo)]. The debate is not merely academic; it has direct implications for a commercial retelling of the story. The most responsible approach is to present the full spectrum of evidence: the points of contradiction with Persian and Chinese sources (like the Xiangyang engineers), the points of strong corroboration from later travelers and modern scholarship (like the paper money system), and the points of ambiguity (like the Kököchin mission). By acknowledging the limitations of the source material and the ongoing nature of the scholarly debate, the final publication can achieve both commercial appeal and intellectual honesty. It can tell the story of a remarkable journey while transparently accounting for the questions and controversies that have surrounded it for generations.
