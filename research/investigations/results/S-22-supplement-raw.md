# Learning Mongol-era accuracy without copying anyone's art

No single modern work of fiction gives this illustrator a ready-made bibliography. The two closest comparators — *Tenmaku no Jaadugar* and *Otoyomegatari* — are both set outside the 1260–1295 window (the former ~30–60 years before it, the latter ~600 years after it), and in both cases the **creators' own research sources are either undocumented or only thinly documented in public**: Tomato Soup has never published a bibliography, and Kaoru Mori's interviews describe "atmosphere" and favorite rug patterns rather than named museums or texts. Across the wider survey of 37 additional works (manga, film, games, Osprey, documentaries, illustrated books), the pattern repeats: the *best-documented* research trails belong to Netflix's **Marco Polo** (costume designer Jo Korer's Mongolia trips, an unnamed historian consultant) and **Ghost of Tsushima** (whose accuracy was audited, critically, by an independent swordsmanship historian) — but even these name no museum accession numbers. The reliable, checkable sources are almost all primary: the **Rashid al-Din *Jami' al-Tawarikh*** manuscripts (Edinburgh Or. MS 20; Khalili Collection), the **"Portrait of Chabi"** (National Palace Museum, Taipei), **Liu Guandao's "Khubilai Khan Hunting"** (NPM, Taipei), the excavated **Quanzhou ship**, the Met's lamellar helmet (1999.158), and William of Rubruck's eyewitness account of the ger-cart. Several of the illustrator's hardest visual problems — Yuan Dadu/Shangdu interiors, an Ilkhanid Tabriz bazaar scene, Kashgar/Khotan street life, 13th-century Venetian galleys — have **no confirmed period visual source at all**, and this report says so plainly rather than inventing one.

## Part 1 — Two works examined in depth

### (a) Tenmaku no Jaadugar / A Witch's Life in Mongol (Tomato Soup)

**What it is.** *Tenmaku no Jaadugar* (天幕のジャードゥーガル), serialized by Akita Shoten from September 2021, is a manga by Tomato Soup licensed in English by Yen Press as *A Witch's Life in Mongol*. An anime adaptation, *Jaadugar: A Witch in Mongolia*, was produced by Science SARU (chief director Naoko Yamada, director Abel Góngora) and premiered July 4, 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Witch%27s_Life_in_Mongol)).

**Period depicted, and the gap to Marco Polo.** The story opens in **1213** with the Mongol destruction of Tus (a city in Khwarazmian Persia, present-day northeastern Iran) and runs through at least **1232** (Ögedei's poisoning illness and the death of Tolui), with the historical model for the protagonist, Fatima Khatun, having died in 1246 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Witch%27s_Life_in_Mongol); [Wikipedia: Fatima (d. 1246)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatima_(d._1246))). That places the entire narrative **30 to possibly 50+ years before Marco Polo's 1271–1295 travels**, and entirely before the Yuan dynasty was formally established (1271) and before the Ilkhanate existed as a state (1256). This is the Chinggis-to-early-Ögedei conquest and consolidation era, not the mature, internationalized Pax Mongolica court culture Polo describes.

**Settings and peoples.** The manga moves between the Khwarazmian Persian city of Tus — its streets, a large mosque, a busy bazaar, a wealthy household — and the Mongol court/steppe, including a kurultai (the supreme decision-making council), a two-fires purification ritual, Mongol wrestling, music, and dance ([Nlab/itmedia](https://nlab.itmedia.co.jp/fav/articles/3953557/amp/); [anime.eiga.com/Hikawa column](https://anime.eiga.com/news/column/hikawa_rekishi/126889/)). For the anime, production traveled to Mongolia for location scouting and museum visits, did a homestay in a traditional dwelling, tried horseback riding, visited a mosque in Tokyo (the Tokyo Camii Diyanet Turkish Culture Center) and interviewed worshippers there, and drew on traditional Iranian/Persian art for visual reference ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/anime/naoko-yamada-abel-gongora-jaadugar-science-saru-263884.html); [Otakukart](https://otakukart.com/naoko-yamada-says-she-stayed-in-a-traditional-mongolian-home-while-preparing-jaadugar-a-witch-in-mongolia-anime/); [medievalists.net](https://www.medievalists.net/2026/08/new-anime-set-in-the-medieval-mongol-empire-draws-praise/)).

**Stated sources — the honest answer is "none traceable."** No bibliography, citation list, museum accession number, or named academic consultant attributable to Tomato Soup herself was found anywhere in the sources searched (Comic Natalie, da Vinci, Wikipedia, Real Sound, Nlab). The only trace of a research process is a secondhand producer claim: Kazuki Endo stated Tomato Soup "supplied a detailed list of historical references and clarified the accuracy and intended meaning of some scenes" for the anime staff — but that list was never published ([medievalists.net](https://www.medievalists.net/2026/08/new-anime-set-in-the-medieval-mongol-empire-draws-praise/)). Production-side interviews mention an unnamed "cultural consultant," unnamed "university professors," and unnamed "specialists on Mongolian and Persian history," but no source names a single scholar, book, or museum ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/anime/naoko-yamada-abel-gongora-jaadugar-science-saru-263884.html); [Anitrendz](https://anitrendz.net/news/2026/08/29/naoko-yamada-jaadugar-a-witch-in-mongolia-interview/)). No source connects this work to Timothy May, Thomas Allsen, Morris Rossabi, named Mongolian costume-studies scholarship, or the *Secret History of the Mongols*.

**What is and isn't transferable to a 1260–1295 retelling.**
- **Transferable** (long material-culture continuity, plausibly stable across the ~30–50 year gap): ger construction and steppe camp layout, the ger-as-sundial detail (light through the smoke-hole marking time), horse tack, bow-and-cavalry equipment, hunting with raptors, open-steppe feast/kurultai staging, and the broad silhouette of Perso-Islamic urban daily life (bazaar, mosque, household) that predates and partially survives the Mongol conquest.
- **Not transferable:** anything specific to Kublai's sinicized Yuan court dress and etiquette, Dadu/Shangdu architecture, Yuan-era nasij cloth-of-gold as it developed under Kublai, or mature Ilkhanid Persian painting conventions (which only emerge as a tradition after Hülegü's 1256 conquest and flourish from the 1280s, e.g., Rashid al-Din's *Jami al-Tawarikh*) — the manga's Persian setting is pre-Ilkhanid Khwarazmian civilization, not the Mongol-Persian hybrid court culture of Polo's era.

**Critical reception is praise without technical audit.** Japanese and Western press reviews (Real Sound, Nlab/itmedia, Hikawa Ryusuke, Mother's Basement, Anime News Network) uniformly call the production "historically careful" or "extremely precise," but none identifies a specific costume, weapon, or architectural detail as correct or incorrect — this is impressionistic praise, not a specialist's accuracy audit, and the illustrator should not treat it as verification.

**Bottom line for the illustrator:** treat this work as a visual mood-board for late-Chinggisid/early-Ögedeid steppe material culture and pre-Ilkhanid Perso-Islamic urban life, but go around it — not through it — for any primary source trail, since none of its own sources are documented.

### (b) Otoyomegatari / A Bride's Story (Kaoru Mori)

**What it is and depicts.** Serialized by Kaoru Mori since 2008 (Enterbrain/Harta, then Kadokawa's Aokishi from 2021), *Otoyomegatari* is set explicitly in the **late 19th century**, in a rural town near the Caspian Sea on the Silk Road, during the **Russian conquest of Central Asia**, with the British "Great Game" response represented through an English character ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Bride%27s_Story)). It depicts, in dense detail: nomadic-versus-settled Turkic/Persian daily life, textiles and embroidery, horse culture, archery, eagle-hunting/falconry, bread and food preparation, wedding and dowry customs, patrilocal and levirate marriage, and Persian urban architecture including public baths ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Bride%27s_Story)).

**This is the single most valuable analysis in the research base: which elements are stable across the ~600-year gap, and which are not.**

| Element | Status for a 1260–1295 setting | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Eagle hunting / falconry | **Stable — safe to use directly.** Attested in Central Asia from the 1st–2nd millennium BC; explicitly documented as continuous *through* the Mongol conquest period (Kyrgyz falconry preserved under Mongol rule after their 1207 surrender to Jochi) | [Wikipedia: Hunting with eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunting_with_eagles) |
| Yurts/gers (general structure) | **Stable as a type.** Marco Polo's own writings attest to yurts at Kublai's court, directly inside the target window; nomadic herding with yurt-type dwellings is described as millennia-old. Construction *details* (lattice design, felt technique, roof-wheel) are **not independently confirmed as unchanged** — treat the general silhouette as safe, the fine joinery as unverified | [Wikipedia: Yurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yurt) |
| General nomadic-steppe lifeways (herding, horse racing, archery) | Plausible continuity, but no source quantifies tack/equipment detail across the span — use for atmosphere, not for hardware specifics | manga + general ethnographic inference only |
| **Ikat textiles** | **Anachronistic — do not use as a 13th-century reference.** Earliest confirmed Central Asian ikat documentation is 18th century, flourishing in the 19th (the manga's own period); the only earlier trace is a disputed, unpublished 4th–9th century fragment | [AleSouk](https://alesouk.com/?p=17949) |
| **Suzani embroidery** | **Anachronistic.** Earliest documented precursor is the early 15th century (Ruy González de Clavijo's description at Timur's court, already a century-plus after Polo); virtually all surviving examples date to the 18th–19th centuries | [Wikipedia: Suzani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzani_rug) |
| Russian imperial presence, Great Game politics, Crimean War veteran, camera, implied firearms | **Hard anachronisms by definition of the manga's own 1870s–1890s setting** — exclude wholesale | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Bride%27s_Story) |

The practical implication: the manga is safe to study for **silhouette, gesture, animal husbandry, hunting practice, and architectural atmosphere** of Turkic/Persian Central Asia, but its signature decorative textiles (ikat, suzani) are a documented later fashion and should not be copied into a 13th-century wardrobe without an explicit anachronism flag; an illustrator wanting embroidered Central Asian dowry textiles for a Polo-era book needs an earlier, pre-Timurid textile tradition instead — a gap this research could not fill.

**Kaoru Mori's documented research sources are thin.** Mori has given few interviews. The fullest available account (a 2025 Overload NZ convention interview, known only via a secondhand blog summary) has her saying she "tries to be accurate with Central Asian cultural designs" but "can't keep it 100% accurate since it is on paper," that she focuses on "atmosphere and feeling," bases faces on photographs, chose the setting partly because she likes rug and carpet patterns, has tried on Central Asian clothing herself, and does not work digitally, using her computer "only for research" (unspecified) ([Overload NZ summary](https://drake.bearblog.dev/log/kaoru-mori-aki-irie-overload-interview/)). A 2022 Setagaya Literary Museum exhibition write-up states she "develops her ideas through detailed research rather than personal travel," preferring "the established, unchanging past" ([Yattatachi](https://yattatachi.com/kaoru-mori-aki-irie-exhibit); [ANN press release](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/press-release/2022-02-09/kaoru-mori-popular-manga-otoyomegatari-gets-an-original-art-exhibition/.182430)). **No interview names a specific book, museum collection, or scholar** she consulted for this series; no dedicated art-book bibliography could be confirmed to exist; and no Yen Press volume afterword/translator's note content could be accessed in this research pass (flagged as the single highest-value follow-up — someone with the physical English volumes should check the back matter directly). A Japanese-language interview (livedoor.com, "漫画『乙嫁語り』森薫×大場渉") was identified but not read in this research pass and remains an unexplored lead ([livedoor.com](https://news.livedoor.com/article/detail/20463829/)).

**Bottom line for the illustrator:** Otoyomegatari is best used as a reference for nomadic/sedentary lifeway contrast, hunting practice, and architectural mood — not for its specific textile patterning, and not by assuming Mori followed any citable research trail, because none is documented.
