# Separating Polo's text from his editors' reconstructions

**Bottom line.** On all five questions, the evidence gathered supports narrower statements than the manuscript and registry appear to use. **(1)** The Chinese notice from the Yongle dadian records a **dispatch order (3rd month of Zhiyuan 27, c. spring 1290) and a provisioning decision (8th month, 17th day, computed c. 21 September 1290, Julian)**. It records no sailing and names neither the Polos nor the princess. Whether Arghun (d. 7 Rabiʿ I 690 AH = 10 March 1291, Julian) died before or after the fleet sailed depends on which reconstructed departure date is adopted. **(2)** Polo's Prologue names an island "Java" and gives no stay there. The five-month detention belongs to the Samara chapter, which is **Book III ch. X in Yule-Cordier, not ch. IX**. The link between the two passages is an editorial reconstruction going back to Marsden (1818) and Yule, and Yule explicitly counted the five months **inside** the 18. **(3)** No evidence was found that the Xi'an stele was visible in Polo's day or that any scholar argues from its absence, so calling the omission a "silence" is not defensible. **(4)** The Prologue explicitly names **Trebizond, Constantinople, Negropont and Venice (1295)**. Zayton as the departure port and Hormuz as the landfall are Yule's reconstructions ("appear", "probably"). **(5)** Jacopo d'Acqui places Marco's capture at "Layaç" (Ayas) with the date **MCCLXXXXVI (1296)**. Emending that to the Ayas battle of 1294 is an editorial move by Yule and Bolognari. The Curzola story is first found in Ramusio's preface of 1559. Every item in this report traces back to a text read through OCR or machine extraction, not to page images, so quotations and page numbers need one more check against scans before print.

## Methods note: what "inspected" means in this report

This report synthesises five research notes. No new sources were consulted for it. Every access label from the notes is kept as given. In those notes, **"inspected passage" almost always means that the text was read through a summarising or extracting fetch tool, or through OCR full text (archive.org djvu text, Gutenberg HTML or plain-text mirrors, NII Digital Silk Road OCR, PDF extraction). It does not mean the wording was checked by eye against a page image.**

Several consequences follow. Italics and sigla in Moule-Pelliot are lost in the OCR. Printed page numbers for Yule-Cordier are frequently unconfirmed: the DSR scan numbers are image numbers, not printed pages. Numerals in OCR (ship counts, survivor counts) may be corrupt. The notes also used four other labels, which are kept here as written:

- **REPORTED / VIA SCHOLAR**: known only through an intermediary, who is named.
- **SNIPPET/METADATA**: a search snippet or catalogue record only.
- **COMPUTED**: the note-taker's own calendar arithmetic, not checked against a published concordance.
- **NOT ACCESSED**: not reached at all.

The researchers could not reach Gutenberg's www host or download from archive.org, so different notes read Yule-Cordier through different routes: a Brown University plain-text mirror of Gutenberg #10636, Gutenberg HTML truncated before the Prologue, DSR scans of the Toyo Bunko copy, archive.org OCR of the 1871 first edition, and Wikisource. Where the 1871 edition was read rather than 1903, this report says so.

## 2–3. Detailed findings, with the requested tables

### Q1. The Chinese notice records preparations in 1290, not a sailing

#### What the Chinese text says

The notice comes from the Yuan postal-relay compendium (*Jingshi dadian*, "Zhanchi" section) as preserved in the Yongle dadian. The text available to the researchers is a **transcription posted by a Douban reviewer (2018)**. It was **not checked against a facsimile** of the Yongle dadian, and the commonly cited juan 19418 is itself unverified ([Douban review](https://book.douban.com/review/9294868/); REPORTED).

On that transcription, the entry dated the **17th day** is a memorial by the Shangshu official 阿難答 and the dushi 別不花 and others. It relays a request from the pingzhang Sha Buding (沙不丁) and says the following:

- "This year, in the 3rd month", an order was received to send **兀魯[𢈪] (Uladai), 阿必失呵 (Abishqa) and 火者 (Qoja)** "取道馬八兒" ("by way of Maʿbar") to the court of Prince Arghun.
- Of the 160 travellers, 90 already drew allowances. The memorial asks that the other 70, said to be gifts to or purchases by officials, receive no rations, and the emperor agrees.

The year and month (Zhiyuan 27, 8th month) come from the surrounding entries; only "十七日" belongs to this entry. Li Zhi'an's 2019 newspaper summary supports the content in outline ([Guangming ribao, 20 Apr 2019](https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/2019-04/20/nw.D110000gmrb_20190420_1-10.htm); inspected via WebFetch summary).

Several points need checking before anything is quoted:

- The researcher remembered "諸王" (princes) where the transcription has "諸官" (officials). The note itself treats this recollection as unverified, so it must be checked.
- The envoy forms in the transcription (兀鲁□, 阿必失呵, 火者) differ from the forms reported for Yang's matching (兀剌台, 阿卜思哈, 火者) ([Douban review](https://book.douban.com/review/9294868/)). Which forms the Yongle dadian actually prints must be checked before any Chinese name is printed.

**Event types in the notice.** The notice contains a retrospectively cited **dispatch order** (3rd month) and a **provisioning decision** (8th month, 17th day). **No departure is recorded.** It does not name Kököchin: Wikipedia, citing Cleaves 1976, says it "does not mention the princess" ([Wikipedia, Kököchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6k%C3%B6chin); REPORTED). The transcription contains no name resembling Polo. It does not say "sea"; the Maʿbar route only implies a maritime passage. Whether the notice names Quanzhou is **unverified**. The identification of Sha Buding as the official in charge of maritime trade in the south-east is likewise unverified in the notes.

**Calendar conversions (COMPUTED, not checked against a concordance).** Zhiyuan 27, 8th month, 17th day ≈ **21 September 1290, Julian (±1 day)**, using new moons of 5 Sept and 5 Oct 1290 and the zhongqi rule. Zhiyuan 27, 3rd month began on either 13 March or 11 April 1290 (Julian) depending on the equinox, so treat it as **"March–May 1290"**. Avoid Gregorian proleptic dates for this period. Verify both conversions with the Academia Sinica 兩千年中西曆轉換 tool before print.

#### What Yang Zhijiu inferred, known only through intermediaries

Yang published 〈關於馬可波羅離華的一段漢文記載〉 in *Wenshi zazhi* 1.12 (1941). The researchers did **not** read it. Two intermediaries report it ([Douban review](https://book.douban.com/review/9294868/); [Li Zhi'an](https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/2019-04/20/nw.D110000gmrb_20190420_1-10.htm); REPORTED):

- He matched the three envoys with Polo's Oulatai, Apusca and Coja.
- He corrected the departure "to early 1291, not the early 1292 that Westerners give" (Li Zhi'an's paraphrase).
- His reasoning, as the Douban reviewer reports it, was that a 3rd-month order and an 8th-month provisioning memorial put the departure at the end of Zhiyuan 27 or the start of Zhiyuan 28.

This is an **inference from the provisioning date plus sailing season**, not a statement of the text.

#### What modern scholars argue

Li Zhi'an reports that **Cleaves** (*HJAS* 36, 1976, 181–203) presented and affirmed Yang's findings, and that **Pelliot** independently reached 1291 ([Li Zhi'an](https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/2019-04/20/nw.D110000gmrb_20190420_1-10.htm); REPORTED). Cleaves was seen only as a citation.

Pelliot's *Notes on Marco Polo* I, entry "Cocacin", pp. 408–410, was read (OCR via tool). There Pelliot says Polo's "very detailed account" of the embassy "is to be entirely trusted", and that the travellers "crossed the western Indian Ocean in the winter-monsoon of 1292-1293" ([Pelliot, *Notes* I p. 409](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/V-1/page/0409.html.en); [p. 410](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/V-1/page/0410.html.en)). The notes do not record Pelliot's own departure year from the *Notes* directly; that comes from Li Zhi'an.

**Wikipedia** says the party left Quanzhou "in the spring of 1291" and arrived "around 1293" (tertiary). That is a third formulation and should not be merged with Yang's "turn of the year".

**Haw (2006), Jackson (1998), Vogel (2013) and de Rachewiltz were not accessed.** Wikipedia cites de Rachewiltz only for the envoy identifications. **"Ma" could not be identified.** Nothing may be attributed to any of these scholars from the notes.

#### What Yule wrote, and what his date depends on

Yule's Introduction §21 says the party "appear to have sailed from the port of Zayton … in the beginning of 1292". A footnote reckons "Say that they sailed from Fokien in January 1292". The footnote is present in the 1871 edition at p. liii and in the 1903 edition at about pp. 23–24 ([archive.org, 1871 OCR](https://archive.org/stream/bookofsermarcopo01polo/bookofsermarcopo01polo_djvu.txt); [Gutenberg #10636](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10636/pg10636-images.html); inspected via OCR and extraction). Yule worked backwards from Hammer's date for Ghazan's reception of the bride, and he admits "I have been unable to trace Hammer's authority". **His 1292 thus rests on an untraced secondary date. Yang's 1291 rests on a primary document of preparation plus the monsoon.** Neither is a recorded sailing date.

One phrase needs re-attribution. The words "two years or upwards", which Wikipedia quotes as Polo's, appear in **Yule's own Introduction §21 main text** ([archive.org 1871](https://archive.org/stream/bookofsermarcopo01polo/bookofsermarcopo01polo_djvu.txt); inspected OCR). Whether Polo's text also has them was not checked. Do not attribute the phrase to Polo.

#### When Arghun died

Faruk Sümer's entry in the TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi gives "7 Rebîülevvel 690 / 10 Mart 1291" and death in Arran, citing Rashid al-Din (Jahn ed. 1957; Alizade ed. 1957) ([TDV, "Argun Han"](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/argun-2); inspected via WebFetch; Rashid not inspected). Yule-Cordier's Prologue note 6 also dates the death to 10 March 1291 ([DSR scan 0334](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-30/V-1/page/0334.html.en); OCR via tool). The tabular conversion (COMPUTED) gives 1 Muḥarram 690 = 4 Jan 1291 and 7 Rabīʿ I 690 = **Saturday 10 March 1291, Julian**. Observational month starts could move this by ±1–2 days, which would explain variants of 7 or 9 March.

| Departure hypothesis (whose) | Arghun at departure |
|---|---|
| Late 1290 / Jan–Feb 1291 (Yang; Cleaves and Pelliot as reported by Li Zhi'an) | **Alive**; he died while the fleet was at sea |
| "Spring 1291" (Wikipedia, tertiary) | **Either**: alive if the fleet sailed before about 10 March 1291. News from Arran could not have reached China for months in any case. |
| "Beginning of 1292" (Yule) | **Already dead** about ten months, though perhaps not known in China |

Yule's notes give two views of when the death became known. Note 6 says the envoys learned of it "probably on landing at Hormuz". A Wikipedia quotation traced to the Gutenberg text says Arghun "had been dead even before they quitted China"; this is probably a Yule note and is unverified. The two are compatible only on Yule's 1292 chronology.

#### What the Persian sources add

The notes reach Rashid al-Din only through intermediaries. Yule's note 6 reports Ghazan meeting the envoy at "Aher" (Abhar), "a little west of Kazvín", while he was returning from Tabriz, and cites Hammer for a marriage in late 1293 or early 1294 ([DSR scan 0334](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-30/V-1/page/0334.html.en)). Pelliot reports Hammer's "Abher". He rejects as "impossible" Hammer's claim that Ghazan had sent the embassy, and he notes Rashid's form Kökāči and the Bulughan kinship motive ([Pelliot p. 409](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/V-1/page/0409.html.en)). *Encyclopaedia Iranica* (Bernardini 2008) says the Cleaves material "appears to be confirmed by Persian sources" but Marco "is not mentioned" ([Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=1530); relied on secondary).

**No Chinese or Persian record seen names the Polos.** The notice implies a sea route only through "Maʿbar", and the Persian notices as reported do not describe the voyage.

**Recommended wording (Q1).**

> A Yuan document preserved in the Yongle dadian, dated the 17th day of the 8th month of Zhiyuan 27 (about 21 September 1290), records that three envoys of the Ilkhan Arghun, Uladai, Abishqa and Qoja, were being sent "by way of Maʿbar" under an order of the third month of that year, and it settles rations for their party of 160. Since Yang Zhijiu (1941), scholars have identified these envoys with Polo's Oulatai, Apusca and Coja. The document records preparations, not a sailing, and names neither the Polos nor the princess. Yang inferred a departure around the turn of 1290/91. Yule, writing before the document was known, had reckoned on early 1292. Arghun died on 7 Rabīʿ I 690 (10 March 1291); depending on which reckoning is followed, the fleet sailed shortly before or some months after his death.

### Q2. The five months belong to the Samara chapter, and the Prologue says nothing of them

#### What the witnesses say

The Prologue chapter is Yule's "How the Two Brothers and Messer Marco took Leave of the Great Kaan…", Prologue ch. XVIII. In all three texts inspected, it gives about three months to an island "towards the South, which is called Java", then **18 months more in the Sea of India**, with **no stay on the island and no named port of departure or arrival**:

- Yule 1871, pp. 32–33 ([archive.org 1871](https://archive.org/stream/bookofsermarcopo01polo/bookofsermarcopo01polo_djvu.txt)), and Yule-Cordier 1903 ([DSR scans 0330–0331](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-30/V-1/page/0330.html.en)). A 1903 cross-reference places the "18 months" on vol. i p. 35.
- F via Moule-Pelliot §18, pp. ~90–91. The OCR is garbled at the unit word after "eighteen" ([archive.org MP](https://archive.org/stream/descriptionofwor01polo/descriptionofwor01polo_djvu.txt)).
- R via Marsden 1818, p. 27: "about three months … Java … eighteen months in the Indian seas" ([archive.org Marsden](https://archive.org/stream/marcopolo00polouoft/marcopolo00polouoft_djvu.txt)).

Z, TA, VA, P, FG and L were not inspected for this chapter, so do not write "all versions".

**Conflict: ship and survivor counts.** Two independent OCR extractions of Yule's English, the 1871 archive.org text and the 1903 DSR scan, both returned **"thirteen ships"** and **"only eight survived"**. Yule's own note 4, however, speaks of "a fleet of fourteen junks", and MP's F-based text has **"fourteen ships"** and survivors "except only eighteen" of about 600. The disagreement could be an OCR or extraction error, or a real difference between Yule's composite and F. It **cannot be settled without looking at the page images**. Until then, attribute any number to a named witness. Ramusio, as reported in Yule's note 5, has only one of the three ambassadors surviving, "Goza".

The Samara chapter is **Yule-Cordier Bk III ch. X**, "The Kingdoms of Samara and Dagroian"; ch. IX is "Java the Less… Ferlec and Basma" ([Wikisource III.9](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo/Book_3/Chapter_9); [III.10](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo/Book_3/Chapter_10)). It contains the following ([Wikisource III.10](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo/Book_3/Chapter_10); inspected HTML, no printed pages):

- Marco "detained five months by the weather";
- "about 2000 men";
- "large ditches" on the landward side;
- "bulwarks or stockades of timber", built "for fear of those brutes of man-eaters";
- islanders who "supplied them with victuals";
- fish, rice and no wheat, and a tree-wine that is "excellent drink";
- Indian nuts "[as big as a man's head]", in Yule's own brackets, whose liquid is "more delicate than wine or any other drink".

**The superlative wine comparison attaches to the coconut liquid, not to the palm wine.** The exact phrase "better than wine" was not found in MP and was not checked in Ramusio's Italian.

Marsden's R (Bk III ch. XIII, pp. 341–343) has the same core, plus two details that need checking against F and Z: natives supplying victuals "according to an agreement made with them", and "blockhouses or redoubts of wood" ([Marsden](https://archive.org/stream/marcopolo00polouoft/marcopolo00polouoft_djvu.txt)).

MP §167 (pp. 372–373) prints first-person wording ("my companions", "I caused") with the following attributions in its margins ([MP OCR](https://archive.org/stream/descriptionofwor01polo/descriptionofwor01polo_djvu.txt); noisy OCR, italics lost):

- **"five towers or castles of beams and of logs"** next to the **Z** siglum;
- **VB reading "six" months**;
- non-F sigla (FB, P, Z, VB, VA, L, LT) near many phrases;
- the nut liquid "of a taste like wine or syrup" (L, Z, VB).

**Whether F itself contains the five-month sentence cannot be determined** until Benedetto, Ronchi, Eusebi or Simion–Burgio is checked.

#### What the editors inferred

Yule's Prologue note 4 glosses "Java" as "in this case Sumatra, as will appear hereafter". His note 1 to the Samara chapter calls the place "very likely to have been sought as a shelter to the Great Kaan's fleet during the south-west monsoon". His Introduction §21 speaks of "long detentions on the coast of Sumatra, and in the South of India". Marsden (1818, p. 341 n. 3) had already timed the five months from a c. 1291 departure using the monsoon. **No inspected witness ties the Samara stay to the bridal fleet.** The identification is an editorial reconstruction going back at least to Marsden. The "detentions in the South of India" are Yule's alone.

Cordier's addition to note 3, signed "H.C.", reports **Parker's view that Samara is more likely Siam than Sumatra**. That is a dissenting identification the manuscript may want to acknowledge.

#### Whether Yule's 18 months include the five

They do. Yule's footnote to Introduction §21 says the eighteen months "must include the five months' stoppage". With that reading, Hormuz falls "about November 1293" and Ghazan's camp "a month or two later". The same footnote gives an alternative of "twenty-six months" to Ghazan's camp in March 1294, and Yule remarks that "March 1294 is perhaps too late a date" ([Gutenberg #10636](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10636/pg10636-images.html), ~pp. 23–24; [1871 p. liii](https://archive.org/stream/bookofsermarcopo01polo/bookofsermarcopo01polo_djvu.txt)).

One extraction run labelled the paragraph "H.C.", but its presence in the 1871 edition settles it as **Yule's**. Yule's arithmetic only works if the island stay falls inside the 18 months. That requires reading the Prologue's three months as the China–Sumatra leg, which is his construction, not the text's. **No other editor's view of the durations was accessed** (Pelliot, Moule, Benedetto, Latham, Ronchi, Haw, Larner).

#### Which ports are named

In the return Prologue as inspected, **none**. Zayton comes from Yule ("appear to have sailed"). Hormuz comes from Yule's note 6 ("probably") and Marsden's footnote. Ceylon, Kayal and "ports of Western India" come from Yule's own "(perhaps)". The notes did not examine ports on the outward itinerary, so that part of the brief's question is **unresolved** here. Yule's general description of Zayton as "the port of embarkation for India" (Introduction, ~p. 110) is not tied to the 1292 voyage.

**Suggested formulation, III-3 (Samara).**

> In the Book's chapter on the kingdom of Samara in Java the Less (Yule-Cordier III.10), Marco is held there five months because the weather (in some witnesses, contrary winds) would not let the ships go on. One witness, VB, gives six. With about two thousand men he has ditches dug on the landward side and timber defences raised against the island's man-eaters. Yule renders these "bulwarks or stockades of timber" and Ramusio "blockhouses"; the Latin Z witness, as printed by Moule and Pelliot, specifies five timber "towers or castles". Meanwhile the islanders supply the party with food. The chapter does not say that this stay was part of the voyage with the Mongol bride. Marsden and Yule made that connection.

**Suggested formulation, III-9 (Prologue / return voyage).**

> The Prologue is brief. After some three months the fleet reached an island "towards the South, which is called Java", and then sailed the Indian Sea for eighteen months more before reaching Arghun's land. In the texts examined it names no port of departure or arrival and mentions no halt on the island. Yule identified this "Java" with Sumatra and the halt with the five months of the Samara chapter. He wrote that the fleet "appear[s]" to have sailed from Zayton, reckoned that the eighteen months "must include the five months' stoppage", and suggested "perhaps" calls at Ceylon, Kayal and western Indian ports before landfall at Hormuz. These are reconstructions, not statements of the text.

### Q3. The stele was underground, so Polo's omission tells us nothing

#### What Polo's text says

Polo's chapter on Kenjanfu (Yule-Cordier Bk II ch. XLI; 1903 vol. II p. 24 per the Synopsis) says the people "are Idolaters", describes Prince Mangalai's palace, and contains **no reference to Christians** ([Wikisource II.41](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Travels_of_Marco_Polo/Book_2/Chapter_41); inspected transcription).

#### What Yule and Cordier say

Yule's note 3 (1903 vol. II p. 27, per the OCR running head) makes Si-ngan fu the site of "flourishing Christian Churches in the 7th century" and of the monument, "the discovery of which a thousand years later disclosed their forgotten existence". The same note says it "was accidentally found in 1625 by some workmen". At pp. 29–30 (page break uncertain) the note adds: "It is reasonably supposed by Pauthier that the monument may have been buried in 845" ([archive.org 1903 vol. II OCR](https://archive.org/stream/bookofsermarcopo002polo/bookofsermarcopo002polo_djvu.txt)).

Cordier's bracketed additions concern Havret's projected work, Legge's 1888 translation and the Havret–Hirth "O-lo-pen" dispute. In the OCR read, Cordier says nothing about the discovery date or about Polo's omission. The doubt Richthofen met at Xi'an (missionaries "emphatically denied that Marco could ever have been at Si-ngan fu", p. 29) concerned **the city's name**, not the stele.

#### The stele's history, separating evidence from assumption

**No primary source records the burial.** The 845 Huichang date is a conjecture: Pauthier's, "reasonably supposed … may have been" in Yule; "perhaps" in Keevak, who nonetheless writes that "it had been underground for eight hundred years" ([Keevak 2008, p. 10, preview](https://hkupress.hku.hk/image/catalog/pdf-preview/9789622098954.pdf); inspected). Wikipedia says "probably" (tertiary).

The physical evidence is only that workmen dug the stone up. The **discovery accounts conflict** on place and circumstance:

- Kircher's 1667 text puts it in 1625, in a ditch dug in "Siganfu".
- Martini, as quoted in Kircher, says "Samyun" (Sanyuan).
- Boym says the village of "Cheu-che" (Zhouzhi) ([Kircher, tr. Van Tuyl, pp. 5–7](https://htext.stanford.edu/content/kircher/china/kircher.pdf); inspected via PDF summary).
- Keevak summarises further accounts (Bartoli; Lin Tong 1679) and reports that Havret and Pelliot judged some of them "fantasized" and "full of errors". He argues that Zhouzhi may be a site of erection rather than discovery (pp. 25–27, 136).

The **earliest dated witnesses** are Zhang Gengyu's rubbing, sent to Li Zhizao, who published the text "in the same year" with a colophon, and the 1625 Jesuit annual letter (Keevak pp. 10–11; REPORTED via Keevak).

**On 1625 against 1623**, 1625 is conventional, not certain. The Lieu/Eccles project PDF says "circa 1623" without argument ([manichaeism.de PDF, p. 15](https://www.manichaeism.de/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Xian-Monument-5.11.2023.pdf); SNIPPET). Wikipedia, citing a Ma 2025, says most researchers place it "between 1621 and 1625" (tertiary). Havret's case for 1623 was **not accessed** and must not be attributed to him without checking.

#### Whether anyone has argued from Polo's silence

**No such argument was found.** Summaries of Wood (1995) list the Great Wall, tea, chopsticks, foot-binding and calligraphy, not the stele ([Wikipedia, *Did Marco Polo Go to China?*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Did_Marco_Polo_Go_to_China%3F); SNIPPET). Wood herself was not read. The seventeenth-century reception actually ran the other way: Ricci and Boym used Polo **as corroboration** that Christians lived in Cathay (Keevak p. 18; Kircher p. 7). The question of whether Polo could have seen the stone has to be kept apart from the history of the argument, and on the evidence above the answer to the first is "no evidence that it was visible".

#### Christians at Xi'an independently of the stele

Chen Yuan's study, read in an online reprint (REPORTED), reports two Yuan-era items for Shaanxi ([Chen Yuan, 《元也里可温教考》](https://www.aisixiang.com/data/175999.html)):

- an edict stele at Zhouzhi, probably 1314 (Yanyou 1), exempting 也里可温 (Christians) along with monks and Daoists;
- a 1300 petition by Shaanxi officials about taxing Erke'ün who traded (*Tongzhi tiaoge* juan 29).

Chen also reports a church at Ganzhou and a 1282 tax order for Erke'ün in Hexi. **Nothing seen places a Christian community at Xi'an itself in c. 1275–95.** Keevak judges that Yuan Nestorians "were almost certainly not descendants" of the Tang community (p. 10). Prince Ananda of Anxi, whose seat was at Xi'an, is associated with Islam (Wikipedia, tertiary).

**Recommendation.** Either omit the point or keep it only as a qualified note. The better-documented observation is that Polo calls Kenjanfu's people idolaters while reporting Christians elsewhere in the north-west. Suggested wording:

> Polo's chapter on Kenjanfu (Xi'an) says only that its people were "Idolaters". Yule's note recalls the city's seventh-century churches and the monument of 781, "the discovery of which a thousand years later disclosed their forgotten existence". It would be easy to call Polo's failure to mention that stone a silence, but the term claims too much. Workmen dug it up only in the 1620s (conventionally 1625). When and why it went into the ground is unknown, and burial during the persecution of 845 is a long-standing conjecture. Nothing suggests it could be seen in Polo's day.

### Q4. The return route: four explicit stops after Persia, and reconstruction before it

#### What the Polo witness names

Yule-Cordier Prologue XVIII names, in order ([DSR scans 0330](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-30/V-1/page/0330.html.en), [0331](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-30/V-1/page/0331.html.en), [0332](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-30/V-1/page/0332.html.en); OCR via tool):

- "Java";
- the Sea of India, for eighteen months;
- Argon dead;
- the lady delivered to Casan;
- sovereignty "held by Kiacatu", who gives "four golden tablets";
- Trebizond, Constantinople, Negropont and Venice, with "the year 1295".

**It names no departure port, no Hormuz, no Abhar and no Tauris** in the passages read. **No witness was found naming Hormuz** as the return landfall. Ramusio's Italian was not checked, and Marsden's R has Ormuz only in his footnote. **Zayton as departure port** is asserted by Yule ("appear") and by Iranica ("left China by sea from Zaytun (Quanzhou)") without a witness locator ([Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=1530)).

A **"nine months"** stay, sometimes associated with the Persian leg, was **not found** in the scans read and should be treated as unverified.

#### Evidence about the embassy against evidence about the Polos

The Chinese notice attests the envoys and their destination. Rashid al-Din, through Hammer, Yule and Pelliot, attests the bride's reception and marriage. **Only the Book places the Polos with the embassy.** The case for their presence rests on the match between the envoy names in the Book and those in the Chinese notice, as argued by Yang, Cleaves and Pelliot (REPORTED).

On **Trebizond**, Bolognari (2024, p. 42) cites heavy commercial losses recorded in "Matteo the Elder's" 1310 will, drawing on Bolognari & Simion 2024 ([Bolognari PDF](https://iris.unive.it/retrieve/aa5a195c-24d8-4473-ab10-5f8f8fde7f6a/Iacopo%20d_Acqui_TranScript.pdf); inspected via extraction; will not inspected). The route note had flagged this archival source as unlocated, so it is now located only at second hand. On **Venice 1295**, Yule-Cordier §22 says "according to all the texts some time in 1295", while Cordier remarks that 1296 would fit the "six-and-twenty years" better ([Gutenberg #10636](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10636/pg10636-images.html)). No archival corroboration of the arrival was found.

### Q5. Jacopo says Layaç and 1296; Curzola appears only in 1559

#### What Jacopo's text says

The best available text is Bolognari's transcription from **Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, D 526 inf. (14th c.), c. 77v**, printed in *TranScript* 3 (2024), pp. 36–37. Bolognari treats Trivulziana 704 as a later related redaction. The text was read via machine extraction of the open-access PDF ([Bolognari 2024](https://iris.unive.it/retrieve/aa5a195c-24d8-4473-ab10-5f8f8fde7f6a/Iacopo%20d_Acqui_TranScript.pdf)). A short excerpt, in Bolognari's spelling, to be checked against the printed page before quotation:

> "Anno Yhesu Christi .MCCLXXXXVI., tempore Bonifacii pape .VI. … factum est prelium in mari de Arminia aput dicitur Layaç de .XV. galeis mercatorum Ianuensium, .XXV. Venatorum"

Jacopo then says that Marco "venetus, qui erat cum mercathoribus illis, qui dictus est Millonus" was taken, led "in carcerem Ianue", and there "facit librum de magnis mirabilibus mundi". Bolognari's apparatus gives "Pro .VIII." for the pope's numeral and "Pro Venetorum" for "Venatorum".

Read this way, **Jacopo specifies** a sea battle off Armenia at Layaç, 15 Genoese merchant galleys against 25 Venetian, Marco captured as one of the merchants, prison in Genoa "for a long time", and the book written there. **He does not mention** Curzola, 1298, Dandolo, a galley command or a wound.

The pope's numeral (VI) is visibly corrupt. Yule's note 30 reports a **Turin manuscript** that "contains no date" and places the capture at "La Glaza" ([Yule-Cordier vol. 1 text](https://atlas-group.cs.brown.edu/data/gutenberg/1/0/6/3/10636/old/10636.txt); inspected via a plain-text mirror with no page numbers). The Historiae Patriae Monumenta edition (Avogadro, Turin 1848) was **not opened**, and the column for the Polo passage is unconfirmed. Yule (n. 29) places Jacopo in the Bellingeri family, attested in 1289, 1320 and 1334. The notes' draft wording calls him "Dominican", but no source for that is cited, so verify it before use.

#### What is known about Ayas in 1294

Yule-Cordier §32 describes a Genoese victory off Ayas, with the Genoese "weaker by about a third", taking all but three Venetian galleys and capturing Marco Basilio. Yule §37 dates it to "May, 1294". Bolognari (pp. 40–41, nn. 43–45) dates the battle to 1294 and reports that Iacopo da Varazze's Genoese chronicle gives **matching galley numbers**, while Pipino narrates the battle without Marco. **No 1296 battle at Ayas was found**, but possible 1296 clashes were not researched.

**"1296" is not impossible in itself.** It falls inside the 1294–99 war, after Marco's return. The conflict arises only if the notice is identified with Ayas in May 1294 **and** the 1295 return via Trebizond is accepted. Yule's own emendation to 1294 is what makes Jacopo "impossible".

#### Yule's treatment and Bolognari's

**Yule (1903, §37)** argues as follows:

- The Ayas statement "cannot be true, for we know that he did not reach Venice till 1295".
- "LXXXXVI is as certainly a mistake for LXXXXIV".
- He keeps the passage as "evidence from a contemporary source that he was taken prisoner in some sea-fight with the Genoese", "in corroboration of the Ramusian Tradition" of Curzola.

This harmonisation is Yule's alone. He concedes that no contemporary authority names Curzola.

**Bolognari (2024)** emends 1296 to 1294, identifies the battle as Laiazzo, and calls Marco's presence there in "patente contrasto" with the return route and the Trebizond losses (p. 42). In the parts extracted he **does not adopt Curzola** and gives no final verdict on where Marco was captured.

#### Ramusio and other evidence

Ramusio's 1559 preface, read in Yule's English (Intro §6), has Lampa Doria with 70 galleys at Curzola and Marco "put in charge of" a galley under Andrea Dandolo. The battle is "on the September feast of Our Lady", and Marco, "being wounded", is sent to Genoa. **No pre-Ramusio source naming Curzola was found.** The Italian original was not seen.

Gullino's *DBI* entry (2015) follows Curzola (8 Sept 1298, Lamba Doria; prisoners released 28 Aug 1299) without discussing Jacopo ([Treccani DBI](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/marco-polo_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); inspected via summary). Wikipedia, citing Puljiz-Šostik 2015 and Latham 1958, favours a 1296 skirmish and calls Curzola a 16th-century tradition (tertiary; pointer only).

The Curzola date in the chronicles varies between 6, 7 and 8 September (Julian). The S. Matteo inscription gives the 7th, which Yule notes was a Sunday (Yule n. 17). The Prologue's statement that Marco was in prison in Genoa in 1298 was **not verified** in these notes.

**Recommended wording (Q5).**

> The chronicler Jacopo d'Acqui, writing in the early fourteenth century, records that Marco was taken with Venetian merchants in a sea-fight "in Arminia" at "Layaç" (Ayas) and carried to prison in Genoa, where he made his book. His manuscript dates this 1296. Yule and, more recently, Bolognari read the date as a slip for 1294 and identify the fight with the Genoese victory off Ayas that year. On that reading the notice conflicts with the Polos' return by way of Trebizond in 1295. The familiar story of Marco commanding a galley at Curzola in September 1298 is first found in Ramusio's preface of 1559, and no earlier source naming Curzola has been found.

## Conclusion

The common pattern across the five questions is **sedimentation**: nineteenth-century editorial reconstructions have hardened into apparent facts. Yule built his route around a departure date derived from an untraced Hammer reference ("Zayton", "Sumatra", "Hormuz", "January 1292", "18 includes 5"). Ramusio added a galley command at Curzola two and a half centuries after the event. Later writers have read a "silence" into a stone that was underground. In each case the primary layer is thinner and more interesting than the composite: a provisioning memo for 160 travellers, an island called only "Java", a chronicler's corrupt numeral pointing to Ayas. The editorial gain lies in letting the reader see where the text stops and the reconstruction begins.

Two dependencies matter most for the manuscript. First, **the departure date controls the Arghun question**: only Yang's early-1291 reading makes "Arghun died while the fleet was at sea" true. Yule's 1292 reading reverses it, so the order should be stated conditionally. Second, **the 1295 return date controls the Jacopo question**: his "1296" becomes "impossible" only after editors convert it to 1294, and Cordier himself wondered whether the return belonged in 1296. One scan-checking pass over Yule-Cordier vol. 1 (Prologue XVIII, notes 3–6, Introduction §§21 and 37) would settle the ship and survivor counts and the page numbers, and would move most of the "hold" items above to "safe".
