# Marco Polo's sea chapters and return voyage (c. 1291–1295) against modern maritime archaeology and Indian Ocean history

This report synthesizes six research-note files into findings on how modern maritime archaeology and Indian Ocean history bear on the sea chapters of Marco Polo's book and the return voyage, using Yule–Cordier (3rd ed., 1903, with Cordier's 1920 supplement) as the base text (Gutenberg ed. 12410 for the text itself). It covers Book III chapters on Chinese junks and Chipangu (III-01), Champa and Java (III-02), Locac/Pentam/Malaiur and the Sumatran kingdoms (III-03), the Nicobars/Andamans/Sri Lanka (III-04), Maabar (III-05), Mutfili (III-06), Coilum/Malabar/Gujarat and the western Indian Ocean littoral (III-07), the Arabian coast and Hormuz (III-08), and the return voyage itself (III-09), followed by a recommended-overviews list (III-F1) and a monsoon-calendar summary. Per the underlying research notes' own repeated caution, a substantial number of named specialist sources (Moule & Pelliot 1938, Cynthia Talbot's epigraphic detail, Jean Aubin on the horse trade, Cleaves 1976 read directly, and others) were not independently accessed in this pass; every such gap is carried through into the verdict/confidence columns and collected in the Unresolved Questions section at the end. Nothing below states a page number, quotation, DOI, or URL that was not explicitly present in the research notes.

**Nanhai I bulkhead-count conflict.** The research notes flag, and this report preserves without resolving, a direct conflict between two descriptions of the same wreck: one source states 14 compartments/14 partition boards, another states 13 transverse watertight bulkheads. This is a conflict within the cited secondary/site-report material itself, not a disagreement between named scholars, and it was not adjudicated in this research pass.

**Gold palace identification.** The Chūson-ji/Konjikidō identification is repeated across non-peer-reviewed tourism and travel-writing sources but has not been located in art-historical or Polo-studies scholarship; the researcher explicitly recommends it be presented as a "plausible, widely circulated, but not scholarly-confirmed" candidate rather than a settled identification.

**Takashima excavation authorship.** The research brief anticipated Kenzo Hayashida as a named excavation authority; the only substantive source retrieved (Sasaki 2015) does not name Hayashida and instead credits the Takashima and Matsuura Boards of Education for the site reports. This is noted as an open discrepancy between the brief's expectation and what was actually retrieved, not as a claim that Hayashida's work does not exist.

**Locac identification.** This is a genuine three-way scholarly split (Lopburi/Lavo vs. Borneo vs. "Khmer Empire" generally), with the 1964 reconstruction essay and the Lopburi tradition the closer approximation to a majority view and Wheatley's Borneo thesis a significant minority position the 1964 article explicitly argues against. The research notes also flag that "Langkasuka," as posed in the original research question, does not appear to be a position any source actually holds — the real debate runs Lopburi vs. Borneo vs. "general Khmer Empire," not Lopburi vs. Langkasuka.

**Malaiur identification.** The Melayu/Jambi identification is the standard toponymic reading in the broader Srivijaya-successor-state literature (Wolters, Hall — not directly accessed in this pass), while a peninsular alternative was floated by Schlegel in the 19th century. No source confirms a "Singapore/Temasek" reading of Polo's text specifically, so that half of the debate as framed in the original brief remains an open question rather than a documented position.

## The monsoon calendar and its governance of sailing schedules

Thirteenth-century shipping across the South China Sea and Indian Ocean was governed by the Asian monsoon cycle, structured around two reversing wind regimes. The Northeast (NE, winter) monsoon blew over the South China Sea roughly October to March, and more weakly over the Indian Ocean roughly December to late February. The Southwest (SW, summer) monsoon blew over the Indian Ocean roughly late April to September, and more weakly over the South China Sea roughly May to August — with the NE wind arriving earlier but also lingering later in the South China Sea, and the SW wind starting earlier and lasting later over the Indian Ocean. Ships leaving China rode the outbound NE monsoon (by one account, departing in the November–December window by the Chinese calendar) and could not sail directly home against it; they had to "wind-wait," often with intermediate layovers in the Malay world/Sumatra, until the wind reversed to the SW monsoon (from roughly April/May) to continue or return. A full round-trip voyage cycle (China → Indian Ocean → China) commonly took about a year to a year and a half, driven by this monsoon cycle rather than by sailing speed; on at least one occasion a fleet's main Chinese departure port held ships for about eight months awaiting the NE wind, and fleets gathered at intermediate ports (e.g., Malacca in early May) to sail home together once the SW wind set in.

This calendar directly explains the pattern in Polo's own account of the return voyage: departing China on the winter NE monsoon, his fleet was obliged to break the Indian Ocean crossing into monsoon-bound stages, with the roughly five-month forced stop in Sumatra being a logical consequence of waiting out the "wrong" monsoon before continuing toward India and the Arabian Sea, rather than inefficiency or choice.

This data is sourced to Deng Hui and Li Xin, "The Asian Monsoons and Zheng He's Voyages to the Western Ocean," *Journal of Navigation* (Royal Institute of Navigation/Cambridge University Press), published online 2 March 2011 (https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/48751F740571B1F57772AB38BD9E2AA1/S0373463310000469a.pdf/the-asian-monsoons-and-zheng-hes-voyages-to-the-western-ocean.pdf). This article describes the early-15th-century (Ming) Zheng He voyages rather than Polo's late-13th-century (Yuan) crossing, but the underlying monsoon-wind geography and seasonal mechanics were effectively unchanged between the two periods, so it is used here as a reasonable analogical source for the physical constraints Polo's fleet faced — flagged explicitly, per the research notes, as describing a slightly later voyage. The journal's specific volume/issue number and DOI could not be confirmed from the material retrieved (only the copyright year and publisher were visible). George F. Hourani's *Arab Seafaring in the Indian Ocean in Ancient and Early Medieval Times* (expanded ed., Princeton UP, 1995) is identified as a foundational treatment of monsoon-dependent Arab/Islamicate navigation covering meteorology in general terms, but page-level monsoon-calendar detail from Hourani himself was not retrieved, so the specific month-by-month data above rests on Deng Hui and Li Xin alone. No dedicated source specifically quantifying the monsoon calendar for the Arabian Sea/Malabar coast leg (as opposed to the South China Sea leg) was located; Prange's *Monsoon Islam* (2018) is flagged as the most likely place to find this but was not accessed at chapter level.

## Unresolved questions

The following were explicitly flagged in the research notes as not found, unverified, or requiring direct consultation of a specific work or the base text itself. Nothing below has been resolved by inference in this report.

1. **Base-text verification needed across the board.** Several findings above depend on a secondary source's paraphrase of what Yule-Cordier (1903/1920) actually says; a direct re-read of the primary text is needed to confirm or correct: (a) whether Polo describes a footprint at Adam's Peak (a tertiary source claims he does not, which conflicts with the research task's own premise); (b) the exact wording and chapter location of the "~600 deaths" figure on the return voyage; (c) the exact presentation of the Trebizond robbery episode and whether Yule's apparatus marks it as deriving only from the Ramusio redaction; (d) the exact text of the "stones under the skin" invulnerability episode in Book III, chs. I–III.
2. **A.C. Moule and Paul Pelliot, *Marco Polo: The Description of the World* (1938)** was not directly accessed at any point in this research pass, despite being the single most frequently recommended follow-up source across nearly every section: for the Chūson-ji gold-palace identification, the Andaman cynocephali motif, the tooth/bowl relic and "Sergamoni Borcan" passage, the Ferlec/Basman/Dagroian/Lambri/Fansur place-name cluster, "Pentam = Bintan," and "Lar" (Gujarat vs. Konkan).
3. **Cynthia Talbot's specific epigraphic discussion** of the Motupalli abhaya-sasanam (with page numbers) was not accessed; only a news report's paraphrase that the 1244 charter belongs to Ganapatideva, not Rudrama Devi, was retrieved.
4. **Jean Aubin's work on the Hormuz/Kish–Maabar horse trade**, and any modern assessment of Wassaf's reported horse-trade figures, was not accessed; only bibliographic leads (an Iranica Online entry's existence) were found.
5. **Francis Woodman Cleaves's 1976 article itself** ("A Chinese Source Bearing on Marco Polo's Departure from China and a Persian Source on His Arrival in Persia," *Harvard Journal of Asiatic Studies* 36) was never read directly; its relationship to the Yang Zhijiu identification of the three envoys' names in the Yongle Dadian (same discovery? separate/earlier discovery? one scholar building on the other?) is unresolved.
6. **Whether the "Jingshi Dadian" and "Yongle Dadian" refer to the same source** for the envoy-ration document, or whether the document was originally compiled in the former and only transmitted via partial preservation in the latter, is unresolved.
7. **Whether the Trebizond robbery is attested anywhere beyond Polo's own account (possibly only the Ramusio redaction)** — no independent Genoese, Venetian, Byzantine, or Trapezuntine documentary source was found or sought with any depth in this pass.
8. **The exact source and reliability of the "~600 deaths" figure** on the return voyage — not traced to a specific page, and no modern scholarly source assessing its plausibility (e.g., against known mortality rates on comparable medieval Indian Ocean voyages) was found.
9. **Kenzo Hayashida's publications on the Takashima excavations**, anticipated by the original research brief, were not located; the only substantive Takashima source found (Sasaki 2015) credits the Takashima and Matsuura Boards of Education instead.
