## 1. Ceylon shark-charmers: 1856 vs 1859 vs 1885

**Verdict:** Not reconciled by records. The three dates come from three different secondary claims, and they need not describe the same event. No dated Ceylon administrative order was inspected.

| Source | Directly inspected locator | What it says | Evidence type |

**Reading of the evidence (cautious):**

- Ostroff splits the ending into stages: a prohibition on *private* patronage, at an unstated date; no charmers *employed* after 1859 (“according to some reports”); and the *system* dissolved by 1885. That distinction (state employment vs. private practice vs. institutional end) may explain why 1859 and 1885 both appear. It is not a record-based reconciliation.
- Fernando's 1856 differs from Ostroff's 1859 even though her note sends readers to Ostroff p. 133. The page this session read does not give 1856. That date has no visible primary source in either text.
- Kunz's 1885 is the end of the whole practice in Kunz's telling. Ostroff appears to use it for the dissolution of the system.
- Ceylon held no fishery 1837–1854 (Ostroff, introduction), so dates in the 1850s refer to the resumed fisheries.

**Safe manuscript wording:** “British Ceylon at first paid shark-charmers, then moved against them. Accounts disagree on when the practice ended, with dates from the 1850s to 1885.” Or name the sources: “Kunz and Stevenson (1908) say they were done away with in 1885.” Do not give one ban year.

## 2. Wen Huanran et al. 1980, historical crocodilians

**Verdict:** The article exists; its contents were **not inspected**. It is therefore no evidence for any Yunnan record.

- Confirmed citation, from the reference list of a 2011 *Progress in Geography* review of the CAS Institute of Geography (ref. 33): 文焕然, 何业恒, 黄祝坚, 等. 历史时期中国马来鳄分布的变迁及其原因的初步研究 [Preliminary study of changes in the distribution of the *Malay crocodile* in China in historical times, and their causes]. 华东师范大学学报(自然科学版), 1980(3): 109–121. A 1980 South China Normal University article cites a 1979 CAS Institute of Geography draft of the same study (authors 文焕然, 黄祝坚, 徐俊传, as OCR'd).
- The title identifies the animal as 马来鳄 (the Malay gharial, *Tomistoma schlegelii*), not a generic “crocodile”. Other Chinese scholarship disputes this: some workers assign Song-period Pearl Delta bones to *Tomistoma*, others to the saltwater crocodile, *Crocodylus porosus* (secondary popular summaries only; not used as evidence).
- Reprint: the paper appears to be included in 文焕然 & 文榕生, 《中国历史时期植物与动物变迁研究》 (Chongqing, 1995; 2006; 2019, ISBN 9787229142216). The published contents list shows 马来鳄 among the animals covered; the chapter page was not seen.
- No full text found online (CNKI-type access would be needed). Whether it contains any Yunnan / Carajan / Yuan-era record is **unknown**.

**Safe manuscript wording:** keep the existing fallback. “The Book's great clawed ‘serpents’ of Carajan are usually taken to be crocodilians; the species, and whether any lived there, remain uncertain.”

## 3. Weisgerber 2004, Kerman turquoise

**Verdict:** The chapter exists; its text was **not inspected**. The GIA wording remains the only accessible statement.

- Confirmed (Abstracta Iranica record of the catalogue): Gerd Weisgerber, “Die Türkisgruben von Nishapur / The Turquoise Mines at Nishapur,” pp. 502–509, in T. Stöllner, R. Slotta and A. Vatandoust (eds.), *Persiens Antike Pracht / Persia's Ancient Splendour: Mining, Handicraft and Archaeology*, exhibition catalogue, Deutsches Bergbau-Museum Bochum, 28 Nov. 2004 – 29 May 2005; an English PDF version was issued on CD-ROM. GIA's citation “Persian Antiques Splendor” garbles that title.
- GIA, Shirdam et al., *Gems & Gemology* Fall 2021 (PDF inspected): “the Shahr-i Babak deposit in western Kerman Province, the latter of which was mentioned in Marco Polo's *Book of the Wonders of the World* between 1390 and 1430 (Weisgerber, 2004).” Elsewhere (p. 251): “Shahr-I Babak turquoise comes from the Meiduk mine, a porphyry [copper deposit].”
- Unverified hypothesis, not a finding: *Livre des merveilles du monde* is the title of illuminated French manuscripts of the early 15th century (e.g., BnF fr. 2810). A 1390–1430 range might therefore refer to a manuscript or its illustration, not to mining. Nothing seen here confirms that the chapter names Shahr-i Babak, Polo or these dates.

**Safe manuscript wording:** “Polo reports turquoise from the province of Kerman.” Do not name the Shahr-i Babak/Meiduk mine as his source or as active in his time.

## 4. Socotra whale line: the 1559 print

**Verdict:** Confirmed at facsimile level for Ramusio. F and Z manuscript folios were **not inspected**.

- **Inspected:** Ramusio, *Secondo volume delle Navigationi et Viaggi* (Venice: Giunti, 1559), “Di M. Marco Polo,” Libro terzo, cap. 34 “Dell'isola di Soccotera,” **fol. 57v**, sections D–E. Heidelberg University Library copy, DOI 10.11588/diglit.9381, IIIF image `ramusio1559bd2:00_57_v` (viewer page ≈0160).
- Transcription (long s normalised, abbreviations kept as printed):
  > …trouasi per gli habitanti alle riue di questa Isola molto ambracano, che vien fuori del ventre delle Balene, & per esser gran mercantia s'ingegnano d'andarle à prenderle, con alcuni ferri, c'hanno le barbe, che ficcati nella Balena non si possono piu cauare, alli quali è attaccata vna corda lunghissima con vna bottesella, che và sopra il Mare, accioche come la Balena è morta la sappino doue trouare, & la conducono al lido, doue li cauano fuori del ventre l'ambracano, & della testa assai botte di olio.
- Cautious translation: “…the inhabitants find much ambergris on the shores of this island, which comes out of the whales' bellies. Because it is a great commodity, they contrive to go and take them with certain barbed irons, which once driven into the whale cannot be pulled out. To these irons is tied a very long cord with a small cask that floats on the sea, so that when the whale is dead they know where to find it. They tow it to shore, take the ambergris from its belly, and from its head many casks of oil.”
- This matches Burgio and Fornasiero's (2011) report: *bottesella* (small cask) as the line float, and *botte* as a measure or container for oil.
- Context on the same folio: the end of cap. 33 also mentions ambergris on the Male/Female Islands. In the same chapter, the islanders are called the greatest enchanters, who raise winds and storms.
- F (BnF fr. 1116) and Z (Toledo, Biblioteca Capitular, Zelada 49.20): **not inspected** at folio level in this run. The comparison relies on Burgio and Fornasiero 2011, p. 133 nn. 19–20, as before.
- Not an independent observation: this is Ramusio's text, in part derived from Z-type sources (Burgio and Fornasiero). It is not evidence that Marco Polo witnessed a hunt.

**Safe manuscript wording:** “In Ramusio's 1559 version, a barbed iron is tied to a very long cord with a small floating cask, so that the hunters can find the dead whale. They take the ambergris from its belly and many casks of oil from its head.”
