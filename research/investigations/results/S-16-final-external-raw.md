# Editorial Guide to Marco Polo’s Uncertainties: A Source-Critical Resolution of Key Toponyms, Events, and Legends

## Archaeological Identification and Coordinates of Old Hormuz

This section provides a source-critical examination of the archaeological identification and geographical coordinates of Old Hormuz, the mainland port city visited by Marco Polo. The analysis prioritizes archaeological surveys and historical-geographical texts to determine the most likely location, assess the evidence for proposed sites, and supply a defensible coordinate where possible. The investigation focuses on reconciling Polo's textual descriptions with modern archaeological findings, specifically evaluating the relationship between site K-103, Aurel Stein's Kalātun, and the broader region near modern Minab.

Marco Polo's account is the foundational text for identifying Old Hormuz. He explicitly describes the city as being located on the Iranian mainland, situated on the Mīnāb river and approximately one farsakh from the sea [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf)]. His description contrasts sharply with the island-based Hormuz that became prominent after the 13th century. This distinction is crucial, as it establishes that the subject of his detailed description was a now-abandoned mainland port. Polo visited this location twice during his extensive travels, first in 1272 and again in 1293, providing contemporary testimony to its status as a flourishing commercial hub at those times [[3](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hormuz-ii/)]. The city was the capital of the kingdom of Hormuz and served as a vital entrepôt for trade between India, Persia, and the wider world, dealing in spices, precious stones, and horses [[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hormuz), [4](https://thirdvenetia.substack.com/p/hormuz-travels-of-marco-poo)]. Its strategic importance is underscored by the presence of a strong castle that acted as a key surveillance point for the entire Persian Gulf [[4](https://thirdvenetia.substack.com/p/hormuz-travels-of-marco-poo)].

A pivotal piece of corroborating historical evidence comes from the well-documented relocation of the city's population. In 1301, following incursions by Tartar forces, King Baha ud-Din Ayaz led the inhabitants and their possessions from the old city of Hormuz, also known as Naband or Dewankhana, to the neighboring island of Jerun (also spelled Jarun or Zarun) [[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hormuz), [3](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hormuz-ii/)]. This move solidified the distinction between the mainland port Polo described and the new island settlement, which became known as New Hormuz [[3](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hormuz-ii/)]. Later chroniclers, including Abulfeda and the traveler Friar Odoric, noted this transfer, reinforcing the understanding that the mainland site was abandoned and thus could be referred to as "Old Hormuz" [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. Ibn Battuta later specified the distance between the old and new cities as three farsakhs, providing a geographical marker for the separation [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. This historical event transforms Old Hormuz from a purely textual construct into a verifiable archaeological reality: the abandoned port of the mainland city from which the modern island city of Hormuz was founded.

Modern archaeological work converges on a specific location for these remains. Daniel Potts, writing in the *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, identifies site K-103 from Andrew Williamson's archaeological survey conducted in 1969–70 as a possible candidate for Old Hormuz [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf)]. This identification is supported by Willem Floor, who places the ruins roughly 10 kilometers southwest of the modern town of Minab [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf)]. More recent excavations led by archaeologist Siamak Sarlak have further refined this location, pinpointing the remnants of the ancient port in the Nakhl-e Ebrahimi area of Minab county in Iran's Hormozgan province [[29](https://www.tehrantimes.com/news/527025/Archaeological-evidence-points-to-ancient-Parthian-naval-base)]. These findings align with geological studies indicating that while the town of Minab has remained stationary for millennia, the ancient port was situated much closer to the coast but lost its maritime access due to siltation [[30](https://www.cais-soas.com/News/2006/April2006/27-04-ancient.htm)]. The archaeological evidence includes traces of a long wharf and extensive ruins located inland, accessible via a creek connected to the Mīnāb River—a landscape perfectly matching Polo's description of a once-flourishing port now partially cut off from the sea [[3](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hormuz-ii/)]. The discovery team, led by Sarlak, confirmed that the central part of Old Hormuz was located near the Strait of Hormuz, while the northern part of Minab was used seasonally by its merchants [[30](https://www.cais-soas.com/News/2006/April2006/27-04-ancient.htm)].

The question of whether site K-103 is the same as the location called Kalātun by Aurel Stein requires careful consideration. While both are identified in the vicinity of modern Minab, the provided sources do not contain explicit comparative analysis to confirm or deny their identity. However, given the high degree of consensus among multiple independent archaeological investigations—Williamson's 1969–70 survey, Floor's geographical assessment, and Sarlak's more recent rediscovery—all pointing to the general area southwest of Minab, it is highly probable that they refer to the same general archaeological zone, if not the exact same surveyed site. The consistency across different methodologies and time periods strengthens the case for this region as the definitive location of Old Hormuz.

Based on this convergence of textual, historical, and archaeological evidence, a defensible approximate coordinate can be supplied for the archaeological site of Old Hormuz. The available information identifies a surveyed site rather than a purely theoretical reference point. The coordinates derived from the Catalan Atlas of 1375, which depicts Hormuz around 1300, are 27° 8′ 48″ N, 57° 4′ 48″ E [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf)]. While this is a cartographic representation and not a direct archaeological survey, it falls within the general region described by modern archaeologists. A more precise figure is not available in the provided sources. The primary uncertainty lies in the lack of a standardized, modern archaeological grid system applied to all the scattered ruins. The term "Minab district" is frequently used, which refers to a regional area rather than a pinpointed urban center [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. Therefore, the coordinate should be understood as representing the general archaeological zone where the port ruins have been found, with a precision limited by the scale of the historical maps and the extent of the surveyed remains. The confidence level in this identification is high, resting on the strong corroboration between Polo's eyewitness account, the documented historical transfer of the capital, and the findings of multiple archaeological surveys.

## Critical Assessment of PEM/PEIN and TENDUC Toponyms

This section undertakes a source-critical evaluation of the identifications for two of Marco Polo's less common Central Asian toponyms: Pein/Pem and Tenduc. The analysis focuses on assessing the competing interpretations of Paul Pelliot, Huntington, and other specialists, incorporating post-2013 scholarship where available. The goal is to provide a provisional recommendation for map labeling, complete with a confidence level and acknowledgment of alternative candidates, adhering strictly to the principle that mere co-location does not prove an identification.

For the toponym Pein (also transcribed as Pem or Peiu), the scholarly debate centers on two primary candidates: Uzun-tatïr and Keriya. Paul Pelliot, a foundational figure in Polian studies, favored the identification of Pein with Uzun-tatïr, a location situated between the modern towns of Keriya and Dandan Uilik [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf), [13](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. This view is widely cited in subsequent scholarship, establishing it as the traditional academic consensus [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf)]. Conversely, the earlier work of Ellsworth Huntington proposed Keriya as the correct identification [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf)]. The value of this debate depends on whether more recent archaeological or historical-geographical work published since Pelliot's time provides independent evidence to support one candidate over the other.

An important contribution to this discussion comes from Rong Xinjiang's analysis, which, while not definitively choosing between Uzun-tatïr and Keriya, strongly supports the general region east of Khotan as the location of Pein [[32](https://www.lotusgemology.com/resources/4-treasures-reference-database/3463)]. Rong Xinjiang identifies the area as an enduringly important administrative district that was also visited by the Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang, who referred to it as Pima or Phema [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm), [32](https://www.lotusgemology.com/resources/4-treasures-reference-database/3463)]. By cross-referencing Yuan dynasty records, Buddhist image traditions, Dunhuang cave inscriptions, and correspondence concerning fine cotton cloth, Rong Xinjiang builds a compelling case for the region's significance during the early Yuan period, which aligns broadly with when Marco Polo traveled [[32](https://www.lotusgemology.com/resources/4-treasures-reference-database/3463)]. This work lends indirect support to the general vicinity suggested by Pelliot's Uzun-tatïr identification. Another source directly identifies the modern site corresponding to Pein as Kiria (Ou-mi), suggesting that this location corresponds to the ancient Pi-mo mentioned by Dutreuil de Rhins and Grenard, who did not themselves find the site but made this suggestion [[13](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/), [24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. Despite this progress, no single piece of post-Pelliot evidence conclusively settles the debate between Uzun-tatïr and Keriya. The evidence points towards the broader region as the correct identification, but the specific site remains disputed.

Given the state of the evidence, placing a definitive point for Pein on a map is premature. The identification is plausible but not certain. The recommended approach for an editorial project would be to label the region east of Khotan as "Pein (approximate location)," reflecting a medium confidence level. This acknowledges Pelliot's influential but unproven choice of Uzun-tatïr/Keriya as the primary candidate while leaving room for future discoveries that might pinpoint the exact location. The key insight from modern scholarship is that the region itself was significant, even if Polo's geographical and mineralogical descriptions there were sometimes misleading [[32](https://www.lotusgemology.com/resources/4-treasures-reference-database/3463)].

The toponym Tenduc presents a more complex picture, with multiple layers of interpretation. Initially, some scholars associated Tenduc with the legendary realm of Prester John, and the related term "Tenduc Province" was identified with Tiande [[31](https://www.academia.edu/121739986/Marco_Polos_Description_on_Nestorians_and_Other_Christian_Groups_in_Yuan_China)]. Later interpretations placed it in a specific Mongol administrative context. One source notes that Polo's mention of a class of people descended from "the race of the Idolaters of Tenduc and of that of the worshippers of Mahommet" connects it to the Ordos bend of the Yellow River, an area corresponding to modern Ningxia [[8](https://www.chinaheritagequarterly.org/editorial.php?issue=010)]. This suggests Tenduc was not a single city but likely a Mongol designation for a territory or people.

A significant development in the study of this region comes from Christopher Atwood, who proposes Olon Süme as the location for another of Polo's toponyms, Sichintingiu [[6](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/935009)]. However, a critical finding from the available sources is that no specialist has explicitly argued that Tenduc and Sichintingiu refer to the same place [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf), [6](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/935009)]. They are consistently treated as distinct entities in scholarly discourse [[5](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/1/11/2019/ica-adv-1-11-2019.pdf), [6](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/935009)]. This represents a negative finding of considerable importance for editorial decision-making: it indicates that Tenduc and Sichintingiu cannot be merged on a map. Furthermore, the connection to Nestorian Christian communities in northwest China, including areas like Kashgar and Yarkant, reinforces the idea that Tenduc was a significant Mongol administrative or cultural region rather than a single urban center [[31](https://www.academia.edu/121739986/Marco_Polos_Description_on_Nestorians_and_Other_Christian_Groups_in_Yuan_China)].

In light of the lack of a clear consensus and the distinct treatment of Tenduc and Sichintingiu, placing definitive points for either on a map would be speculative. The best course of action is to recommend a regional label for Tenduc, acknowledging its probable nature as a territorial designation. For both Tenduc and Sichintingiu, a low-confidence regional label on a gazetteer or map would be appropriate. This approach reflects the current state of scholarship, which understands these names as administrative or ethnic designations rather than precise geographical markers for single cities.

## Competing Interpretations of the "Dry Tree"

This section provides a deep analytical dissection of the "Dry Tree" (Albaro Solo or Arbre Sec) as described by Marco Polo. The analysis is structured to untangle four distinct questions: the manuscript reading and meaning of the name; the botanical species implied by Polo's description; the potential connection to the Zoroastrian Cypress of Zoroaster; and the contested physical location of the tree. The focus is on synthesizing the arguments of key scholars such as A. Houtum-Schindler, Paul Pelliot, and Angelo Michele Piemontese to identify areas of agreement and disagreement, and to distinguish between a literary-symbolic interpretation and an attempt to identify a physical landmark.

The textual foundation for the "Dry Tree" is found in Polo's narrative, where he mentions passing by it in the province of Tonocain (or Khorasan) [[42](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.31953/2015.31953.A-New-Account-Of-East-India-And-Persia-Being-Nine-Years-Travels-1672-1681--Vol-2_djvu.txt), [43](https://archive.org/stream/newaccountofeast02john/newaccountofeast02john_djvu.txt)]. The name itself is subject to philological debate. Henry Yule, in his critical edition, notes that the manuscript reads "Arbre Sol" or "Arbre Sec" and suggests that "Solque" might be a corruption of "Seule," meaning "solitary" [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. This implies the tree was known colloquially as the "Solitary Tree." The variation between "Sol" and "Sec" (dry) may reflect textual transmission errors or simply two descriptive epithets applied to the same phenomenon [[41](https://archive.org/stream/landsofeasternca00lest/landsofeasternca00lest_djvu.txt)]. This minor textual issue does not significantly alter the core mystery of what the tree represented.

The botanical identity of the "Dry Tree" is a matter of intense scholarly dispute, with several prominent theories emerging from different intellectual traditions. A. Houtum-Schindler, in his 1909 article, advanced the theory that the "Arbre Sol" was not just any tree, but the sacred Cypress of Zoroaster, a historically significant tree said to be 1450 years old at the time of his writing [[41](https://archive.org/stream/landsofeasternca00lest/landsofeasternca00lest_djvu.txt)]. This interpretation roots the tree firmly in a pre-Islamic, Zoroastrian tradition, connecting it to a specific mythological and historical lineage. However, this is a symbolic identification rather than a botanical one.

In contrast, Paul Pelliot, a master of oriental philology, approached the problem from a different angle. He argued that the "Dry Tree" legend was not original to Polo but was preserved through the widespread tradition of the Alexander Romance [[13](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. Specifically, he linked it to the Oracular Trees of the Sun and Moon from the Pseudo-Callisthenes narrative, which were said to prophesy Alexander's death [[18](https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/6350/1/CDaniotti_Dissertation_volume%201.pdf), [24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. This perspective frames the "Dry Tree" as a piece of legendary folklore that Rustichello da Pisa, a romance writer, would have readily incorporated into his narrative [[26](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rustichello_da_Pisa), [28](https://librivox.org/author/5527)]. Pelliot's argument emphasizes the literary provenance of the tale over its botanical or historical veracity.

More recently, Angelo Michele Piemontese (1997) offered a concrete botanical identification, proposing that the "Dry Tree" was the šul, scientifically known as *Cydonia indica*, a type of Indian quince [[13](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/), [47](https://www.scribd.com/document/457361488/Marco-Polo-and-His-Travels-Secondary-Source-Compilation)]. This theory attempts to ground the legend in a specific, identifiable plant species. Other scholars have favored different trees. Marsden and Cordier identified it as the Oriental Plane, or Chinar, a large and majestic tree common in the region [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. An anonymous source also suggests it was a giant plane-tree [[40](https://archive.org/download/persiaitspeople00syke_0/persiaitspeople00syke_0.pdf)]. The debate thus ranges from symbolic/mythological interpretations (Zoroastrian cypress) to literary origins (Alexander Romance) to specific botanical candidates (Indian quince, Chinar plane).

A crucial aspect of the "Dry Tree" legend is its conflation with Christian hagiography. Multiple medieval European texts, including the works of John Mandeville and Rustichello himself, connect the "Dry Tree" motif to the Oak of Abraham at Hebron [[20](https://dokumen.pub/the-bloomsbury-reader-in-christian-muslim-relations-6001500-9781350214101-9781350214095-9781350214132-9781350214125.html), [25](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37386/37386-h/37386-h.htm)]. According to this Christian tradition, the oak was lush and green until the crucifixion of Christ, at which point it dried up and will only turn green again when a lord from the West conquers the Holy Land and sings Mass beneath its branches, leading to the conversion of many Saracens and Jews [[20](https://dokumen.pub/the-bloomsbury-reader-in-christian-muslim-relations-6001500-9781350214101-9781350214095-9781350214132-9781350214125.html), [25](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37386/37386-h/37386-h.htm)]. This powerful eschatological symbol, combined with the Alexandrian tradition, appears to have been blended by Polo or Rustichello into a single, composite narrative [[25](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37386/37386-h/37386-h.htm)]. The connection is so strong that some scholars argue referencing Alexander provides the same sense of grandeur to the text as invoking this Christian prophecy [[19](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/context/gc_etds/article/7137/viewcontent/Donaldson_Diss_The_Truth_and_Other_Fictions.pdf)].

Finally, the physical location of the "Dry Tree" remains speculative. Sources suggest it was in the province of Tonocain or Khorasan, near the cities of Bostam or Damghan [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. Yule speculates that its mention might be connected to the headquarters of Ghazan Khan and the famous battle tradition between Alexander and Darius, further embedding it in a landscape rich with heroic and mythical associations [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. The "Dry Tree" is described by Polo as a sort of landmark or milestone at the end of a great desert, functioning more as a geographical marker than a natural wonder in its own right [[44](https://dhamma-library.cdn.winmetta.org/13.%20Rear-Buddhist-Books-1/Maco%20Polo%27s%20Travels%20%28Edt.%20E.%20Rhys%29.pdf)]. Given the weight of the evidence, the most defensible conclusion is that the "Dry Tree" is not primarily a record of a specific, verifiable botanical specimen but rather a literary construct. It is a composite symbol built from pre-existing legends—both Christian and Alexandrian—that were popular in the Europe of Rustichello's time. The botanical debates, while interesting, may be secondary to its function as a narrative device, a moment of wonder and symbolism inserted into the geographical account. A confident identification of its species or a precise physical location is not supported by the available evidence.

## Witness Matrix Analysis: The Death of the Caliph and the Nizari Garden

This section presents a source-critical analysis of two of Marco Polo's most famous and controversial narratives: the death of the Abbasid Caliph al-Mustaʿṣim and the legend of the Nizari Ismaili "Garden of Paradise." The analysis is structured into two witness matrices designed to visualize the transmission of these stories, highlighting areas of agreement and divergence among primary and secondary sources. The purpose is to provide a clear framework for understanding the reliability and provenance of these accounts, thereby informing editorial choices regarding their presentation in a historical retelling.

**Matrix 1: The Death of the Last Abbasid Caliph al-Mustaʿṣim**

Marco Polo's account of the caliph's death is a dramatic narrative of starvation, confined in a tower filled with gold and silver [[10](https://www.jstor.org/stable/27036670), [24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. This version, however, stands in stark contrast to nearly all other contemporary and near-contemporary sources, which attribute his execution to other methods. The creation of this matrix reveals a fundamental schism in the historical record, suggesting that Polo's story was not based on direct observation but was adapted from popular European literary and religious tropes.

The table below summarizes the key elements of the death narrative according to various witnesses. *Analysis of Matrix 1:* The data clearly show that Polo's narrative is an outlier. Historians like Nassima Neggaz note that Polo's account was likely crafted for his Christian audience, serving as a moral lesson about the vanity of worldly riches [[10](https://www.jstor.org/stable/27036670)]. The story of the starving caliph fits perfectly into the European tradition of hagiographical tales, such as the miracle of Muqaṭṭam Mountain, where a Coptic patriarch challenges Christians to move a mountain with their faith. Polo adapts this motif, transplanting it from Cairo to Baghdad and changing the protagonist from a saint to a Muslim ruler, creating a narrative of divine retribution [[20](https://dokumen.pub/the-bloomsbury-reader-in-christian-muslim-relations-6001500-9781350214101-9781350214095-9781350214132-9781350214125.html)]. The details of starvation and confinement appear to be later embellishments that gained traction in European chronicles, whereas the most reliable sources, including Rashid al-Din and various Arabic historians, attest to a swift execution. The method of rolling the caliph in a carpet and trampling him was a common practice among the Mongols to avoid shedding royal blood, making it a more historically plausible account than starvation [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm), [36](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/south-asian-history-biographies/hulagu-khan)]. The authorship and dating of the Persian account attributed to Ṭūsī are disputed, but its content aligns with the mainstream historical consensus rather than Polo's version [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. Therefore, for an editorial retelling, Polo's account should be presented not as a factual report but as a legendary narrative with clear literary antecedents.

**Matrix 2: Nizari "Garden" Motifs**

The second narrative, concerning the Old Man of the Mountain and his paradise garden, is arguably even more iconic. This story, detailing how young followers were drugged and brought to a paradisiacal garden to ensure their blind obedience, has become synonymous with the Nizari Ismailis in the Western imagination [[9](https://www.originalsources.com/Document.aspx?DocID=H33XFITJ8WW2GEP)]. The witness matrix for this story reveals that the entire narrative, as told by Polo, is a singular construction. While the existence of the Nizari state centered at Alamut is a well-documented historical fact, the specific details of the garden and the use of hashish are considered by modern scholarship to be a "fanciful history" [[15](https://www.academia.edu/107327957/An_Old_Man_a_Garden_and_an_Assembly_of_Assassins_Legends_and_Realities_of_the_Nizari_Ismaili_Muslims), [16](https://www.academia.edu/figures/7893851/figure-1-marco-polo-in-the-prison-of-genoa-dictating-his)].

Some secondary sources note a parallel between Polo's account and that of the Franciscan friar Oderico da Pordenone, suggesting this anecdote was circulating in late-medieval Europe [[13](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/polo-marco/)]. However, this does not establish a direct evidentiary chain back to the Nizaris themselves. Instead, it points to a persistent rumor or legend that was adopted and elaborated upon by writers like Rustichello. Modern scholarship, drawing on Nizari sources where they survive, portrays them not as a murderous cult but as a sophisticated community engaged in philosophical and diplomatic activities, exemplified by figures like Nasir al-Din Tusi [[15](https://www.academia.edu/107327957/An_Old_Man_a_Garden_and_an_Assembly_of_Assassins_Legends_and_Realities_of_the_Nizari_Ismaili_Muslims), [38](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/fall-of-alamut/)]. The Mongol destruction of the Alamut library in 1256 severely curtailed the Nizari historical record, leaving a vacuum that was filled by sensationalist tales propagated by their enemies and later travelers [[15](https://www.academia.edu/107327957/An_Old_Man_a_Garden_and_an_Assembly_of_Assassins_Legends_and_Realities_of_the_Nizari_Ismaili_Muslims)]. Therefore, an editorial retelling should treat the garden story as a powerful literary invention by Polo and Rustichello, a product of medieval European perceptions and stereotypes, rather than a factual account of Nizari practices.

## Chronology of the Mongol Conquest of Alamut and the Pamir Epithet

This final section addresses two distinct chronological and terminological queries arising from Marco Polo's narrative. First, it resolves the apparent discrepancy in the reported dates of the surrender of the Nizari stronghold of Alamut and its leader, Rokn al-Din Khurshah. Second, it investigates the earliest securely dated usage of the epithet 'Roof of the World' for the Pamir Mountains, distinguishing between travel diaries, manuscript compositions, and published works.

The confusion regarding the fall of the Alamut state stems from conflicting reports in authoritative sources. Farhad Daftary's *Encyclopaedia Iranica* biography of Rokn al-Din states that the surrender at Maymundiz occurred on November 19, 1256, while the surrender of Alamut itself followed in December of the same year [[38](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/fall-of-alamut/)]. In contrast, the *Iranica* entry on Hulāgu Khan assigns the November date to the fall of Alamut [[35](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hulagu-khan/)]. This creates a chronological anomaly that needs to be clarified. A review of external historical records, particularly those of the Mongol campaign, resolves this discrepancy. The Mongol invasion of the Nizari fortresses was a systematic operation led by Hulagu Khan, who departed Mongolia in autumn 1253 [[36](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/south-asian-history-biographies/hulagu-khan)]. The decisive events unfolded as follows: Rukn al-Din Khurshah, the Ismaʿili leader, surrendered to Hulagu at Maymun-Diz in the third week of November 1256 [[38](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/fall-of-alamut/)]. The main fortress of Alamut itself fell to the Mongols in December 1256, effectively ending the Nizari state's independence after more than 160 years [[38](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/fall-of-alamut/)]. Rukn al-Din was subsequently sent to Möngke Khan in Mongolia, where he was executed en route in 1257 [[35](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hulagu-khan/), [38](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/fall-of-alamut/)]. Girdkuh, the last Nizari fortress, held out until December 1270 [[38](https://www.learnpersianonline.com/iran/history/mongols-and-ilkhanate/fall-of-alamut/)].

Marco Polo's account, written decades later around 1298, contains a chronological error. He states that Alaü besieged the castle for three years starting in 1252 [[9](https://www.originalsources.com/Document.aspx?DocID=H33XFITJ8WW2GEP)]. This date is incorrect. The discrepancy likely arises from a simple misreporting of the timeline. Polo may have confused the start of the Mongol military operations in 1253 with the final surrender in 1256, or he may have conflated events from the beginning of the campaign with its conclusion [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. The manuscript variants GT/Pauthier offer alternative dates like 1242 or 1262, but none correct the fundamental error [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. The historical record is clear: the capture of Maymundiz happened in November 1256, and the fall of Alamut occurred shortly thereafter in December 1256. Therefore, the initial discrepancy in the *Iranica* sources can be explained by consulting a more detailed chronological study of the Mongol conquests, which clarifies that the surrender at Maymundiz preceded the fall of the main fortress of Alamut. For an editorial retelling, it is more accurate to follow the timeline established by historians like Daftary and Møller, noting that Polo's date of 1252 is an error.

The second query concerns the origin of the epithet "Roof of the World" for the Pamir Mountains. The phrase is famously associated with Lieutenant John Wood, who is recorded as having used the term *Bām-i-Dunyah* upon reaching the Pamir plateau in 1838 [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. From this mid-19th-century British exploration, the English translation "Roof of the World" gained widespread currency globally [[45](https://bigthink.com/strange-maps/roof-of-the-world-map/)]. However, the user's request is to find the earliest *securely dated* usage of this specific epithet. The provided sources indicate that the concept was native to the region long before its adoption by Europeans. The mountain people themselves had been calling their homeland *Bām-i-Dunya* ("Roof of the World") for ages [[45](https://bigthink.com/strange-maps/roof-of-the-world-map/)]. The etymology of the word "Pamir" itself has been linked to the Persian phrase "pay-I-mehr," which also means "roof of the world" [[34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamir_Mountains)].

Despite the antiquity of the concept and the local term, the search for the earliest dated usage of the *specific phrase* "Roof of the World" in a verifiable primary source yields no results before the 19th century. The provided materials state that the earliest securely dated usage of the epithet is not found in the primary sources listed before the 19th century [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. The renowned 7th-century Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang described the region as being "midway between heaven and earth," but he did not use the exact phrase [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm)]. Early Islamic geographers referred to the region as a 'Highland or mountain pass', not "Roof of the World" [[21](https://wordorigin.app/etymology/pamir)]. Although T.E. Gordon's book, reviewed by Yule in 1876, uses the term, this is still a step removed from the initial usage [[46](https://www.battle-of-qurman.com.cn/literature/Middleton-2013-1.pdf)]. Therefore, the evidence leads to a "not found" verdict for any earlier example. The earliest verifiable usage appears to be tied to the 19th-century explorations of figures like John Wood, whose writings and those of his contemporaries cemented the phrase in the global lexicon, even if it was translating a much older local term [[24](https://readingroo.ms/1/0/6/3/10636/10636-h/10636-h.htm), [45](https://bigthink.com/strange-maps/roof-of-the-world-map/)].

## List of Unresolved Questions

While this research has resolved several key source-critical questions, the following matters remain uncertain or unresolved based on the provided sources. These points represent areas where further investigation, potentially involving direct access to primary manuscripts or new archaeological discoveries, may be required.

1.  **Precise Datum and Boundary for Old Hormuz:** Although the general location of Old Hormuz is confirmed to be southwest of modern Minab, the provided sources do not specify a standard geographic datum (e.g., WGS84) or allow for the precise demarcation of the ancient urban boundary versus the port area. A definitive, modern GIS coordinate with high precision cannot be supplied.
2.  **Definitive Site for Pein/Pem:** Despite confirming the general region east of Khotan, no post-Pelliot evidence conclusively identifies whether the site is Uzun-tatïr or Keriya. The debate remains open, and the exact location of Pein is therefore not definitively settled.
3.  **Single Definitive Candidate for Tenduc:** The evidence indicates Tenduc was likely a Mongol administrative region rather than a single city. The sources do not provide enough information to propose a single, defensible map point for it.
4.  **Botanical Identity of the Dry Tree:** The scholarly debate over the "Dry Tree's" botanical species (cypress, plane, quince) remains unresolved. There is insufficient evidence to definitively favor one theory over the others, and it may be a literary construct rather than a specific plant.
5.  **Origin of the Hashish Motif in the Nizari Story:** While the entire "garden" story is considered a literary invention, the specific motif of using a drug (hashish) for indoctrination is not found in any source prior to Polo. The ultimate origin of this particular embellishment within the vast network of medieval European rumors remains unclear.
6.  **Exact Mechanism of Chronological Error in the Alamut Narrative:** While it is established that Marco Polo's date of 1252 is incorrect, the precise reason for the error—whether it was a misunderstanding of the siege timeline, confusion with other events, or inaccurate information relayed to him—is not definitively stated in the provided sources. The explanation is inferred rather than explicitly documented.
