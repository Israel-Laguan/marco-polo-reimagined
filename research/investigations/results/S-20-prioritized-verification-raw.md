Marco Polo Reception, Attributions, Cartography, and Manuscript Evidence: Verification ReportAcross the six research areas investigated, direct bibliographic catalogues, official state speech transcripts, institutional repository records, and open-access scholarly extracts confirm key anchors while exposing significant documentary gaps behind widely repeated claims. In East Asian and Middle Eastern reception, bibliographic extracts verify Zhang Xinglang's 1929 and 1936 Chinese translations and Feng Chengjun's 1936 annotated translation, institutional and journal records document 2019 and 2024 (NEW) Mongolian editions of Ramusio's 1559 text published in Ulaanbaatar, a user-curated Goodreads entry reports a 2017 (NEW) Persian translation, and Chinese President Xi Jinping explicitly invoked Marco Polo in official 23 September 2023 (NEW) and May 2024 (NEW) speech texts—whereas his 14 May 2017 Belt and Road Forum keynote does not mention Polo in the accessible text. In screen, infrastructure, and myth history, Giuliano Montaldo's Marco Polo (1982) appears in film scholarship (NEW) as a six-partner international television co-production predating the formal bilateral Italy–China co-production treaty by decades, La Nuova Venezia (2018, NEW) reconciles Venice Tessera Airport's 1960 runway paving and 31 July 1961 terminal inauguration while leaving its formal naming date unresolved, Olga Orlić (2014, NEW) classifies Korčulan birthplace claims as a modern "invented tradition", and Giordano da Pisa's 23 February 1306 Florentine sermon—relayed via Carlo Delcorno's critical edition and Vincent Ilardi's translation—supports an Italian origin of spectacles around 1286 ([Second-hand]), strongly qualifying modern claims linking eyeglasses to Polo's travels.In cartography, marginalia, and manuscript codicology, Henry Vignaud's 1901–1920 total-forgery critique of the 1474 Toscanelli correspondence contrasts with Clement[s] Markham's defense and archival links—including Toscanelli and Fernão Martins jointly witnessing Cardinal Nicholas of Cusa's will on 6 August 1464 and the 1860 Seville discovery of a Latin copy in Columbus's hand. Institutional catalogues also confirm 2,125 total postils across all of Columbus's surviving books, a specific Marco Polo postil on fol. 57 ("WAS this island"), L. A. Vigneras's 1956 Simancas discovery of John Day's letter, watermark-based backdating of the Foligno Biblioteca Jacobilli manuscript, and BnF fr. 2810's Gallica resolution (ark:/12148/btv1b10021503v).At the same time, strict separation of the six inspection tiers reveals hard access limits across several core items. No direct access was available to Victoria Carlsson's 1930 article beyond a truncated OCR header, to Teresa Shawcross's December 2023 article (NEW) beyond its bibliographic metadata, to Piero Falchetta's 2006 Fra Mauro critical edition and CD-ROM map image beyond publisher catalogues, or to the primary texts of Ramusio (1559), the October 1929 Macaroni Journal pasta story, Peruško on Pasini (1847), and Ravenstein on Behaim's globe. Accordingly, this report flags every unverified page, hand count, shelfmark, translation base, and pre-modern attribution as unresolved rather than smoothing over documentary limits.1. Chinese, Mongolian, and Iranian ReceptionA post-2016 Firenze University Press historiographical chapter (NEW, author and exact year absent from the accessible extract, Tier 5) documents the formative twentieth-century Chinese reception of Marco Polo. Preceded by Wei Yi's 1913 translation (Yuandai keqing Mage Boluo youji, Beijing: Zhengmeng shuju) and Ding Qian's geographical studies (1915, 1917–1918), Zhang Xinglang (张星烺) published Zhang yi Mage Boluo youji (张译马哥孛罗游记, Zhang's Translation of Travels of Marco Polo) in Peiping with the Yenching University Library in 1929, followed by Mage Boluo youji (马哥孛罗游记, Travels of Marco Polo) in Shanghai with the Commercial Press in 1936 (Tier 5). A separate truncated extract from a loanwords monograph mentions Zhang Xinglang immediately before "First published in 1930 and later expanded and", which cannot be securely attached to his Polo translation rather than another work. In the same year, Feng Chengjun (冯承钧, 1887–1946, dates from the secondary loanwords extract) published his translated and annotated Make Boluo xingji (马可波罗行纪, Travels of Marco Polo) in Shanghai with the Commercial Press (1936), later issued with additional annotations by Dang Baohai (Shijiazhuang: Hebei renmin chubanshe, 1999). A 19 April 2024 Sixth Tone feature by Haiyun Wu (NEW, Tier 3) likewise highlights Feng Chengjun's translation alongside Peking historian Rong Xinjiang's thirteen-year effort to produce a definitive Chinese critical edition. Crucial caveat: neither the Firenze University Press bibliography extract nor Sixth Tone identifies the European translation bases used by Zhang or Feng, leaving their source editions not found. For Yang Zhijiu (杨志玖), the consulted bibliography displays only the collected volume title Yang Zhijiu wenji: Make Boluo yu zhongwai guanxi (杨志玖文集: 马可波罗与中外关系, Yang Zhijiu's Collected Works: Marco Polo and Sino-Foreign Relations, Tier 5); his individual publication dates and historical arguments remain inaccessible (Tier 6).For Mongolian reception, institutional records and academic journals document two Ulaanbaatar publications derived from Giovanni Battista Ramusio's 1559 Venetian version (NEW). The 2019 imprint—cited in Études mongoles et sibériennes, centrasiatiques et tibétaines (EMSCAT), Acta Mongolica (2019), and Ca' Foscari's Marco Polo bibliography—is Marko Pologiin ayalal: 1559 onii Ramuziogiin xuvilbar (Ulaanbaatar: Soyombo), crediting Ganzorig Bat-Uchral, Elisabetta Ragagnin, and Samuela Simion as editors ((eds), Tier 4/5). Five years later, Ca' Foscari University of Venice's institutional repository (ARCA card 10278/5103467 / IRIS handle 10278/5103467, Tier 2) catalogues a 2024 critical text edition (Edizione critica di testi) authored by Elisabetta Ragagnin, Bat-Uchral Ganzorig, Raima Auyeskhan, and Samuela Simion titled Marko Pologiin ayalal: Venetsiin Ramuziogiin 1559 onii huvilbar / The travels of Marco Polo: Ramusio's Venetian version (1559) (Ulaanbaatar: Soyombo, pp. 1–182, ISBN 9789919521622). An earlier 2018 overview by Ragagnin and Simion (NEW, pp. 135–144, Tier 3/5) reports Ca' Foscari's digital critical edition of Ramusio's Dei viaggi di messer Marco Polo, coordinated by Eugenio Burgio, Marina Buzzoni, and Antonella Ghersetti. However, because the 2024 IRIS abstract breaks off after four words and neither full Soyombo volume was accessible, the editorial relationship between the 2019 and 2024 books (whether revised reissue or distinct edition) and the exact division of translation responsibility among Bat-Uchral, Ragagnin, Simion, and Auyeskhan remain unresolved provenance (Tier 6).Concrete Iranian and Persianate reception is attested in four modern contexts, whereas no pre-modern or nineteenth-century Persian translation of Polo was located. First, a Goodreads catalogue entry (NEW, Tier 3/5) records a 1 April 2017 Persian hardcover translation, سفرنامه مارکوپولو (Safarnameh-ye Marco Polo, subtitled ایل میلیونه یا کتاب مارکوپولو, Il Milione ya Ketab-e Marco Polo, 320 pp.), naming translators آنجلادی جوانی رومانو (transliterated as Anjelā dī Jovānī Romānū) and سید منصور سجادی (Seyyed Mansour Sajjadi); because Goodreads is a user-edited social database, bibliographic authority remains moderate. Second, an undated (in readable plain text) SSRN paper (abstract=3674354, Tier 5, largely obscured by font-encoding corruption) titled "Marco Polo in Persia" argues in its legible opening lines that Polo's account of the Old Man of the Mountain may refer to Ghal'eh Kuh of Ferdows rather than Alamut Castle in Qazvin. Third, the Encyclopædia Iranica entry "POLO, MARCO" (Tier 3/5) analyzes Polo's Persianate vocabulary and topography (including Darband-e Āhanin, Belor/Balor, and Angelo Michele Piemontese's 1997 identification of the Dry/Lone Tree with šul), complemented by a truncated extract noting Polo's reliance on Persian along the Silk Road. Fourth, contemporary Iranian cultural-tourism writing (TapPersia blog, Tier 3) narrates Polo's travels across Hormuz, Kerman (qanats), Yazd, Khorasan, Saveh, and Tabriz. By contrast, an Iranian Studies article on an 1844 Qajar Safarnameh-ye Bokhara invokes Polo only as a modern scholarly comparison, and Rekhta's Marco Polo Ka Safarnama is Urdu rather than Persian.In Chinese diplomacy and scholarship, official PRC releases verify direct presidential invocation of Marco Polo, while no PRC state school textbook teaching Polo was found. In his full English remarks released by Xinhua/Qiushi on 23 September 2023 (NEW, Tier 1/3) at the welcoming banquet of the 19th Asian Games Hangzhou, President Xi Jinping stated: "For centuries, Hangzhou has been known for its breathtaking natural beauty, rich endowment and a thriving cultural life. Marco Polo described it as 'the City of Heaven, the finest and the noblest in the world.'" A May 2024 PRC Ministry of Foreign Affairs transcript extract (NEW, Tier 5, URL directory 202405/t20240530) likewise records Xi citing "Great adventurers, including Du Huan of China, Marco Polo of Italy and...", and a 1 April 2019 State Council Information Office page (NEW, Tier 5) links to an Italian tour of the Chinese opera Marco Polo. Crucially, Xi Jinping's 14 May 2017 Belt and Road Forum keynote address does not mention Marco Polo in the accessible text, demonstrating that Polo references cannot be inferred from generic Belt and Road speeches. On the scholarly side, Rong Xinjiang's April 2024 interview (NEW, Tier 3) defends Polo's historicity using Venetian archival contracts and argues that Polo's absence from official Yuan chronicles reflects his status as an ortoq merchant rather than a court official. Meanwhile, "Marco Polo" at Xi'an International School is merely the institutional name of its Chinese-language program (Tier 3), and Education About Asia discusses teaching Frances Wood's 1995 book in English classrooms (Tier 3), leaving a dated PRC school textbook not found.2. Literature, Screen, and Venice Airport ChronologyBeyond Dalibor Bukal's 2006 Milion, Libar Marka Pola, Italo Calvino's 1972 Le città invisibili, and Xue Yiwei's 2012 Traveling with Marco Polo (established in prior context), no additional recent novel centered on Marco Polo appeared in the consulted corpus. Bibliographic mentions of screen works such as La fabuleuse aventure de Marco Polo (1965) and Ma-ko Po-lo / The Four Assassins (1975) represent earlier films, and other entries such as Pinocchio in Mongolia (2024) are unrelated.For Giuliano Montaldo's Marco Polo (1982), recent scholarship (NEW) resolves the apparent contradiction over its status as the "first Italian–Chinese co-production" by distinguishing commercial television collaboration from formal intergovernmental treaties. A September 2014 article in the Journal of Italian Cinema and Media Studies (vol. 2, no. 3, author absent from ResearchGate extract, Tier 5) titles Montaldo's series "the first Italian and Chinese film co-production", a framing repeated in a 2023 book chapter ("Marco Polo Returns to China: Giuliano Montaldo's TV Series (1982)", pp. 99–116, Tier 5) and a University of Milan AIR abstract (Tier 5). The 2014 bibliography specifies the six-entity production consortium—Vides Cinematografica, RAI, NBC, Procter & Gamble Productions, Tokyo Broadcasting System (TBS), and CCCC (China)—while co-writer and producer Vincenzo Labella's Television Academy biography states the eight-episode production was "the first Western production to film in the Forbidden City and Ming Tombs in China" (Tier 3). However, Lepri (2025) (Cinematic Encounters: A Perspective on Sino-Italian Film Co-production, Sapienza IRIS, p. 14, NEW, Tier 5) characterizes Montaldo (1982) as a "turning point in the history of China-Italy cinematic contacts" while noting that the formal bilateral Agreement on Film Co-production between Italy and the PRC was signed decades later (the exact day and ratification years are damaged in OCR as "December 4, 2 4" and "late 2 12 and earl...", so those digits cannot be asserted without clean inspection). No specific broadcast dates by country beyond RAI 1 (1982–83) were located (not found), although an anonymous La Repubblica report of 19 January 1985 (p. 23), "Ai cinesi piace 'Marco Polo' di Montaldo", provides an indirect terminus ante quem for Chinese reception.For Venice Marco Polo Airport (Tessera), regional press evidence reconciles the competing 1960 (Wikidata, Mapy) and 1961 (travel portals) opening claims while leaving the formal naming date unresolved. A 27 December 2018 historical retrospective in La Nuova Venezia (NEW, Tier 3/4) reports that the foundation stone was laid on 29 March 1958 in the presence of Patriarch Angelo Roncalli, the runways were paved in 1960 (aligning with the official 1 August 1960 air-traffic decree established in prior context), and the passenger terminal (aerostazione) was formally inaugurated on 31 July 1961, with the first Jumbo arriving in 1974. Neither Gruppo SAVE's corporate website (veneziaairport.it) nor any ENAC or municipal document in the consulted sources gives the date of formal naming (intitolazione) after Marco Polo, leaving that specific milestone not found.3. Attributions, Portraits, Pool Game, Korčula, and MilioneNo evidence for an authentic contemporary (lifetime) portrait of Marco Polo appeared in the consulted corpus. All verifiable depictions are posthumous manuscript or early modern illustrations, notably the 1375 Catalan Atlas (depicting a caravan, cited in Sixth Tone 2024, Tier 3), the fifteenth-century Livre des merveilles du monde (BnF, cited in Europeana 2014, Tier 3), and an unspecified fifteenth-century departure miniature. A ResearchGate PDF entry mentioning a miniature "believed to date between 1298–1299" appears to be an unreferenced Wikipedia scrape lacking any manuscript shelfmark or provenance and cannot substantiate a contemporary likeness.For the swimming pool game "Marco Polo", the earliest located textual attestation—relayed second-hand via Wikipedia (Tier 3/4)—is the 1965–1970 Dictionary of American Regional English (DARE) survey, in which respondents named "Marco Polo" as an American water game, followed by its reported spread to expatriate pools in Hong Kong by the mid-1970s. Informal Reddit recollections claiming early-1960s or 1970s childhood play lack documentary reliability (Tier 3 forum). Wikipedia and commercial pool blogs agree there is no authentic historical connection between the game and the medieval merchant, and four independent, uncorroborated naming stories circulate in modern commentary: the whimsical gag that Polo "didn't have a clue as to where he was going" (Wikipedia, Pinch A Penny, Reddit); a storm-at-sea story claiming Polo fell overboard or passengers called "Marco!" / "Polo!" in fog while escorting the Mongol princess to Persia (Millennium Pool blog, student blog, and Logan DeLoye in Mental Floss, 25 August 2026, NEW); a desert-lost variant in China (Millennium Pool blog); and an anachronistic claim that the game "originated aboard a ship in the 12th century"—prior to Polo's c. 1254 birth (Pinch A Penny, 25 March 2022, NEW).On Korčula birthplace claims, Dalmatian family origin, and "million lies", Olga Orlić's paper "The curious case of Marco Polo from Korčula: An example of invented tradition" (uploaded 24 July 2014, NEW, Tier 3/5) treats contemporary Korčulan identity claims—invoking the local Depolo surname, a "house of birth" tower, and annual naval reenactments—as a modern "invented tradition". Orlić notes that the one secure historical event linking Polo to the island is his capture near Korčula during a Venetian–Genoese naval battle, citing Foretić (1940, p. 70) ([Second-hand / Tier 4]; note that Foretić's battle year is not printed in Orlić's extract, while a commercial shop site dates the Battle of Curzola to 1298 and other popular summaries state 1296). While commercial and user-edited sites repeat Korčula birth or ancestry claims, Wikipedia summarizes the scholarly consensus favoring birth c. 1254 in Venice (exact date and birthplace archivally unattested) and rejecting Korčula or Constantinople birthplace theories. Neither Peruško's discussion of Pasini (1847) on possible Dalmatian family origin nor the earliest located Korčula birthplace attribution appeared in any consulted source (Tier 6), and no source located the earliest print occurrence of the exact phrase "million lies", though Wikiquote (Tier 3/4) notes late-medieval readers dismissing the text as "a book of fables".Secondary summaries of competing Milione etymologies contain factual errors that must be corrected against institutional records. Wikipedia (Tier 3/4) relays both the wealth/exaggeration explanation (Messer Marco Milioni from recounting Kublai Khan's millions) and Luigi Foscolo Benedetto's derivation from Emilione (arguing that because Marco's father Niccolò also bore the name Milione, it served as a branch surname distinguishing them from other Venetian Polo families). However, Wikipedia misdates Ramusio as a "15th-century humanist" and Benedetto as a "19th-century philologist": Ca' Foscari institutional records confirm Ramusio's edition appeared posthumously in 1559 (16th century), and Benedetto's text is a twentieth-century edition (translated into English by Aldo Ricci; see also the 1928 citation in Encyclopædia Iranica). A brief extract from Yule and Cordier (1903, Tier 5) adds that explanations of Milione began "with his contemporary Fra Jacopo d'Acqui". As for the deathbed saying ("I have not told half of what I saw"), multiple secondary sources—Wikiquote citing Dent (1926, p. xxiv) and Vito Bianchi (2009), History Today, and an Archive.org edition (Tier 3/4)—attribute it to Dominican friar Jacopo d'Acqui's Imago mundi seu Chronica (c. 1330), whose Tartar passages draw largely on the LB Latin text according to a 2024 Transcript abstract (NEW, Tier 5). No account of the deathbed saying earlier than Jacopo d'Acqui was located (not found). Throughout, the historical artifact or event (the Devisement text; the Korčula naval battle; the San Marco Qingbai jar's 1932 Raphael proposal noted in context; Venice's airport) remains distinct from its later naming and legend.4. Food, Technology, and Early Optical ChronologyFor Victoria Carlsson (1930), the Semantic Scholar index preserves only a short OCR fragment: "1930. s On Recent Research In Cookery And Allied Subjects II. Ice Cream And Freezing VICTORIA CARLSSON Instructor in Household Arts, Teachers College HISTORY" (Tier 5). Neither the journal title, page numbers, nor any passage mentioning water and milk ices or Marco Polo is accessible (Tier 6), leaving the alleged attribution unverified (not found). Similarly, on the pasta legend, a food-history blog (The Ambrosian Pantry, Tier 5) relays that "One of the best-known versions of the legend is a story called 'A Saga of Cathay' in the October 1929 issue of the Macaroni Journal, the trade magazine of the...". That blog confirms the October 1929 title and venue second-hand, but neither the 1929 Macaroni Journal article text, Ramusio's 1559 passage, nor any named historian analyzing both strands is accessible (Tier 6).Across gunpowder, paper money, and eyeglasses, separating technological history from the history of Polo claims yields sharp evidentiary contrasts. Gunpowder is completely absent from the consulted corpus (not found), with neither a Polo textual claim nor a modern attribution located. By contrast, Marco Polo's Devisement genuinely described paper money (Yuan legal-tender banknotes), a fact noted in modern editorial prose (, Tier 3/4) and Hans Ulrich Vogel's 2013 study Marco Polo Was in China (, Tier 5), whereas the modern diffusion claim that "Marco Polo brought back the idea of paper money" to Europe appears in Beth Daley's 9 January 2014 Europeana Foundation blog post (NEW, Tier 3), with no earlier source for the European-import claim located here.Daley's 2014 Europeana post (NEW, Tier 3) is likewise the sole consulted source attributing European eyeglasses to Polo's report: "some think his descriptions of coal, eyeglasses and a complex postal system eventually led to their widespread use in Europe". Against that popular narrative, secondary scholarship on medieval optics—relayed via History Medieval (citing Carlo Delcorno's critical edition of Giordano da Pisa's Quaresimale fiorentino and quoting Vincent Ilardi's translation in Renaissance Vision: From Spectacles to Telescopes, Tier 3/4), Superscout (summarizing Edward Rosen 1956 and Ilardi, Tier 3/4), and Medium (noting manuscripts at the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Tier 3/4)—documents that on 23 February 1306 at Santa Maria Novella in Florence, Dominican preacher Giordano da Pisa (Giordano da Rivalto) declared that the art of making spectacles had been discovered less than twenty years earlier, adding: “I saw the one who first discovered and practiced it, and I talked to him.” Placed beside 1300–1301 Venetian commercial regulations on spectacles and the Santa Caterina convent chronicle recording that Pisan friar Alessandro della Spina (d. 1313) independently reproduced and shared the craft, Rosen and Ilardi place the invention of riveted convex spectacles in northern Italy around 1286 ([Second-hand / Tier 4]). Popular blogs contain minor discrepancies—one cites "1305" without explanation and a school page mislabels the sermon a "1306 letter" mentioning Alessandro—and the implication for Polo remains explicitly qualified: History Medieval notes that Giordano's "less than twenty years" phrasing points to "sometime after about 1286, probably during the later 1280s or early 1290s", and emphasizes that the sermon neither names the inventor nor specifies Pisa. Even with those qualifications, an Italian invention in the late 1280s or early 1290s followed by Venetian guild regulation by 1300–1301 precedes or coincides so closely with Polo's 1295 return that it strongly undercuts claims of Chinese introduction via Polo, while Daley's qualified phrasing claims only influence on "widespread use".5. Marginalia, Columbian Sources, and CartographyOn Columbus's surviving annotated Latin copy of Marco Polo (Pipino's version) in the Biblioteca Capitular y Colombina in Seville, HathiTrust catalogues Juan Gil's critical edition (El libro de Marco Polo (ejemplar anotado por Cristóbal Colón y que se conserva en la Biblioteca Capitular y Colombina de Sevilla), presentation by Francisco Morales Padrón, Tier 2), and Reading Columbus (2019 reprint, NEW, Tier 5) confirms archival access to Columbian postils in Seville. Across all of Columbus's surviving books combined, Columbus and the Ends of the Earth (UC Press, Tier 5) reports a total of 2,125 postils (printed as 2,125.9. with attached footnote superscript 9). Within Columbus's Marco Polo specifically, a Scribd extract of Samuel Eliot Morison's Admiral of the Ocean Sea locates one postil on fol. 57 ("WAS this island"), and The History of Cartography (vol. 3, pt. 1, ch. 7, Tier 5) mentions a scholarly disagreement over whether a geographic point appears "only in a postil added by Columbus". However, specific postil counts within Marco Polo alone, paleographic separation of hands (Columbus versus his circle), and internal dating evidence are inaccessible (Tier 6).For Teresa Shawcross's article "Materiality and Marginalia across the World: The Role of Things in Christopher Columbus's Annotations on Marco Polo", The Medieval History Journal 26(2) (December 2023): 376–424, DOI: 10.1177/09719458231213480 (NEW), Semantic Scholar, ResearchGate, and Princeton History verify the bibliographic citation (Tier 5), but the article body is inaccessible (Tier 6). Consequently, p. 380 n. 12, p. 377, fol. 66v, any reference to Vasco da Gama, the contradictory shelfmarks, and any 1499 / 1500 / 1501 dating argument could not be inspected or verified through any independent scholarly account, and no "1499–1501 annotation window" can be inferred. Similarly, for John Day's 1497/98 letter, a Memorial University of Newfoundland PDF confirms L. A. Vigneras's 1956 discovery of the letter at Simancas (Tier 5), but the scholarly arguments and dissent equating Day's promised book with Columbus's surviving Seville copy were not found (Tier 6).The 1474 Toscanelli correspondence, by contrast, is substantively documented across institutional catalogues and secondary accounts, clearly separating the 1474 letter to Fernão Martins from the later cover letters to Columbus. Henry Vignaud (1830–1922) led the wholesale forgery case in La lettre et la carte de Toscanelli (1901), Toscanelli and Columbus (London: Sands & Co., 1902, 365 pp.), and The Columbian Tradition... (1920, addressed to opponents Hermann Wagner and Carlo Errera) (Tier 2). As summarized in The Open Court (c. 1902, Tier 3/4), Vignaud contended that Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli never wrote the 1474 Martins letter, never drew the western-route chart, never corresponded with Columbus, and that both the Martins text and the Columbus letters preserved by Fernando Columbus and Bartolomé de las Casas were forgeries hiding the real inspiration behind Columbus's voyage. On the defending side, Ricardo Olvera (Fidelio 1(2), Spring 1992, Tier 3/4) relays Clement[s] Markham's counterargument ("[f]ew documents of this period are so well certified") alongside two material/archival anchors: the 1860 discovery in Seville's Columbus Library of a Latin transcript of the Martins letter in Columbus's own hand on the flyleaf/frontispiece of Pope Pius II's Historia rerum ubique gestarum, and archival proof linking Toscanelli, Cardinal Nicholas of Cusa, and Fernão Martins—both Toscanelli and Martins witnessed Cusa's will on 6 August 1464, and Martins's relative Bishop Antonio Martins of Oporto came from Chaves near Roriz. Distinguishing the 1474 Martins text (transcribed onto Columbus's Pius II flyleaf) from the two cover letters purportedly written directly to Columbus (transmitted via Fernando Columbus and Las Casas) clarifies why later scholars separate total rejection from targeted doubt over the Columbus letters alone.The remaining pre-Columbian and early modern cartographic questions hit firm access limits (Tier 6). For the Catalan Atlas (1375), consulted sources note only its caravan illumination of Polo and a Turkish article title ("Katalan Atlası'nın Yazıları"), leaving the absence of a Chipangu label versus Japan-like islands and specific textual links unlocated (not found). For Fra Mauro's World Map, Brepols, Google Books, and Open Library catalogue Piero Falchetta, Fra Mauro's World Map: With a Commentary and Translations of the Inscriptions (Turnhout & Venice: Brepols / Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, 2006, Terrarum orbis 5, ISBN 978-2-503-51726-1, 829/820 pp. + CD-ROM with high-resolution image, Tier 2); because the book and CD-ROM image are inaccessible (Tier 6), Cimpangu/Cimpagu (#1334), Hachbaluch/Hacbaluch (*2306), competing source hypotheses, and royal ownership versus commission debates cannot be resolved here. For Martin Behaim's globe, an Archive.org OCR extract notes that "John Maundevile... is acknowledged by Martin Behaim alongside that of Polo" (Tier 5), whereas E. G. Ravenstein's Cipangu transcription and page reference were not found (Tier 6).6.
