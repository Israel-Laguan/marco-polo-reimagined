## Part 1 — the two seed works

### 1a. Tenmaku no Jaadugar (天幕のジャードゥーガル)

**What it is.** Manga by Tomato Soup (トマトスープ), on Akita Shoten's web magazine *Souffle*, 2021– (**NEW**). The author's own summary [read, TRANSIT]: set in early-13th-century Mongolia; the heroine is a girl born in what is now Iran, a slave in a scholar's household, taken to the Mongol court when her town falls. She is modelled on the historical Fāṭima, confidante of Töregene. Period: late Chinggis, Ögedei (r. 1229–41), Töregene's regency (1241–46)‡. That is one to two generations **before** Polo.

**Anime.** Exists. TV series, Science SARU; chief director Yamada Naoko, director Abel Gongora, character design Yoshida Kenichi; TV Asahi "IMAnimation" slot from 4 July 2026 [search]. **Historical supervisors: Tanigawa Haruna (谷川春菜) and Masuya Tomoko (桝屋友子)** [read, Animate Times]. Tanigawa is a Mongol-empire historian (Souffle bills her as a researcher at the National University of Mongolia and lecturer in Tokyo) [read]. Masuya is an Islamic-art historian‡. The staff did a location hunt in Mongolia covering gers, horse culture, food and dress [search]. Tomato Soup says the anime rebuilt buildings and characters "from scratch" in three dimensions, because the manga "fudged many things for the sake of composition" [read]. So for object geometry (ger interiors, architecture seen from several angles) the **anime's setting art is the better reference** than the manga panels.

**Creator's stated sources** [read, Souffle column note 1, in Tomato Soup's own words]:

| Use | Work she names |
|---|---|
| Overall chronology (first book opened) | d'Ohsson, 『モンゴル帝国史』, tr. Saguchi Tōru, Heibonsha |
| Checking d'Ohsson point by point | Sugiyama Masaaki 『モンゴル帝国の興亡』 (Kōdansha); Honda Minobu 『モンゴル時代史研究』 (Univ. of Tokyo Press); Shimo Hirotoshi 『モンゴル帝国史研究』 (Univ. of Tokyo Press); Miya Noriko 『モンゴル時代の「知」の東西』 (Nagoya Univ. Press) |
| **Fine detail of customs** | **Carpini and Rubruck**, 『中央アジア・蒙古旅行記』, tr. Mori Masao, Kōfūsha |

Method [read]: start from library books, follow each bibliography "like a relay"; when stuck, search the web only to find a paper or book, then CiNii Research and NDL Search; accept a detail only if it does not contradict the core books. She admits the bar is lower than a scholar's because the result is fiction. Travel [read, TRANSIT]: a solo trip as a student (Ulaanbaatar, Karakorum ruins, December); a research trip with her editor in May (museums in Ulaanbaatar and Karakorum, and the reputed site of Ögedei's seasonal palace near Karakorum). She had the anime supervisors correct chronology and cultural errors in the manga itself and advises buying the **latest printing** [read].

**For this book.** The usable chain is: her customs detail → Rubruck and Carpini → which the book can cite directly. The companion column 『もっと！天幕のジャードゥーガル』 by Tanigawa (Souffle, bi-monthly) explains each motif with scholarship and is the fastest route to object-level sources; I read only one instalment.

**Accuracy for c. 1260–1295: mixed.** Right: felt gers and the ordo, sheep slaughter without spilling blood (she witnessed it as a child in Inner Mongolia [read]), court women's political role, the multi-ethnic court. Limits: the drawing style is deliberately simplified; the court is pre-Yuan (no Dadu, no Chinese-style palace, less Chinese silk tailoring than under Kublai).

### 1b. Otoyomegatari (乙嫁語り, A Bride's Story)

**What it is.** Manga by Kaoru Mori (森薫), 2008–, set in mid-19th-century Central Asia [read, 47NEWS]. It depicts‡: embroidery and dowry textiles, carpet and felt work, yurt framing and furnishing, carved-wood house interiors, tandoor bread and communal cooking, falconry with eagles, mounted archery and horse care, sheep herding, bride-price and wedding ritual, Aral-shore fishing villages, and a Persian urban household with its bathhouse.

**Documented sources** [read, 47NEWS interview by Kunieda Nana, Kyodo, 2026]:

- Early reading: **Gustav Krist**, 『ソ領トルキスタン潜入記』 (travel in Soviet Turkestan, 1920s); **Eleanor Lattimore**, 『トルキスタンの再会』 (*Turkestan Reunion*).
- **Biggest single influence:** the 1999 Suntory Museum of Art exhibition 『シルクロードのかざり 中央アジアとコーカサスの美術』. She later visited "the Moscow museum that holds the exhibits" (the interview does not name it; the exhibition catalogue will).
- Fieldwork: the **Russian Museum of Ethnography, St Petersburg**; towns "where old culture survives" in Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. The interviewer met her at the Boysun folk festival in Surxondaryo, Uzbekistan.
- No consultant. She says she chose to do all the research herself, and that many patterns on vessels and clothes "are not in any book".
- Earlier profile pieces say the work rests on research rather than travel [search]; the travel is therefore later than the series' start.

**Stable 13th → 19th century (usable for content, with care)‡:** felt-making; the trellis-and-roof-pole tent in principle; tandoor flatbread; eagle and falcon hunting from horseback (Polo describes Kublai's eagles); mounted archery; herd management; hospitality and bride-wealth customs; the hammam as an institution; reed screens, felts and piled bedding as furniture.

**Clearly later — do not carry over‡:** firearms of every kind; the Russian presence and the Great Game frame; samovars, tea service and porcelain teapots as everyday ware; silk **ikat** robes and large **suzani** embroideries (the forms shown are 18th–19th century); 19th-century Turkmen and Karakalpak silver-and-carnelian jewellery sets; the horsehair face veil and enveloping town robe; quilted striped khalat cuts; tobacco and water pipes; New World foods (tomato, potato, maize, chilli); glazed windows, pocket watches, cameras. Also note the tents: Mori draws the **Turkic bent-pole yurt**; Rubruck's Mongol tents had a projecting felt "neck" at the smoke hole and were often moved whole on carts.
