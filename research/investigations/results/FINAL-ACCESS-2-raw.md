## 2. Corrections the notes force on the draft

**Identifications and pages**

- **Rockhill 1915, p. 623 (page image)** prints "KAN-MAI-LI (甘埋里). Comoro Islands (?)." A Ganmaili = Hormuz equation needs another authority ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.56082)).
- **Rubruck p. 55 must be dropped.** The page image shows Mongol cart-borne houses ("so large that they are sometimes thirty feet in width") and no kumis. The kumis passages are pp. 62–63, 66–67 and 85 ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/journeyofwilliam00will)).
- **The *bocheta* is "'gioiello d'oro e di gemme', dal lat. bŭcca"**, not a *boghta* headdress ([Formentin and Schiavon 2024 PDF](https://www.rmoa.unina.it/7054/1/RM_1-2024_Formentin-Schiavon.pdf)).
- ***Yuan dianzhang* hunting-ban date: 20th, not 28th.** The 捕獵 summary on the page image reads 「正月為頭至七月廿日除[鷙?]禽猛獸外，但是[含?]獸胎孕卵之類，不得打捕」. The numeral is 廿, not 廿八. Square brackets with a query mark are the reader's uncertain readings, and the character after 除 was flagged as unclear. This rests on the summary table; the full article 禁治打捕月日 was not read ([shidianguji](https://www.shidianguji.com/book/CADAL02052896/chapter/1l1thuhsovtpe)).
- ***Nancun chuogeng lu*: the clubs and the giants are separate entries.** The text does not pair them; the draft's pairing is inference.
  - The 骨朶 are carried by the 雲都赤, close attendants.
  - The giants are the 鎮殿將軍, called 大漢.
  - Juan 1 has no 玉斧. The axe of 蒼水玉 is the separate 劈正斧 entry in Siku juan 5, found in the first pass.
- ***Tashrīf al-ayyām*: the envoy's death is at the top of p. 172** of the Cairo 1961 edition (the sentence runs from p. 171). The section runs pp. 170–173.
- **Hammer 1842 was published in Darmstadt** (title page image), not Pesth.

**BnF Arabe 5847**

- **Date.** BnF metadata dates the manuscript to **634 AH / 1236–1237**, copied "par le peintre lui-même", on 167 folios. The brief's 1237 and the caption's 1236 both fall within that range; 635 is not supported.
- **The "caravan" folios are not caravans:**
  - **f. 10r:** three seated men under a tree beside a couched camel.
  - **f. 19r:** a mounted procession with banners, long trumpets and drums. It is horsemen, not camels, and could be called a "caravan/escort" only loosely.
  - **f. 101r:** a herd of about nine camels with a herdsman.
- **The ship (f. 119v) and the village (f. 138r) are confirmed.** The village's Maqama number was not verified.

**de Rachewiltz**

- **hdl 1885/41883 is the 2002 working paper, not ZAS 27 (1997).** Its n.1 refers readers to the ZAS review article for detail. Viatica n.2 says that article was "développé ensuite dans un article en ligne". It cannot supply ZAS page numbers.

- Bellela's death by 1326–27 rests only on Bolognari 2020.

**Item 16 (Gadrat-Ouerfelli, *Viatica* 13, 2026; CC BY 4.0).** Source: [OpenEdition](https://journals.openedition.org/viatica/5918). The article's conclusions, in paraphrase:
- **§4:** Pipino's anecdote (the uncle "Matheus" affirming the book's truth to his confessor) and Jacopo d'Acqui's ("non scripsi medietatem") were told to affirm veracity. Reading them as contemporary doubt is "un contresens".
- **§§8–9:** Ramusio's 1559 preface (the banquet and unpicked seams, and "Milione" as millions) later hurt Polo's credibility. The nickname was borne by several Polos and is unrelated to the book (citing Benedetto 1930, Gallo 1955, Larner 1999).
- **§10:** Purchas and Horn blamed errors on corrupt Latin editions.
- **§§11–14:** Jesuits (Ricci, Martini 1655, Magalhães 1688) confirmed that Cathay was China while criticising Polo's use of Mongol and Persian names. n.33 says he probably knew some Chinese (Atwood 2016).
- **§18:** John Green (Astley's *New General Collection* IV, 1747, pp. 582–583) was "il me semble être le premier" to note the absence of the Great Wall.
- **§§19–20:** Wood (1995) adds absences to the older arguments: the Wall, tea, footbinding, script, porcelain and cormorant fishing. She also notes that no Chinese source names Polo and that his account of the Xiangyang siege is an anachronism.
- **§§22–23:** Wood's arguments rest on a long historiographical tradition focused on China. Sinologists (Haw 2006, Vogel 2013) did most to refute them, and Polo studies have been renewed since the 2000s.

- **(a) Great Wall (reply to Wood's points 4–5).** "the fact is that the Wall, as we know it, did not exist in Marco's time." He adds: "As shown by A.N. Waldron, the magnificent Wall we see today is the fortification built or re-built by the Ming government".
  - He argues that the pre-Ming walls were discontinuous rammed-earth ramparts. No 13th-century Chinese source mentions the Wall as a material reality, and it first appears in the 1579 *Guang yu tu* (n.2: Fuchs 1946).

- **(b) Yangzhou.** The governorship claim "is an exaggeration to say the least". He also cites the Genoese Yilioni family at Yangzhou (n.3, R. S. Lopez).
- **(c) Gazetteer.** He states Wood's argument as point 7 ("no gazetteer of Yang-chou mentions him"). His reply does not address gazetteers directly: by denying the governorship, it implies that none would be expected. Point 8 answers that Chinese sources do not name the Polos because their Chinese or Mongol names are unknown and Mongols used nicknames.
