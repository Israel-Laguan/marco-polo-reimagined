## 1. Li Tan (II-10)

**1a. Year. Contradicted.**
- Source: Pelliot, *Notes* II, "Liitan sangon", pp. 762–763 (pages confirmed by the vol. 3 index; scans 166–167) [OCR page].
- Li Tan revolted in the 2nd moon of 1262, marched from Yidu and took Jinan from its hereditary governor Zhang Hong.
- Pelliot judges the manuscripts ten years out, the revolt falling about thirteen years before Polo reached China.
- Pelliot cites the biography in Yuan shi 206. I saw only the opening of that chapter (to c. 1260–61) [extract]; the revolt passage and the annals (j. 5) were NOT SEEN.
- Limitation: "ten years out" implies a manuscript reading of 1272. Check which reading your retelling's 1273 follows.

**1b. Commanders and Li Tan's end. Supported, via Pelliot.**
- Qubilai ordered Aju against Jinan; a prince Pelliot tentatively restores as Qabichi held nominal command; the minister Shi Tianze came to direct the siege.
- Li Tan tried to drown himself in a lake, was pulled out, and was executed at Shi Tianze's request on 6 August 1262, with his fellow rebel Nangiadai.
- Not in what I saw: the name of the lake (Daming) and the length of the siege. Revolt to execution spans roughly February/March to 6 August 1262. That is the whole episode, not the siege.
- Amnesty: NOT FOUND in anything I read.

**1c. "Aguil". Supported as Aju, not "Aqul".**
- Yuan shi 128, Aju's biography [extract]: 「中統三年,從諸王拜出、帖哥征李璮有功」, "In Zhongtong 3 [1262] he followed the princes Baichu and Tiege against Li Tan and won merit."
- Pelliot reads the form as "Agiul" and rejects the older identification with a prince Agul, father of Nayan.
- He identifies him as the son of Uriyangqadai, with dates 1227–1280 (I, p. 15, inferred; scan 31) [OCR page].
- Limitation: the biography says he campaigned against Li Tan. His presence at Jinan itself rests on Pelliot's reading of the annals (YS 5, 2a), NOT SEEN.

**1d. "Mongotay". Disputed, unresolved.**
- Charignon's "Nangiadai" fails: the Nangiadai of the Li Tan texts was a Mongol officer executed with the rebel.
- Pelliot prefers a Tatar Manghutai (d. 1290), but that man's biographies do not connect him with the campaign. Pelliot calls his own solution very hypothetical (II, p. 783, inferred; scan 187) [OCR page].

**Cautious wording:** "The revolt belongs to 1262, not 1273; Li Tan was executed at Jinan on 6 August 1262. 'Aguil' is the general Aju, whose Yuan shi biography records service against Li Tan. 'Mongotay' has not been securely identified (Pelliot)."

## 2. Negüder and the Qaraunas (I-07)

**2a. Polo's genealogy and date. Not supported.**
- Source: Pelliot I, "Caraunas", p. 195 (header seen; scan 211) [extract].
- Pelliot found no mention of the Qaraunas before 1270 and no nephew of Chagatai named Negüder; he places Polo's account after Hülegü's arrival in Persia.
- On p. 193 (inferred) he argues from Armenian transcriptions that one candidate's name was Tegüder, not Negüder [extract]. The context was NOT SEEN.

**2b. "Dalivar". Disputed.**
- Source: Pelliot II, "Dilivar", p. 626 (inferred; scan 30) [extract].
- Marsden explained it as "of Lahore"; Yule agreed on finding that the Qaraunas had plundered Lahore.
- Pelliot suspects the readings "Delidinar" (VB) and "Dely" (R) point to Delhi.
- Lahore was sacked in 1241, a generation before Polo's "Negodar".

**2c. "Asedin Soldan". Partly supported.**
- The same entry glosses the name as Ghiyath al-Din, sultan of Delhi.
- "Balban" specifically was not in the text I saw. Pelliot's "Asidin soldan" entry was NOT SEEN.

**2d. Aubin and Jackson. NOT SEEN; metadata only.**
- Aubin: *Turcica* 1 (1969), 65–94 (citation confirmed second-hand through a *Modern Asian Studies* footnote).
- Jackson 1999: relevant pages 119–22, 217–27 and 328 by the same footnote; Appendix III, "Qara'unas and Negüderis", is at p. 328 per the table of contents.
- A Turkish review of Jackson (Dergipark) says the Negüderis took their name from Negüder, a commander of the Jochid line. That is a reviewer's summary, not Jackson's text.
- Rashid al-Din: NOT SEEN.

**Cautious wording:** "Mongol bands called Qaraunas or Negüderis are attested in Afghanistan and raiding toward India in the later 13th century, but Polo's 'nephew of Chagatai' is not confirmed, and 'Dalivar' may conceal Lahore or Delhi."

## 3. Möngke's funeral (I-12)

**3a. Killings on a khan's funeral road. Parallel attested for Chinggis, second-hand.**
- Yule's own note 4 cites Rashid al-Din: the escort carrying Chinggis to burial slew all whom they met, and forty girls were sent to serve him.
- A 2014 blog post quotes Thackston's translation to the same effect: the party carrying the coffin killed every creature met on the way back to the ordus. The intermediary is the blog; Thackston was NOT SEEN, and no page is given.
- A 19th-century *Indian Antiquary* article reports Rashid's account of Ögödei sending forty richly dressed girls to serve Chinggis in the other world.

**3b. Möngke specifically. NOT FOUND.**
- I found no Rashid al-Din or Yuan shi passage reporting such killings for Möngke, and no source for the 20,000 figure outside Polo. I did not read either source's account of his burial.

**3c. Modern studies. NOT SEEN.**
- Elverskog, Atwood and de Rachewiltz were not opened.
- Leads from a footnote in a Szeged *Chronica* article: Pelliot, *Notes* I, 305–330; D. C. Wright (1997) on Chinggis's death in Mongolian, Chinese, Persian and European sources; Drobyshev (2006) on Mongol funeral ritual.
- The same blog calls the Rashid passage legendary; that is opinion.

**Classification on this evidence:** a contemporary Persian report about Chinggis (attested, second-hand here); Polo's transfer to Möngke with a number (unparalleled in what I found); "European topos" neither shown nor excluded.

**Cautious wording:** "Rashid al-Din tells a similar story of Chinggis Khan's cortège; no independent source has been found applying it to Möngke or giving a figure."

## 4. Kaidu, Nomoghan and George (II-15)

**4a. Nomoghan's capture. Supported.**
- Yuan shi 12, under the first month of Zhiyuan 19 (1282) [extract]: 「時皇子北平王以軍鎮阿里麻里之地,以禦海都。諸王昔里吉與脫脫木兒…撒里蠻等謀劫皇子北平王以叛…海都不從」.
- Translation: "The imperial prince, Prince of Beiping, was garrisoning Almaliq against Qaidu. The princes Shiregi, Toq-Temür … and Sarban plotted to seize him and rebel … Qaidu did not go along."
- Pelliot II, "Nomogan", p. 796 (inferred; scan 200) [extract]: the princes raided the camp by night and took Nomoghan, Kökchü and Antong.
- Per Rashid, Nomoghan and Kökchü went to the Golden Horde and Antong to Qaidu; the two princes reached Beijing on 26 March 1284.
- Pelliot dates the capture 1276–77 and says Qaidu was no party to the plot (I, p. 128, header seen).

**4b. George with Nomoghan. Contradicted by Pelliot's reading.**
- Source: Pelliot II, "Giorge", p. 737 (header seen) [extract].
- George of the Önggüd was killed in 1298 fighting Qaidu and Du'a, after Polo had left, and no text connects him with Nomoghan.
- His father Ai-buqa fought Sarban, one of the captors, after 1277.
- Pelliot suggests Polo put George in his father's place.

**4c. Which battle, and is the date defensible? Not resolved.**
- I did not check the year your edition prints, and I found no source for a drawn battle between Qaidu and Nomoghan near Karakorum.
- The nearest attested events are the 1276–77 mutiny and the fighting that followed on the Orkhon.

**Cautious wording:** "Nomoghan was seized by mutinous princes in 1276–77 at Almaliq, not defeated by Kaidu; George is first attested in these wars later, and Polo may have confused him with his father Ai-buqa (Pelliot)."

## 6. Chagatai and the Samarkand church (I-10)

**6a. The hanging pillar. Independent witness, reported by Pelliot.**
- Source: Pelliot II, "Marsarchis", p. 775 (header seen) [extract].
- The same legend occurs in the inscription Liang Xiang composed for one of Mar Sargis's churches at Zhenjiang; Pelliot thinks Polo probably had the story from Mar Sargis.
- Mar Sargis's family came from Samarkand.
- Pelliot's references: Moule 1930, p. 146; *T'oung Pao* 1915, p. 632. The Chinese text (*Zhishun Zhenjiang zhi*) and Moule were NOT SEEN.
- Limitation: if the informant was the same man, the witness is independent of Polo's book but not of his source.

**6b. A church of St John. Partly supported, as interpretation.**
- A Cambridge Core article on Christian architecture in Sogdiana (full citation not captured) reports that James Ryan doubts the account, noting a suspiciously similar report of Eljigidei (1327–30) building a church of St John the Baptist at Samarkand.
- It also says church remains noted in a Registan excavation report could be Polo's church. That is conjecture, not physical proof.
- Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Christianity iii": a Nestorian metropolitan see of Samarkand existed, its foundation date uncertain, and a Latin bishop was appointed in 1329.
- Yule himself held that Marco evidently never was at Samarkand.

**6c. Chagatai's conversion. NOT FOUND.**
- No Syriac, Persian or mission source in what I read supports it. Hunter and Dickens on this point were NOT SEEN.

**Cautious wording:** "The pillar legend also appears in a Chinese inscription of 1281 for a church founded by a Samarkand Christian, so Polo did not invent it; Chagatai's baptism is unconfirmed."
