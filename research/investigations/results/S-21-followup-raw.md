# Marco Polo Critical Source Audit — Working Reference for the Retelling

**Edition under retelling:** Henry Yule (trans./ed.), revised by Henri Cordier, *The Book of Ser Marco Polo*, 3rd ed. (London: John Murray, 1903), with Cordier's *Notes and Addenda* (vol. III, 1920). All locators below are to this edition unless stated otherwise.

**Method note on project IDs.** Per the project's own method, the project ID tags (e.g. "III-04," "I-11") attached to findings below are **routing hints only** — pointers to where a claim sits in the draft's chapter scheme — and are **not independent proof of a claim's geography, date, or accuracy**. Treat them as a filing index, nothing more.

**What this report is and is not.** This is a synthesis of nine independent research passes ("Group 1" through "Group 9" notes files), each already structured around separating (i) the Book's own claim, (ii) a modern identification or correction, (iii) what modern evidence can and cannot establish, and (iv) whether a claim is eyewitness, reported, or editorial inference. This report reproduces those distinctions; it does not re-research anything, upgrade any researcher's stated confidence, or resolve any gap the original notes left open. Where a notes file flagged a detail as paraphrase-only, secondhand, or unverified, that caveat is preserved verbatim in substance here. Two works — A.C. Moule & Paul Pelliot, *Marco Polo: The Description of the World* (London: Routledge, 1938), and Pelliot's *Notes on Marco Polo* (Paris: Imprimerie Nationale/Librairie Adrien-Maisonneuve, 1959–1973) — recur across **every one of the nine groups** as a source the researchers wanted but could not access. This is the single biggest cross-cutting gap in the whole audit and is flagged once here rather than repeated in every row.

No single accuracy percentage is given anywhere in this report, by design.

## B. Findings by group

### Group 1 — Sumatra and the Andamans

#### B.1.1 Unicorn / rhinoceros (Basma, Book III, Ch. IX)
**Polo's claim:** A one-horned beast at Basma (Sumatra) — black thick horn, buffalo-like hair, elephant-like feet, boar-like head carried toward the ground, mud-loving, attacks with a prickle-covered tongue and pins victims with its knees. The text explicitly **rejects** the maiden-taming legend, calling the animal "the very opposite" of the European fable. No "I saw" framing was found in the material inspected — this is best read as **reported/compiled information**, not a confirmed personal-sighting claim (per the project's rule against inferring eyewitness status from accuracy).
**Modern finding:** No single uncontested species identification exists. Yule's own footnote assigns the two-horned *Rhinoceros sumatrensis* (now *Dicerorhinus sumatrensis*) as Sumatra's "characteristic" rhino; L. M. Talbot ("Marco Polo's Unicorn," *Natural History* 68(10), 1959, pp. 558–565 — full text inspected) treats the passage as "a composite of the Sumatran and Javan rhinos," crediting the hairiness to the Sumatran species but reading the one-horn/mud-wallow/boar-bite description as closer to the one-horned Indian/Javan type. Talbot reinterprets the "prickly tongue" weapon as a garbled account of the rhino's real weapon — two sharp lower incisors used boar-fashion — a **correction**, not a confirmation, of the stated mechanism.
**What the evidence can/cannot establish:** No zooarchaeological or documentary record dated 1270–1300 confirms rhinoceros presence in Sumatra specifically; Granger et al. (2026), *Current Biology* (full text inspected), gives documentary spans of 1835–2015 (Sumatran rhino) and 1630–2010 (Javan rhino) with no medieval Sumatra data point. The inference that a rhino was present in Sumatra in Polo's time rests on modern/19th-century distribution plus absence of evidence for an earlier range contraction — **explicitly inferential, not a dated record.**
**Verdict:** Reported, composite/garbled description; species ID unresolved between composite readings.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. II (Internet Archive OCR scan `bookofsermarcopo02polo`), full text of Talbot 1959 (via Rhino Resource Center PDF), Granger et al. 2026 (*Current Biology*, DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2026.02.001), Save the Rhino species page (secondary/NGO).
**Inspection level:** OCR full text (not page-image verified for short clauses — flagged); Talbot and Granger full PDF text.
**Confidence:** High on textual content; medium on species identification; low/inferential on 13th-century Sumatra occurrence.
**Chapters affected:** III-03/04.
**Unresolved / inference:** Exact wording of the maiden-legend rejection needs page-image re-verification; Moule & Pelliot's commentary on this passage not accessed (gap, not absence).

#### B.1.2 "Pygmies" (monkey-fabrication, Basma) and dog-headed Andamanese (Angamanain)
**Important correction carried through from the notes:** These are **two separate claims about two separate islands** (Basma/Sumatra vs. Angamanain/Andamans) in two separate chapters, and must not be merged into one "monstrous races of the region" claim.
**Pygmies (Basma, Bk III Ch. IX):** The Book's own text calls the claim "a lie and a cheat," stating the "pygmies" are small monkeys (man-faced) that are caught, shaved, stuffed, dyed with saffron, dried, and sold as fake pygmies; it states no genuine pygmy race of that size exists anywhere. **This is the Book debunking itself** — no modern zoological correction is needed; the "modern identification" is already Polo's own text.
**Dog-headed Andamanese (Angamanain, Bk III Ch. XIII):** The text asserts, without hedge or rejection, that the islanders "have heads like dogs, and teeth and eyes likewise," are idolaters "no better than wild beasts," and eat anyone they catch who is not of their own race. Yule's footnote calls the dog-head motif "at least as old as Ctesias" and cites parallel accounts (Ibn Battuta's dog-mouthed tribe, Friar Jordanus's dog-headed islanders, a Nicobar legend of canine female descent) as **parallel, independently attested occurrences**, not a demonstrated chain of borrowing into Polo.
**Deeper genealogy:** Dana Rehn, "Going to the Dogs..." *Otherness* 5(2) (2016), pp. 112–141 (full PDF inspected) traces the cynocephali motif from Ctesias (c. 400 BCE) through Pliny, the *Alexander Romance*, John of Plano Carpini (1247) and others — but Rehn's own attribution to Polo of "Tartar chiefs with dog's-head followers who ate their victims" (p. 119) **does not match** the Yule-Cordier Andaman passage and could not be corroborated; flag as a possible misattribution/conflation by Rehn, not usable as fact about Polo's text.
**Modern-history framing:** Uditi Sen, "Developing Terra Nullius..." *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 59(4) (2017), pp. 944–973 (DOI 10.1017/S0010417517000330, full text inspected) treats the animal-headed/cannibal motif explicitly as **myth** underpinning later colonial "terra nullius" dispossession of the Andamanese — useful for framing the dog-head claim as legendary stereotype, not ethnographic reality, though it does not itself cover the medieval period.
**Verdict:** Pygmy fraud — reported and self-debunked by the text. Dog-heads — reported at face value by the text, embedded in a well-attested but not traceably-transmitted ancient motif; no scholar located asserts a specific transmission chain into Polo.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier OCR (Internet Archive) + Digital Silk Road Project facsimile pp. 338–341, 363, 366; Rehn 2016 (full PDF); Sen 2017 (full PDF, Cambridge Core).
**Confidence:** High on textual content for both claims; low on any transmission chain (none found, not invented).
**Chapters affected:** III-03/04.
**Unresolved:** Moule & Pelliot (1938) and Pelliot's *Notes* not accessed; al-Idrisi's and Odoric of Pordenone's primary texts not accessed despite being flagged by Yule as relevant; Indigenous/Andamanese-centered scholarship on the motif not located.

### Group 2 — Spices and woods

#### B.2.1 Java's spice list (nutmeg, clove, pepper, cubeb, galangal, spikenard)
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. VI, "The Great Island of Java"):** Java (the Great) "produc[es]" black pepper, nutmegs, spikenard, galingale, cubebs, cloves, "and all other kinds of spices." Polo never landed on Java the Great; this is reported/hearsay information.
**Yule's own correction (in situ):** Note 1 states "Java does not produce nutmegs or cloves," but that Java "was a great mart for these" — i.e. an entrepôt explanation, supplied by Yule in 1903, not first caught by modern scholarship.
**Modern layer:** Nutmeg (*Myristica fragrans*) and clove (*Syzygium aromaticum*) are natively restricted to a handful of Moluccan islands (Banda for nutmeg; Ternate/Tidore/Makian/Moti/Bacan for clove) and were not cultivated in Java/Sumatra until Dutch-era transplantation. Pepper (*Piper nigrum*, native Malabar) had plausibly begun spreading into the archipelago by this date but the exact 1270–1300 cultivation extent is unclear. Cubeb (*Piper cubeba*) is genuinely native to Java/Sumatra — plausible as local. Galangal is botanically plausible for the region but not independently confirmed for Java at this date (gap). Spikenard (true *Nardostachys jatamansi*) is Himalayan and implausible for Java as stated — likely mistranslation, a different commodity, or error (no specific Yule note located on this term — gap).
**Verdict:** Mixed. Nutmeg/clove = corrected in the source itself (entrepôt-goods-as-local-produce error, flagged by Yule). Pepper/cubeb = plausible local goods. Galangal = plausible, unconfirmed. Spikenard = unresolved, likely wrong or mistranslated.
**Proposed Note:** "Yule's own 1903 footnote on this passage already objects: 'Java does not produce nutmegs or cloves' — those were Moluccan products Polo likely encountered as goods passing through Javanese ports, not local crops. Pepper and cubeb, by contrast, probably were grown in the Java/Sumatra region."
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 2 (gutenberg.org/ebooks/12410), offset ~750,000–850,000 (fetched/paraphrased, with short quoted fragments, e.g. "Java does not produce nutmegs or cloves"); general botanical range knowledge for *Myristica*, *Syzygium*, *Piper* spp. (not freshly re-pulled from Kew POWO this session — gap).
**Inspection level:** Fetched/paraphrased primary text with short direct quotes.
**Confidence:** High on the nutmeg/clove mismatch and Yule's footnote; medium on galangal/spikenard.
**Chapters affected:** III-04 (touches III-02/07).
**Unresolved:** Moule & Pelliot / Pelliot's *Notes* not accessed for this passage; exact Franco-Italian source-manuscript wording behind "spikenard"/"galingale" not checked.

#### B.2.2 Nicobar (Necuveran) cloves and red sandalwood
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. XII, "Necuveran"):** The islanders "live like beasts" without king or chief; produce listed includes "Red Sanders and Indian-nut and Cloves," plus Brazil and "sundry other good spices" (continuation not verified verbatim).
**Yule's own correction (in situ):** Note 1 identifies Necuveran = Nicobars, and quotes a 19th-century colonial source, "Colonel Man," directly disputing the claim: "I do not believe in cloves, but we have lots of the wild nutmeg." A second instance, in the same stretch of text, of Yule's own apparatus registering skepticism about an island-attributed clove claim.
**Modern layer:** The Nicobars are not and were not a clove-producing location (clove native only to a few North Moluccan islands). "Wild nutmeg" is a real local plant (a wild Myristicaceae relative, not true *Myristica fragrans*) plausibly mislabeled by a non-botanist informant. Red sandalwood (*Pterocarpus santalinus*) is an Indian mainland tree, not a plausible Nicobar native either — suggesting the whole Necuveran produce-list reads as a generic "valuable Indian Ocean goods" list rather than a botanically accurate local inventory (researcher's own inference, flagged as such).
**Verdict:** Disputed, with the dispute already present in Yule's own apparatus; best read as goods-of-the-wider-trade-region attributed to one stopping-point.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 2, Bk III Ch. XII, gutenberg.org/ebooks/12410, offset ~850,000–950,000 (fetched/paraphrased with short direct quotes).
**Confidence:** High that Yule's footnote disputes the clove claim; medium-high on the "generic trade-goods list" inference (explicitly an inference, not a cited scholarly claim).
**Chapters affected:** III-04.
**Unresolved:** No dedicated modern ethnobotanical source on Nicobar flora located this session.

#### B.2.3 Lambri "brazil" (sappanwood) and the Ceylon/Coilum parallel
**Correction to routing:** The Lambri "brazil" passage sits in the Sumatra-kingdoms cluster (III-03/04), not III-07. A **second, separate** brazilwood reference exists near Ceylon (Bk III Ch. XIV, footnote "Brazil Wood and Cinnamon") and at Coilum/Quilon (Ch. XXII, footnote "Brazil Wood: notes on the name") — confirmed to exist via the chapter synopsis but **not textually verified** in this research pass; these two references must not be conflated with the Lambri passage.
**Polo's claim (Lambri):** The kingdom has camphor and other spices; the people "sow the brazil, transplant it at a small-shoot stage, let it grow for three years, then uproot it." Polo's text further claims **he personally carried brazil seed back to Venice**, where it failed to grow (attributed to climate) — a first-person, experiential claim, distinct in evidentiary category from most spice claims in this group.
**Modern finding:** The only botanically possible identification is Old World sappanwood, ***Biancaea sappan*** (syn. *Caesalpinia sappan*), not the New World *Paubrasilia echinata* (unknown to Europe before the late 15th century). The English word "brazil" in Yule's translation is a period-authentic equivalence (the dye-wood term predates and gave its name to the country of Brazil, not the reverse), not a translation error.
**Verdict:** Confirmed (species identification), with inherited terminological confusion. The "grown for three years" husbandry detail is plausible on agronomic grounds but rests on Polo's/Yule's text alone — not independently modern-sourced.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 2, Lambri chapter, gutenberg.org/ebooks/12410, offset ~750,000–850,000; Oxford University Herbaria sappan/brazilwood distinction (project-established fact, not re-fetched this session).
**Confidence:** High on species ID and the Lambri cultivation claim's textual existence; medium on the 3-year-cycle detail; low/unresolved on the Ceylon/Coilum parallel passages (not fetched).
**Chapters affected:** III-03 (Lambri, confirmed); an unconfirmed separate chapter for the Ceylon/Coilum material.
**Unresolved — explicit gap requiring follow-up fetch:** Ceylon (Ch. XIV) and Coilum (Ch. XXII) brazilwood passages not retrieved verbatim.

#### B.2.4 Rhubarb of Sukchur/Jiuquan
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XLIII, "Of the Province of Sukchur"):** Rhubarb "is found in great abundance" in the province's mountains; merchants travel there to buy it and carry it "all over the world." A separate, adjacent claim: a poisonous mountain plant causes foreign livestock that eat it to lose their hooves (reported local travel-advice, not a claim Polo tested himself).
**Yule's footnotes:** Identifies the plant as *Rheum palmatum*, naming Siningfu and Kwan-Kian as chief markets, and states "the genuine root resists cultivation" — i.e. wild-harvested, not plantation-grown. The poisonous-plant identification (cf. *Andromeda ovalifolia*) is Yule's own 19th-century comparative-botany inference.
**Modern layer:** Sukchur/Sukchu = Suzhou/Suhchow, Gansu (modern Jiuquan), a real Hexi Corridor/Silk Road waystation; Gansu-Qinghai high mountains are within the real range of wild *Rheum palmatum* and related species used in the medieval overland "Chinese/Turkey/Russia rhubarb" drug trade. No dedicated post-2013 paper on medieval Gansu rhubarb trade was pulled this session (gap).
**Verdict:** Confirmed, with the nuance (from Yule, not the draft) that the rhubarb was wild-harvested and traded onward, not deliberately cultivated as a crop.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 1, Bk I Ch. XLIII, gutenberg.org/ebooks/10636, offset ~1,200,000–1,300,000 (paraphrase + short quoted fragment).
**Confidence:** High on identification and the wild-vs-cultivated nuance; medium on broader modern trade-history corroboration.
**Chapters affected:** I-11.

#### B.2.5 Ginger/cassia/cinnamon (Tibet, Ch. XLVI; Caindu, Ch. XLVII)
**Polo's claims:** Tibet — "cinnamon also grows there in great plenty," plus unspecified spices "never seen in our country." Caindu — "ginger and cinnamon in great plenty," plus a clove-producing tree with laurel-like leaves and small white flowers.
**Yule's corrections:** Tibet's "cinnamon" was probably coarser cassia, not true cinnamon (Note 3). Caindu's "cloves" were probably cassia buds (resembling cloves in shape), per medieval merchant-manual terminology (*fiori di canella*, Pegolotti/Uzzano); Yule explicitly rejects Pauthier's rival suggestion that the tree was tea, and notes a later traveler (Captain Gill) independently identified the tree as cassia using Polo's description as a guide. Yule candidly reports a source conflict on cassia's range at this altitude: Richthofen confirmed cassia in the Kien-ch'ang valley (equated with Caindu) at ~2,600 ft; Garnier's expedition found none there.
**Modern layer:** *Cinnamomum cassia* is genuinely native to and cultivated in southern China; true cinnamon (*C. verum*) is Sri Lankan/South Indian and not plausible for Tibet/Caindu at all. Ginger's broad East/South Asian range makes the Caindu ginger claim unremarkable.
**Verdict:** Corrected (cinnamon → cassia; cloves → cassia buds); ginger confirmed-with-editorial-caveat (unproblematic); the clove-like tree disputed/uncertain between cassia and Pauthier's rejected tea hypothesis.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 2, Bk II Ch. XLVI–XLVII, gutenberg.org/ebooks/12410, offset ~100,000–200,000 (paraphrase + short quoted fragments; footnote content here is secondhand-paraphrase, lower confidence on exact wording than other entries in this group).
**Confidence:** High on the cinnamon→cassia / cloves→cassia-buds correction; medium on exact footnote wording.
**Chapters affected:** II-07, II-12.
**Unresolved:** Ginger-specific footnote (Ch. XLIII, Acbalec Manzi) not fetched; no fresh modern botanical paper on the Richthofen-vs-Garnier conflict; current accepted *Cinnamomum cassia* vs. *C. aromaticum* synonymy not checked against Kew POWO.

#### B.2.6 Fansur/Barus camphor
**Polo's claim:** "In this kingdom of Fansur grows the best Camphor in the world called Canfora Fansuri," "so fine that it sells for its weight in fine gold."
**Yule's footnotes:** "The Camphor called Fansúrí is celebrated by Arab writers at least as old as the 9th century." Fantsur = Barus identified independently by 19th-century philologists (van der Tuuk; Gustaf Schlegel: "Fansur or Pantsur can be naught but Baros"). Yule explicitly calls the "weight in gold" claim **hyperbole**. (Minor internal tension flagged: Yule's "chief shipping-place for at least three centuries" line is not fully reconciled with his own 9th-century Arab citation.)
**Modern layer — strongest-evidenced claim in this group:** Barus (directly fetched, Wikipedia "Barus") is corroborated by a genuinely contemporaneous-to-near-contemporaneous chain: the Arab merchant Sulaiman (851 CE) described camphor plantations at Fansur; al-Masudi (10th c.) linked Fansur's yield to seismic/storm years; and — most importantly — the Chinese customs writer **Zhao Rukuo (Chau Ju-kua), writing ca. 1225**, listed "Binsu/Pansur" among camphor-producing countries, genuinely contemporaneous with (and slightly predating) Polo's own travels (1271–1295). The tree's current name is ***Dryobalanops aromatica*** (Yule's "*Dryabalanops Camphora*" is an obsolete spelling/synonym); species-level confirmation specifically at Barus (vs. a congener) was not independently re-verified.
**Verdict:** Confirmed, with Yule's own "hyperbole" flag retained rather than smoothed over.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 2, Fansur chapter, gutenberg.org/ebooks/12410, offset ~850,000–950,000; Wikipedia "Barus" (directly fetched and read).
**Confidence:** High on Fansur=Barus and the 1225 CE Zhao Rukuo corroboration; medium on the precise tree species.
**Chapters affected:** III-03.

#### B.2.7 Aloeswood (lignaloes), Champa
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. V):** Champa has "lignaloes in great abundance" and forests of "Bonús" wood (Yule identifies as ebony).
**Yule's footnote:** Champa is the chief lign-aloes/eagle-wood source; the name has "nothing to do with aloes or eagles" (etymological false-friend flag); fine eagle-wood is the product of disease in a tree — **but the fetched paraphrase's claim that Yule calls the tree "leguminous" could not be confirmed as Yule's actual wording** and is explicitly flagged as possibly a fetch-tool summarization error, not a fact. Agarwood is in reality produced by *Aquilaria* spp. (family Thymelaeaceae, not Leguminosae) — so if the "leguminous" detail is genuinely in Yule's footnote, it would itself be a notable period misattribution, but this is **unverified, not asserted.**
**Verdict:** Confirmed at the level of the trade-good and region (Champa as a premier historical agarwood source); the "leguminous" detail is explicitly unresolved and must not be published as fact pending re-verification of the primary footnote text.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 2, Bk III Ch. V, gutenberg.org/ebooks/12410, offset ~700,000–800,000 (paraphrase + quoted fragments — "leguminous tree" detail is paraphrase-only).
**Confidence:** High on Champa-as-agarwood-source; low/unresolved on the "leguminous" detail.
**Chapters affected:** III-02.

#### B.2.8 Dhofar frankincense
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. XXXVIII, "Dufar"):** "Much white incense is produced there"; trees "resemble small firs," are notched with a knife to release resin, sometimes exuding "without any notch," attributed by Polo to heat. Reads as a reported/informant-based production account.
**Yule's footnotes:** Cross-checks Polo against Ibn Battuta's independent 14th-century account of the same city and horse trade; a long discussion of frankincense/olibanum trade history; explains "white incense" as the milky color of fresh resin; no discussion of "red incense" located in the material accessed (gap).

The Wikipedia page fetched did not give period-specific (11th–14th c.) detail pinning the exact 1270–1300 trade state.
**Verdict:** Confirmed at the general level (major real production/export center; tapping description broadly matches real *Boswellia* harvesting); period-specific (1270–1300) archaeological pinpointing is an explicit, unresolved gap.
**Sources inspected:** Yule–Cordier Vol. 2, Ch. XXXVIII, gutenberg.org/ebooks/12410, offset ~1,200,000–1,300,000 (paraphrase, not verbatim — fetch tool could not reproduce full text); Wikipedia "Land of Frankincense" (directly fetched).

#### B.3.5 Shark-charmers (Gulf of Mannar pearl fishery)
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. XVI, "Maabar"):** Pearl-diving season runs "from the beginning of April till the middle of May"; divers pay "those men who charm the great fishes" (sharks) one-twentieth of their catch, with the charm holding "for that day only."
**Yule's footnote:** Cites Sir James Emerson Tennent's 19th-century account of an actual institutionalized shark-charmer role at the historical Arippu/Mannar fishery — a hereditary family role, Tamil name "Kaḍal-Kaṭṭi" ("Sea-binders"), paid by the colonial government, receiving "ten oysters from each boat daily." Yule flags that the practitioners "do not now seem to have any claim to be called Abraiaman or Brahmans," disputing Polo's own caste label for the charmers. Tennent notes "not more than one authenticated accident from sharks" occurred during a protected fishery season.
**Important efficacy caveat:** Neither Polo's text nor Yule's footnote constitutes evidence of literal charming efficacy. A low shark-accident rate during a season when charmers were employed is compatible with many non-magical explanations (shark behavior/seasonality, lookouts, netting) and **must not be reported as confirming the charm works.**
**Verdict:** The institution (hereditary shark-charmer role at this fishery) is real and independently documented, though only at a ~600-year remove (19th century via Tennent, cited secondhand through Yule, not independently read); this shows continuity of the *kind* of institution Polo describes, not a dated 13th-century confirmation. Polo's "Abraiaman"/Brahmin caste label for the charmers is itself disputed by Yule's own note.
**Sources inspected:** Gutenberg Vol. 2, Bk III Ch. XVI, offset ~900,000–1,000,000 (direct read with quotes).
**Confidence:** Medium-high on the institution; efficacy is explicitly not a factual claim this evidence can settle.
**Chapters affected:** III-05.

The two groups' notes are **consistent**, not conflicting, but were produced independently — see B.4.3 for the season-timing detail Group 3 did not cover in depth.

#### B.4.1 Baku / naphtha fountain
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. #### B.4.2 Nasij / gold textiles
**What "nasij" denotes:** Based on a review of Thomas Allsen's *Commodity and Exchange in the Mongol Empire* (1997) — **the review only, not the monograph itself, was inspected.** Nasij ("Tatar cloth") is characterized as a gold-brocade category of Near Eastern/Islamic design tradition produced under Mongol patronage; the review supports "broader named category" more than "one specific weave structure," but does not explicitly rule either way.
**Workshops/relation to Polo:** The review states Allsen argues Mongol rulers established dedicated production centers via forced artisan resettlement, with Yuan-period domestic (China-based) production eventually reducing import dependence; it does **not** name specific cities/workshops. The review states Allsen relies on Polo's own textual evidence and that Allsen's work is understood to counter Frances Wood's skepticism about whether Polo reached China — but the review does **not** define or map the Latin *panni tartarici* onto nasij.
**Critical evidentiary caveat, from the review itself:** "Technical analysis of the textiles is still needed to confirm Allsen's conclusions, which rest mainly on textual evidence" — i.e. even the specialist literature flags this as not yet confirmed by material/archaeometric analysis.
**Verdict:** Reported/partially confirmed, with a significant evidentiary caveat. Whether "nasij" is Polo's own term, a translator's gloss, or a retrospective art-historical label is **unresolved.**
**Proposed Note:** "Historians of Mongol-era textiles (notably Thomas Allsen) use 'nasij' as a name for the gold-brocaded silks prized at Mongol courts, citing Polo's own account among their evidence — but by Allsen's own reviewer's admission, this rests mainly on texts, not yet on technical/material analysis of surviving cloth."
**Sources inspected:** A scholarly review of Allsen (1997) at faculty.washington.edu/dwaugh/CA/alls-rev.html (directly inspected) — a secondary source, not the monograph. Allsen's own text: **not inspected.**
**Confidence:** Low-medium — the thinnest-evidenced topic in Group 4; no second independent textile-history source cross-checked.

**Unresolved:** Specific workshop locations; whether "nasij" is Polo's own vocabulary; whether post-2013 archaeometric textile scholarship has since supplied the material confirmation the 1997 reviewer called for.

### Group 5 — Ambergris and coastal trade

#### B.5.1 Quote/locator for Polo's witness (Socotra, Madagascar, Zanzibar)
**Polo's claim:** All three relevant chapters attribute ambergris **to the whale**, not a sea-bottom spring, tree resin, or other creature. Socotra (Bk III, Ch. XXXII, pp. 406–408): "The ambergris comes from the stomach of the whale" (verbatim quote obtained); islanders harpoon whales with barbed darts on a cord and buoy, tow the carcass ashore, cut it open, and extract ambergris and oil — presented as reported general islander custom ("they do thus..."), not as something Polo says he personally witnessed. Madagascar (Ch. XXXIII, pp. 411–415) and Zanzibar (Ch. XXXIV, pp. 422–425): merely assert ambergris is present/traded there, tied to whale abundance, with **no extraction narrative** — the Madagascar/Zanzibar sentences are **close paraphrase only, not confirmed verbatim** (fetch-tool limitation).
**Yule's footnote to Socotra (verbatim quotes obtained):** Surveys rival medieval theories as a live debate, not consensus — Mas'udi (ambergris = morsels swallowed by a fish called *Awál*, which chokes, dies, and releases the material); Kazwini (describes whale-oil harvesting at Basra, not ambergris per se); **Bennett (1840)**: "Ambergris is a morbid secretion in the intestines of the cachalot, deriving its origin either from the stomach or biliary ducts, and allied in its nature to gall-stones..."; the Chinese *Pen ts'ao* (via Bretschneider/Cordier) calls it "dragon's saliva perfume," glossed as "a pathological secretion of the intestines of the spermaceti whale." Yule's own identification favors the sperm whale, supported (he says) by Maury's Whale-Chart.
**Verdict:** The Book's claim is unambiguous (whale-origin) and, notably, comparatively "modern-sounding" versus rival medieval theories it does not adopt. Only the Socotra chapter carries an active-extraction narrative.
**Sources inspected:** Project Gutenberg #12410 (explicitly labelled as the complete 1903 Yule-Cordier text with Cordier's 1920 addenda) — fetched at large offsets, several converging fetches on the same content; the Socotra sentence and Bennett 1840 quotation returned as verbatim strings; Madagascar/Zanzibar sentences are paraphrase only.
**Confidence:** Medium-high on Socotra locator/content; medium on Madagascar/Zanzibar exact wording; low/unresolved on Pelliot's specific commentary (not located).
**Chapters affected:** III-04, III-07.
**Unresolved:** Exact verbatim wording for Madagascar/Zanzibar sentences needs re-verification against a primary scan; Moule & Pelliot (1938) and Pelliot's *Notes* not located for this passage.

#### B.5.2 Modern sperm-whale mechanism vs. the "bile" question
**Finding:** Modern peer-reviewed marine biology (per the project's already-established baseline, Brito, Jordão & Pierce, *J. Mar. Biol. Assoc. UK*, DOI 10.1017/S0025315415000910) locates ambergris formation in the sperm whale's **intestines**, treating irritation by indigestible cephalopod beaks as a **possible**, not proven, mechanism — the word "biliary" does **not** appear in that abstract.
**"Bile"/"biliary" traced:** This framing is a **separate, older (pre-1900s) historical hypothesis**, preserved in Yule's own 1903 footnote via the 1840 Bennett quotation ("stomach or biliary ducts... allied... to gall-stones"), not a term used or endorsed by the modern baseline. A secondary popular-science source (*Current Conservation*, inspected via AI-mediated summary) states the modern mechanism in lay terms as "a waxy secretion from a sperm whale's intestines" formed "when the intestines are chafed... by chitinous cuttlefish beaks," and uses neither "bile" nor "biliary" anywhere.
**Verdict:** A retelling that casually writes "ambergris comes from bile" risks conflating a superseded 19th-century conjecture (Bennett 1840, as preserved by Yule) with modern biology (intestinal secretion, possibly cephalopod-beak-triggered). If the historical "bile" idea is mentioned at all, it should be explicitly attributed to the historical sources Yule quotes, not presented as current science.
**Sources inspected:** Brito, Jordão & Pierce (already-checked project baseline, not re-verified here); Yule's footnote (verbatim Bennett 1840 quote); *Current Conservation* article (AI-mediated summary, not independently re-read verbatim; its own underlying citations, Dannenfeldt 1982 and Srinivasan 2015, not independently checked).
**Confidence:** High on the Bennett/Yule-footnote point (directly quoted); medium on "no modern peer-reviewed source uses 'biliary'" (a limited, non-exhaustive search).
**Chapters affected:** III-04, III-07.

#### B.5.3 Medieval Indian Ocean acquisition and trade, c. 1270–1300
**Finding:** Medieval Indian Ocean ambergris was predominantly **beachcombed/collected as flotsam** (Socotra, East Africa, Maldives, Bay of Bengal islands, Sumatra) per several non-Polo medieval sources reported via a secondary summary (not independently verified): Ibn Sina described an "ocean fountain" (the closest parallel to a sea-bottom-spring theory, though not the theory the Yule-Cordier text itself adopts); Yuhanna ibn Sarabi called it a "marine mushroom"; Sulaiman of Basra reported ambergris washing up on Bay of Bengal shores. Active whale-harpooning for ambergris extraction, as the Book's Socotra chapter describes, is **comparatively unusual** in the wider medieval literature and should be treated as the Book's own reported claim about that specific island, not the general/typical mode of medieval acquisition.
**Verdict:** The Book's Socotra account is reported local custom ("they do thus..."), not an eyewitness claim of Marco Polo personally watching a hunt or, still less, witnessing ambergris's biological formation (which would be biologically invisible without necropsy and is not claimed anywhere in the text). This eyewitness/reported framing is **explicitly flagged as an inference** from the text's general third-person phrasing style, not a verified pronoun-level fact — the exact narrative framing should be re-verified against a verbatim quote before being stated as settled.
**Sources inspected:** *Current Conservation* secondary summary (AI-mediated, not independently verified verbatim); Margariti (2007), *Aden and the Indian Ocean Trade* — review/catalogue listing only, content **not inspected**; al-Idrisi and Ibn Battuta primary passages on ambergris — **not located** in this pass.
**Confidence:** Medium on the general picture (beachcombing-dominant, Polo's account unusual); low on period-matched (1270–1300) primary-source corroboration.
**Chapters affected:** III-04, III-07.
**Unresolved:** Margariti's monograph, al-Idrisi's and Ibn Battuta's primary texts, and Pelliot's/Moule & Pelliot's commentary all not directly inspected.

### Group 6 — Horses, cats, and other animals

#### B.6.1 Horse trade (deaths and feed practices)
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. XVII, Maabar):** Horses are not bred locally; the kingdom buys them yearly from Gulf merchants. "Those people do not know in the least how to treat a horse"; dealers "never bring any farriers... [and] also prevent any farrier from going thither"; horses are fed "boiled rice and boiled meat"; "that is the reason why all the horses die off." Polo also claims India could not naturally breed good horses even from fine imported stock ("is proven by trial").
**Corroboration layer:** Yule's footnote (citing Wassāf, a Persianate Ilkhanid-era historian) documents a commercial indemnity clause ("if any horses should happen to die, the value... should be paid from the royal treasury") — independent evidence that the Hormuz–India horse trade was large and that horse mortality was a recognized commercial risk, though this corroborates the trade's **scale**, not Polo's specific **cause** (mismanagement/diet/deliberate farrier exclusion).
**What modern evidence can/cannot establish:** No modern veterinary, archaeozoological, or climatic study was located testing *why* horses died at the claimed rate. Candidate explanations (tropical climate/humidity stress, disease, husbandry mismatch, deliberate market manipulation) are all plausible but **none is scholarship-confirmed** in the sources inspected.
**Verdict:** Reported — faithfully reproduced; causal explanation is the Book's own assertion, partially corroborated only as to trade scale.
**Sources inspected:** Gutenberg #12410, Vol. 2, offset ~900,000–1,000,000 (direct quotes).
**Confidence:** Medium.
**Chapters affected:** III-07 (possibly III-05).

#### B.6.2 Striped "lions" (tiger)
**Polo's claim (Bk II, Ch. XVIII and XX):** The Great Khan's hunting beasts include animals "striped all along the sides with black, red, and white," called "Lions" in the text, trained to catch boar, wild cattle, bears, wild asses, stags; carried to the hunt "in a covered cart" with "a little doggie." Tent coverings are similarly described as "lions' skins, striped with black and white and red."
**Yule's footnote:** States the concept of the tiger had nearly vanished from the medieval European mind, so "Polo can only call the Tigers... Lions," attributing the confusion partly to ambiguity in the Persian word *sher* (lion or, regionally, other big cats).
**Verdict:** Corrected — no lion is striped; the identification as tiger (*Panthera tigris*) is already made within the primary critical apparatus (Yule, 1871/1903), not first caught by later scholarship. No serious rival reading was found. Subspecies-level identification (e.g. *P. t. amoyensis*) is **not** independently verified and is not asserted.
**Sources inspected:** Gutenberg #10636, Vol. 1, offset ~1,700,000–1,800,000 (direct quotes).
**Confidence:** High for species ID; medium for precise historical range/subspecies (not attempted).
**Chapters affected:** II-04, II-07, possibly II-15.

#### B.6.3 Hunting cats (cheetah vs. lynx)
**Polo's claim (same chapter):** "The Emperor hath numbers of leopards trained to the chase, and hath also a great many lynxes taught in like manner to catch game."
**Yule's footnote:** "Leopards" = "the Cheeta or Hunting-Leopard... very distinct from the true leopard" (period name *Gueparda jubata*; modern name *Acinonyx jubatus*). "Lynxes" (French/Italian "Loup-cervier"/"Leu cervers") = true lynx ("There is no doubt that the Loup-cervier is the Lynx").
**Modern corroboration:** Trained hunting cheetahs are a well-documented Central/Inner Asian and Mongol-Yuan elite practice; Thomas Allsen's *The Royal Hunt in Eurasian History* (2006) is the standard reference, but was **identified only bibliographically, not directly read** this session (gap). Eugene N. Anderson, "Birds of the Mongol Empire," *Ethnobiology Letters* 7(1) (2016), DOI 10.14237/ebl.7.1.2016.715 (full PDF read) corroborates the broader picture of a large, taxonomically diverse Mongol court menagerie of trained hunting animals.
**Verdict:** Confirmed/corrected — "leopard" → cheetah on the strength of Yule's own footnote plus general historical plausibility; "lynx" taken at face value (no correction indicated).
**Confidence:** High for cheetah identification; medium for lynx=true lynx (Yule's gloss only, not independently cross-checked).
**Chapters affected:** II-04, II-07.
**Unresolved:** Allsen 2006 not directly read; whether it specifically discusses this Polo passage is unknown.

#### B.6.4 Falcons
**Polo's claim:** Gerfalcons, peregrines, and generic "falcons"/goshawks used in a large-scale imperial falconry establishment; a hyperbolic figure of "360,000" falconers/whippers-in at court; "more than 200 gerfalcons alone" kept at Shangdu (per Anderson's quotation of Polo, not independently located in Yule's own text in this pass); gerfalcons from Christian traders sometimes diverted to "the Prince of the Levant" rather than the Khan.
**Modern corroboration (NEW, directly read):** Eugene N. Anderson (2016, cited above) confirms gerfalcons at the Mongol/Yuan court via William of Rubruck's independent 13th-century account, and documents a long-distance Eurasian gerfalcon trade reaching Arctic breeding grounds. Anderson explicitly notes the paper found **no mention of saker falcon** in Polo's account despite its historical importance, and that a bird Polo calls "Barguerlac" **"cannot be identified."** Modern Mongolian falconry today primarily uses golden eagles, not falcons — a present-day practice difference not to be read back onto the 13th century.
**Verdict:** Confirmed — species identifications are straightforward (no correction needed, unlike tiger/cheetah); general practice and gerfalcon-trade claims independently corroborated; specific numeric claims (200 gerfalcons; 360,000 falconers) are Polo's own figures, not independently verified counts.
**Confidence:** High for species ID and general practice; low/unresolved for specific numbers.
**Chapters affected:** II-04, II-07.
**Unresolved:** The "200 gerfalcons at Shangdu" passage not located directly in Yule's primary text (known only via Anderson's quotation of Polo).

#### B.6.5 Yak / wild cattle (Sinju/Erguiul)
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. LVII):** Wild cattle "as big as elephants," with long shaggy hair, some tamed and crossed with ordinary cows.
**Verdict:** Confirmed as to species identity — almost universally read in Polo scholarship as wild yak, *Bos mutus* (domestic form *Bos grunniens*), consistent with the Qinghai–Gansu border region named. "As big as elephants" is rhetorical, not a measured claim. Crossbreeding of wild/domestic yak (dzo/dzomo) is a historically plausible and still-practiced technique, though no modern source specifically traces this exact 13th-century crossbreeding claim.
**Confidence:** Medium-high on species ID; low on independently-sourced modern range confirmation (IUCN Red List account for *Bos mutus* not directly checked — gap).
**Chapters affected:** I-10, I-11.

#### B.6.6 Musk deer and extraction method
**Polo's claim (same chapter, a few paragraphs on):** A gazelle-like animal; musk extracted from "at the navel between the flesh and the skin something like an impostume full of blood," which Polo's own folk-physiological model treats as "the blood inside this impostume is the musk."
**Yule's footnote:** Ramusio adds a New Moon hunting-timing detail (a textual-variant addition, not necessarily in the main F-tradition). Yule corrects Polo's description of "four tusks": "the musk deer has canine teeth only in the upper jaw." Yule situates the animal within genus *Moschus*.
**Verdict:** Reported/partially confirmed — genus-level identification (*Moschus* sp.) is solid and already present in Yule's apparatus; **species-level identification is explicitly left open** per the project's precision rule (several *Moschus* species are geographically plausible for this region; none can be confidently singled out from the sources inspected). The extraction-method description (killing the animal, removing a navel gland/sac) is broadly consistent with traditional musk harvesting, though Polo's "musk = blood" model is a period folk-biological misunderstanding (modern biology: a dedicated preputial/musk gland, not blood) — this correction is not fully made explicit by Yule's own note.
**Confidence:** High for genus-level ID and extraction-practice plausibility; low/unresolved for species-level ID and for independent circa-1270–1300 trade-route confirmation.
**Chapters affected:** I-10, I-11.

#### B.6.9 Yunnan "serpents" and gall-medicine
**Polo's claim (Bk II, Ch. XLIX, "Carajan"/Yunnan, near Dali):** Huge serpents "ten paces in length"; hunters extract the gall, which "sells at a great price" and is used to treat mad-dog bite, difficult childbirth, and skin disease.
**Yule's note:** Reads the "serpents" as crocodiles (acknowledging anatomical description errors re: legs/habits), comparing the described prominent eyes loosely to the Gharial (*Gavialis gangeticus*) — a comparison for one physical trait only, **not** a claim the Yunnan animal is a gharial (a freshwater Indian/Nepali species with no range near Yunnan).
**Gall-medicine efficacy:** No modern pharmacological or ethnozoological source was found specifically testing or corroborating the claimed uses. One unrelated modern lab study on crocodile **oil** (not gall) anti-inflammatory effects exists but must **not** be conflated with verification of 13th-century gall-medicine claims. Gall-as-medicine is a well-known East/Southeast Asian cultural-practice pattern generally, lending plausibility but not confirmation.
**Verdict:** Reported — Yule's crocodile-not-serpent correction is well documented; gall-medicine efficacy is reported period belief, not verified by any source inspected.

#### B.6.10 African giraffes/ostriches (Madagascar, Zanzibar, Abyssinia)
**Polo's claim:** Giraffes attributed to Madagascar (Ch. XXXIII — a geographic misattribution, since giraffes never occurred on Madagascar), Zanzibar (Ch. XXXIV — "Its colour is all red and white in round spots"), and Abyssinia/Abash (Ch. XXXV, "produced in the country," alongside bears, leopards, lions, and ostriches "nearly as big as asses"). "Beautiful cocks and hens" in Abyssinia are glossed as probably guinea fowl.
**Modern layer:** Giraffes are and were native to African mainland regions including the Horn of Africa and East African coastal hinterland — the Abyssinia and "Zanzibar" (likely meaning the adjacent mainland coast) attributions are geographically plausible for real mainland populations even though Polo's island geography is confused; the **Madagascar attribution is simply false** (giraffes never native there). Ostriches are and were native to Abyssinia/Horn of Africa and Arabia (consistent with a separate Dufar/Dhofar reference).
**Ming/Yuan tribute question, specifically checked:** The well-documented historical giraffe-tribute episode is the **1414 Ming Yongle-era** event (via Bengal, ultimately from East Africa/Malindi, tied to Zheng He's voyages). **No Yuan-dynasty (pre-1368, Polo-era) giraffe tribute episode was found.** Polo's travels ended by 1295 — over a century before 1414. **Any claim linking Polo's giraffe material to the 1414 Ming episode, or to a Yuan-court tribute giraffe, is unsupported and should be flagged as a conflation if it appears elsewhere in the draft.**
**Verdict:** Confirmed as to general range plausibility (Abyssinia/mainland East Africa); corrected as to Madagascar; disputed/ruled-out — no Ming/Yuan conflation connection exists.
**Confidence:** High that the 1414 episode is unconnected to Polo; medium on detailed range plausibility (not checked against a dedicated IUCN range map).
**Chapters affected:** III-05.

XXI ("The Land of Darkness")** — **not** attached to "Bargu" as the project's framing suggested; "Bargu" denotes a different northern region (near Lake Baikal) in the material searched. Separately, general "Tartar dogs" material (hunting/companion dogs, dogs eaten as food) sits in Book I, Ch. LII ("Customs of the Tartars") — a different topic that should not be merged with the sled-dog claim.
**Modern context:** General (not location-specific) corroboration exists that Siberian/Arctic sled-dog technology has deep roots going back millennia (archaeogenomic work on sites like Zhokhov Island), supporting general plausibility of a 13th-century Siberian dog-sledge relay system, but the specific "driverless" detail is **not independently verified** as documented practice.
**Verdict:** Reported — textual location corrected and distinguished from unrelated material; general technology plausibility supported; the specific claim not independently verified.
**Confidence:** Medium.
**Chapters affected:** Likely II-15 or an unlisted Book IV-specific ID — the project's own ID mapping for "Bargu" should be re-checked, since the actual sled-dog passage attaches to "Conchi"/"Land of Darkness," not Bargu.

#### B.6.12 Crocodilians / Yunnan "serpents," species-level identification
**Task instruction:** Distinguish *Alligator sinensis* (lower Yangtze, not historically Yunnan) from true crocodiles (e.g. *Crocodylus siamensis*, historically present in parts of mainland Southeast Asia), or explicitly leave the ID open.
**Finding:** *Alligator sinensis*'s documented range (AnimalDiversity.org, directly read; Wang et al. 2016, *PeerJ* 4:e2356, directly read — peer-reviewed paleontology) is centered on the lower Yangtze and adjacent Chinese provinces (Guangdong, Anhui, Shandong fossil localities); **neither source mentions Yunnan.** *Crocodylus siamensis* (Wikipedia, directly read) has a native range given as Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam, and Kalimantan — Laos and Vietnam border Yunnan, which is geographically suggestive but **not a sourced claim** of historical Yunnan occurrence (the researcher's own inference, flagged as such). A speculative, non-peer-reviewed blog proposal (*Hanyusuchus*, an extinct giant South China crocodylian) is flagged as **geographically mismatched** (associated with Guangdong/the Pearl River Delta, not Yunnan) and should not be treated as an established identification.
**Verdict:** Left explicitly open at the species level, per task instruction. *Alligator sinensis* is reasonably excluded by range evidence; *Crocodylus siamensis* is plausible on regional-biogeographic grounds but not confirmed for Yunnan specifically; the best defensible description is "a crocodilian (order Crocodylia), genus/species unresolved."
**Confidence:** Medium-high that it is not *A. sinensis*; low that any single species can be confidently named without further Yunnan-specific archaeozoological evidence (not located — Chinese-language zooarchaeological literature on Yunnan crocodilian remains was not searched, an explicit gap).
**Chapters affected:** II-04.

### Group 7 — Minerals, processes, and currency

#### B.7.1 Badakhshan balas (spinel) and lapis lazuli
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XXIX):** Balas rubies dug from a single mountain ("Syghinan"); the king personally controls digging, limiting output to keep prices up, forbidding others on pain of death. A second mountain yields "azure" (lapis), "the finest in the world," in a vein "much like silver."
**Yule's footnotes:** Confirms balas = spinel ("like the Spinel, a kind inferior to the real Ruby of Ava"), placing the mines near Ish Káshm/Ghārān on the Oxus (with an editorial geographic correction to Polo's own text). Lapis mines placed in the Upper Kokcha Valley/Yamgán tract (= modern Sar-e-Sang district), with 19th-century output reported as "inferior," ~30–60 poods/year.
**Modern finding:** Kuh-i-Lal (spinel) and Sar-e-Sang (lapis) are real, long-documented deposits — uncontroversial. A gemology-trade source (skyjems.ca, not peer-reviewed) dates a legendary discovery tradition to an 8th-century earthquake but addresses neither the 13th century nor Polo specifically.
**Verdict:** General mineral identity — confirmed. Verified historical deposit — confirmed, long-documented. **13th-century-specific active mining/royal monopoly — reported only**, with Polo himself as the primary evidence; no independent contemporary corroboration found.
**Confidence:** Medium-high on text/general-deposit identity; low on the 13th-century-specific mining claim.
**Chapters affected:** I-07/08.

#### B.7.2 Kerman vs. Nishapur turquoise
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XVII):** Kerman has "the stones called turquoises in great abundance." **Polo names Kerman explicitly and does not mention Nishapur anywhere in this passage** — the famous Persian turquoise source is simply absent from his text.
**Modern finding:** A 2021 GIA *Gems & Gemology* article ("Persian Turquoise: The Ancient Treasure of Neyshabur," Fall 2021, **NEW**, peer-reviewed gemological trade journal) states Iran's turquoise deposits "were never limited to Neyshabur," naming **Shahr-i Babak, in western Kerman Province**, as a historical source, and stating it "was mentioned in Marco Polo's travel account" — citing Weisgerber (2004), which was **not independently inspected** (secondhand, one level removed).
**Verdict:** Confirmed that Polo names Kerman, not Nishapur, and that this is **not an error** — Shahr-i Babak/Kerman is an independently documented, if less famous, separate historical turquoise source.
**Proposed Note:** "Polo credits turquoise to Kerman, not the far more famous Nishapur mines — and this isn't an error: Shahr-i Babak in Kerman province is an independently documented historical and modern turquoise source."
**Confidence:** High on the textual fact; medium on the modern corroboration (secondhand citation chain to Weisgerber 2004, not independently verified).
**Chapters affected:** I-07/08.

#### B.7.3 Kerman ondanique/crucible steel and etymology
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XVII and Ch. XXI, "Cobinan"; also reused at Chingintalas, Ch. XLII):** Kerman has "plenty of veins of steel and Ondanique"; Cobinan has "much Iron and Steel and Ondanique," used to make large, fine steel mirrors.
**Yule's etymology:** Proposes the underlying word is ***Hundwáníy*, "Indian Steel"** (citing Johnson's Persian dictionary, Vüllers, Edrisi's *Hindiah*, and the Spanish *alhinde*/*alfinde*) — a philological argument, confirmed as Yule's own, matching the "Hindwani = Indian steel" etymology the brief asked about.
**Modern finding:** Alipour & Rehren (2014), "Persian Pulād Production: Chāhak Tradition," *Journal of Islamic Archaeology* 1(2): 231–261 (**NEW**, peer-reviewed archaeometallurgy, read via summarized PDF): the first *archaeologically documented* Persian crucible-steel site is **Chāhak** (Yazd province, near but administratively distinct from Kerman) — **Kerman itself is not described in this paper as a production site.** A 13th-century Persian text, Ṭūsī's *Tansukh-Nāma* — exactly Polo's era — is the first detailed crucible-steel text mentioning Chāhak, comparing its blades to "Indian and Harāt steel," textually consistent with (not direct proof of) a Kerman-region "Indian steel" association.
**Verdict:** General identity (ondanique = prized crucible/"Damascus-type" steel) — confirmed. Etymology — confirmed as Yule's own argument. **13th-century production specifically at Kerman city/province — not found / disputed-by-silence** in the one archaeometric paper inspected.
**Confidence:** High on text/etymology; medium on archaeometric picture (single paper, summarized).
**Chapters affected:** I-07/08.

#### B.7.4 Kuh-Banan tutty/spodium
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XXI, "Cobinan"):** A furnace process in which "a certain earth" is heated under a grating; collected smoke/moisture is Tutia (tutty); leftover slag is Spodium.
**Yule's footnotes:** Identifies Cobinan with Kuh-Banán; quotes **Teixeira** (17th century — centuries after Polo) that tutia was "found only in Kermán," describing ore preparation and shipment to Hormuz; compares to Galen's pompholyx/spodos.
**Verdict:** General identity (tutty = zinc oxide, used medicinally/cosmetically) — reported, consistent with Yule's note but not independently re-confirmed against a modern mineralogical source. **13th-century-specific production — not found/not completed** — even Yule's own corroborating citation (Teixeira) postdates Polo by ~300 years.
**Confidence:** Low-medium, reliant mainly on Yule's own 19th-century editorial sourcing.
**Chapters affected:** I-07/08.

#### B.7.5 Chingintalas asbestos and the salamander legend
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XLII):** A mountain vein from which "Salamander is made"; processing described (crushed, fiberized, dried, spun, woven, whitened by fire). **Polo explicitly and directly rejects the animal-salamander legend:** "the Salamander is no beast, as they allege in our part of the world, but is a substance found in the earth"; "Any other account of the matter is fabulous nonsense."

#### B.7.8 Yunnan cowries
**Polo's claim (Bk II, Ch. XLVIII, "Carajan"):** "White porcelain shells that are found in the sea" used as currency; "80 of these porcelain shells pass for a single weight of silver."
**Yule's note:** Polo's word *porcellani* glossed as "Pig-shells" (*Cypraea*-genus dialect name); citing Klaproth, Yunnan was "the only part of China where cowries stayed in use," at 80 cowries = 6 cash; a Sylhet/Bengal 1778 cowrie-to-rupee ratio is offered as a scale analogy only, not period evidence for Yunnan.
**Modern finding:** A 1995 *Sino-Platonic Papers* study (Ke Peng & Yanshi Zhu) identifies the relevant currency-cowrie species generally as *Monetaria annulus*/*moneta*, but its Yunnan material concerns the much earlier Dian-culture period (Warring States–Western Han) — **not period-matched** to Polo. Bin Yang's *Cowrie Shells and Cowrie Money: A Global History* (Routledge, 2019, **NEW**) is the most relevant modern monograph, but **only a book review of it, not the monograph itself, was inspected** — the review states the book frames Yunnan as "the farthest region reached by the Bengal monetary world," with a periodization extending through a "tenth-to-thirteenth-century" phase, but gives no Polo-era passage specifically and does not confirm the exact overland route.
**Verdict:** Species — the general East/South Asian currency-cowrie group is confirmed; exact 13th-century-Yunnan species not confirmed. Trade route — reported, secondhand (via one book review). Polo's own "80 shells = 1 weight of silver" exchange rate is a specific, checkable, period-claimed figure, distinct from and not to be conflated with Yule's 19th-century Klaproth/Sylhet analogies.
**Confidence:** Medium on Polo's text; low-medium on modern scholarship specifics.
**Chapters affected:** II-07.

#### B.7.9 Ceylon king's ruby
**Finding:** Yule's corroboration note (Bk III, Ch. XIV, "Seilan," Note 5) assembles an explicit chain of independent witnesses to the fame of a great Ceylonese royal ruby spanning roughly a millennium: Cosmas Indicopleustes (6th c., predates Polo), Hiuen Tsang/Xuanzang (7th c., predates Polo), Hayton (early 14th c.: "the king of that Island of Celan hath the largest and finest ruby in existence"), Odoric and Ibn Battuta (14th c., postdate Polo), Friar Jordanus and Andrea Corsali (14th/16th c., postdate Polo), and a Chinese account of an early-14th-century envoy sent specifically to buy a Ceylonese carbuncle for an emperor's cap.
**Important caveat:** Polo's own base-text sentence describing the stone was **not independently re-confirmed** in this pass (only Yule's corroboration-note content was obtained).
**Verdict:** This is squarely a case of **reported fame across many witnesses, not verification of one specific physical gem.** The repeated "Ceylon's king has a famous ruby" motif across centuries most plausibly reflects a recurring topos applied generation after generation to whatever stone the current king held, not one continuously-tracked object — there is no independent documentary or physical proof of a single identifiable gem.
**Confidence:** Medium — good on the corroboration-chain content, incomplete on the base Polo quotation.
**Chapters affected:** III-04.

#### B.7.10 Mutfili location, diamond workings, and the eagle/meat legend
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. XIX, "Mutfili"):** Diamonds found in torrent-beds after rains; mountains infested with "great serpents... to a marvellous degree"; miners throw lean meat into ravines, diamonds stick to it, "white eagles" that feed on the serpents carry off the meat (with diamonds attached); people drive off the eagles by shouting or later collect diamonds from the birds' droppings.
**Yule's location identification:** Mutfili = **Telingana**, under the **Kakatiya (Kákateya/Ganapati)** dynasty reigning at Warangal; "Mutfili" = the port of Motupalle (Guntur district); the reigning queen was probably **Rudrama Devi** (a real, historically attested Kakatiya queen — a specific, checkable prosopographical claim). The real Golconda-associated diamond mines are placed in the Kistna/Krishna alluvium near Kadapa and Kurnool, within the Kakatiya realm.
**Legend vs. practice — Yule's own explicit position:** Yule does **not** assert the eagle/meat method was real 13th-century mining practice. He calls it "a very ancient and widely diffused" legend, directly connecting it to the Herodotean cinnamon-harvesting story and tracing an attestation chain from **Epiphanius, Bishop of Salamis** (4th c. — "the earliest known occurrence," per Yule) through Edrisi, Al-Qazwini (who relocates the legend to Ceylon in his version — a geographic slippage worth noting), a Chinese account of Hulagu's campaigns (Ch'ang Te, 1259, contemporary with Polo's era) and explicitly flags **Sindbad's version as "the closest of all to our author's."** This Sindbad connection is Yule's own 1903 observation, not a modern retrospective reading.
**Verdict:** General mineral identity and verified historical deposit (Golconda-region diamond fields) — confirmed. 13th-century-specific mining at/near Mutfili — plausible but not pinned to the decade by the sources inspected (modern searches returned mostly tourism content, not peer-reviewed archaeology). **The bird/meat retrieval method — disputed as real mining practice, explicitly flagged as legendary by Yule himself in 1903**, not a modern correction.
**Confidence:** High on Yule-text findings (location ID, legend-vs-practice judgment, attestation chain); low-medium on pinning modern archaeology to the exact 1270–1300 window.
**Chapters affected:** III-04/06.

### Group 8 — Marvel-motif genealogies and explanations

#### B.8.1 The Dry Tree / Solitary Tree ("Arbre Sec" / "Arbre Sol")
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XXII):** An immense plain with a great tree Polo's French redaction calls the *Arbre Sec* ("we Christians call it the Arbre Sec"), with white bark on one side, associated with the Alexander-Darius battle site.
**Textual crux:** At least three attested readings — Pauthier's "Solque" (a corrupt reading); Ramusio's Italian "Albero del Sole" ("Tree of the Sun"); Yule's own editorial preference for **"Arbre Sol"** ("Sun Tree") as closer to the authentic underlying reading, while the Christian-legend tradition calls it "Arbre Sec" (Dry Tree). Yule explicitly **rejects** Pauthier's proposed *Ficus Vasta* identification, favoring instead the **Chínár / Oriental Plane** (*Platanus orientalis*) on botanical-descriptive grounds (via Marsden and Olearius).
**Literary-motif connection:** Yule's own introductory essay explicitly connects Polo's Arbre Sol/Sec to the *Alexander Romance*'s "Legend of the Oracular Trees of the Sun and Moon" (which foretell Alexander's death), stating later redactions "mixed up" this pagan motif with the Christian Dry-Tree legend. The Alexander Romance's episode long predates Polo (Pseudo-Callisthenes tradition, 3rd c. CE core, with Latin derivatives like the 10th-century *Historia de Preliis*).
**Named scholarly dispute:** A. Houtum-Schindler, *JRAS* 41.1 (1909): 154–162, argues "Arbre Sol" means not "Sun-tree" but specifically the Zoroastrian "Cypress of Zoroaster/Kishmar" — inspected only as a title/metadata abstract (paywalled); the argument itself is **not verified.**
**Ferula identification explicitly checked and not found:** A targeted search for a "Ferula" (giant fennel) identification of the Dry Tree found **no trace of it** in any source inspected — not in Yule, not in Houtum-Schindler's metadata, not in a 2025 literary-genealogy article (Karin Ueltschi, *Studia Romanica Posnaniensia* 52/2 (2025): 77–90, DOI 10.14746/strop.2025.52.2.6, full PDF read, **NEW**) which discusses the broader Dry Tree/Tree-of-Life legend-complex (Mandeville's oak identification) but does **not** discuss Polo or this textual crux specifically. **This should be treated as unverified/possibly apocryphal until a source is found, not repeated even as a "proposed rival" identification.**
**Verdict:** A genuine, multiply-attested textual crux reflecting conflation of a Christian Dry-Tree legend and the Alexander Romance's Oracular Trees motif; Yule's botanical guess (chinar) is inferred from physical description, not from the name, and remains disputed (Houtum-Schindler). No scholar located argues a single clean transmission line from one specific pre-Polo text into Polo's dictation.
**Confidence:** Medium-high on the crux's existence and shape; low-medium on Houtum-Schindler's actual argument (title only); low on "Ferula" (absence confirmed by search, not proof of non-existence elsewhere).
**Chapters affected:** I-08, I-10.

#### B.8.2 Desert voices / singing sands (Desert of Lop)
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XXXIX):** Travelers crossing the Desert of Lop hear voices calling them by name (sometimes sounding like a companion), leading them astray, even in daytime; the voices are explicitly framed as belonging to evil spirits. A separate, non-supernatural practical detail in the same chapter (hanging small bells on animals to avoid losing them in fog/dark) must not be conflated with the spirit-voices motif.
**Real acoustic phenomenon:** Booming/singing sand dunes are a well-documented real geophysical phenomenon (synchronized grain avalanching producing a resonant hum), independent of Polo. **No specific peer-reviewed geophysics paper confirming documented booming/singing dunes within the historical Lop Nor/Taklamakan region specifically was located** — the geographically adjacent, well-known Mingsha Shan ("Singing Sand Mountain") near Dunhuang should not be assumed to transfer to Lop Nor proper without a dedicated source.
**Literary genealogy:** Not independently traced to a specific named pre-Polo text; Yule's own remark ("men have ever conceived of the Wilderness as the haunt of evil spirits") is generic folkloric observation, not a citation to an earlier text naming Lop Nor specifically. Xuanzang's 7th-century *Great Tang Records on the Western Regions* (which does include supernatural desert-crossing material for a similar route) was **not checked** in this pass.
**Verdict:** The real acoustic phenomenon is a plausible underlying stimulus for a folk "ghost voices" explanation generally, but **no scholar-argued, source-dated causal chain** from Lop Nor dune acoustics specifically to Polo's specific wording was located or can be asserted — the connection should be presented as suggestive, not demonstrated.
**Confidence:** Medium-high on what Polo's text says; low on both comparative layers (acoustic siting, literary genealogy) — explicit gaps.
**Chapters affected:** I-11.

#### B.8.3 Pamir fire and altitude
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. XXXII, Pamir plateau):** "Because of this great cold, fire does not burn so brightly, nor give out so much heat as usual," and food "does not cook... so effectually" at that height; Polo states he saw no birds flying there. **Polo's own stated cause is cold, not explicitly "thin air" in a scientific register** — this distinction matters and must not be collapsed.
**Yule's footnote:** Ramusio's recension frames the effect as something "asserted" to Polo (implying secondhand report); **Alexander von Humboldt** is cited as having doubted Polo personally crossed the Pamir, partly on this basis. **Major T.G. Montgomerie** (19th-century surveyor, cited by Yule) reports the **cooking claim specifically holds for boiled food** (water boils at ~170–180°F at Pamir altitude — genuinely consistent with real high-altitude physics), but attributes the "dull fire" itself mainly to **poor fuel (dung, small sticks)**, not to altitude/thin-air chemistry as such; Huc and Gabet (19th-c. missionary travelers) corroborate that dung fuel produces smoke and little flame at high passes.
**Verdict:** A claim-to-mechanism mismatch that must not be collapsed: the one named expert witness (Montgomerie) splits the explanation between a real but secondary altitude/boiling-point effect (for boiled food specifically) and a fuel-quality effect unrelated to altitude physics. A reader-facing note should **not** simply say "science confirms thin air explains Polo's fire claim."
**Confidence:** Medium-high on text and Montgomerie's testimony; the general combustion-chemistry statement (reduced oxygen partial pressure affecting flame at altitude) is stated as general science, **not backed by a specific peer-reviewed citation this session** — a gap.
**Chapters affected:** I-13.

#### B.8.4 Roc / Rukh
**Polo's claim (Bk III, Ch. XXXIII, Madagascar):** Islanders south of Madagascar "call the bird Ruc, and it has no other name"; eagle-like but enormous, able to seize an elephant, carry it aloft, and drop it to death; a 90-span feather reportedly brought to the Great Khan's court. **Verdict:** Polo's Rukh sits within a well-attested, centuries-older Indo-Arabic/Mediterranean giant-bird fable-complex (Mas'udi, Marvels of Hind, Sindbad, Benjamin of Tudela, Duke Ernest poem — all pre-Polo and cited by Yule), but **no scholar located argues a single specific transmission line** into Polo's text; Yule frames it as general diffusion within a shared fable-family (explicitly naming Garuda, Simurgh, 'Anqā', and the Greek griffin as cognates).
**Confidence:** High on Polo's claim and the directly-cited pre-Polo Arabic/Hebrew/Latin comparanda; medium-low on the elephant-bird attribution chain; low on the raffia-palm hypothesis.
**Chapters affected:** III-01, III-06, III-07.

### Group 9 — Food and other items

#### B.9.1 Hormuz date-wine and dried fish as fodder (two separate claims)
**(a) Date-wine at Hormuz — confirmed, exact wording found (Bk I, Ch. XIX, "Descent to the City of Hormos"):** "In this country they make a wine of dates mixt with spices, which is very good." Diet: "Their food when in health consists of dates and salt-fish (tunny, to wit) and onions"; "the people never eat meat and wheaten bread except when they are ill." Yule's Note 2 quotes an Ibn Battuta proverb about this diet ("Dates and fish make an Emperor's dish!").
**(b) Dried fish as animal fodder — corrected, misattributed location.** This claim is **not** in the Hormuz chapter at all (confirmed by a line-by-line check of Ch. XIX and its notes). It belongs to **Book III, Ch. XXXVII, "Concerning the City of Esher"** (Shihr, South Arabian coast): "all their cattle, including horses, oxen, and camels, live upon small fish... dried and stored, and the beasts are fed on them from year's end to year's end... The cattle will also readily eat these fish all alive." Independently and strongly corroborated by Yule's Note 4 quoting Ibn Battuta: "all kinds of cattle are fed on these sardines, and sheep likewise"; "On the coast of Shehr, men and animals all live on fish" — a still-practiced regional custom.
**Verdict:** Date-wine — confirmed exactly as stated for Hormuz. Fish-fodder — a real Polo claim, but misattributed to Hormuz in the project's framing; corrected to Esher/Shihr.
**Confidence:** High on exact wording and location of both passages.
**Chapters affected:** Likely I-07/08 (flag for location correction if currently mapped to Hormuz alone).

#### B.9.2 Sapurgan melons

(Exact chapter number not confirmed — sourced via a derivative-edition page transcription, not a canonical chapter numeral — gap.)

No dedicated peer-reviewed agricultural-history source specifically on Shibarghan melon cultivation circa 1270–1300 was located — plausible by regional analogy only.

**Confidence:** High on the quote; low-medium on independently-sourced period-specific evidence for Shibarghan itself.

#### B.9.3 Mare's-milk kumiss
**Polo's claim (Bk I, Ch. This is an **eyewitness-register claim** in Polo's own narrative voice, consistent with his general practice of describing Mongol customs he observed directly.

**Verdict:** Confirmed.
**Confidence:** High.

#### B.9.5 Rice wine and grape wine (two distinct claims)
**(a) Grape wine — confirmed, exact wording found, located specifically at Taianfu (Taiyuan, Shanxi), not generically "China" (Bk II, Ch. XXXVII):** "Many excellent vines, supplying great plenty of wine," and — a striking, checkable detail — **"in all Cathay this is the only place where wine is produced."** Yule's notes add that Shanxi grape-wine later supplied early Jesuit missions, and that Mongol patronage helped spread viticulture into north China.
**(b) Rice wine — reported, not run to a confirmed, precisely located quote.** The only candidate fragment found (the same "wine of snow bubbles and apricot blossom" fragment noted under B.9.4) could not be confirmed as describing rice wine specifically, or confirmed as Polo's own voice. **Verdict:** Grape wine — confirmed, and a good, specific, falsifiable claim ("only place in all Cathay") consistent with the genuinely limited, Mongol-introduced viticulture of Shanxi; the text itself already distinguishes grape wine (rare/localized) from rice wine (not claimed absent elsewhere). Rice wine — unresolved, needs a follow-up direct read of the Kinsay/Hangzhou chapters.

#### B.9.6 Sago/tree flour and coconut ("Indian nut")
**(a) Sago — confirmed in substance** (Sumatran "Lambri and Fansur" material, Bk III): a large tree's trunk pith ground into flour, mixed with water, beaten so bran rises and "the pure flour sinks to the bottom," drained, made into "pasta in strips and other forms." **Exact literal 1903 Yule-Cordier wording not 100% pinned down** — the passage as retrieved was framed through a comparison with Marsden's older (1818) translation, so this needs a final verbatim check before quotation.
**(b) Coconut/"Indian nut" — confirmed, exact wording found, located specifically at Samara (Sumatra), not generically "various tropical locations":** "They have also great quantities of Indian nuts [as big as a man's head], which are good to eat when fresh," and on the water inside: "better to the taste, and more delicate than wine or any other drink that ever existed."
**Modern layer:** Both sago as a Southeast Asian starch staple (independently corroborated by the 1225 Chinese source Zhao Rukuo's *Zhu Fan Zhi*, describing sago in Brunei, wholly independent of Polo) and coconut as a major Sumatran resource are well established; Polo's description of coconut water's taste matches modern description closely.
**Verdict:** Both confirmed as real Polo claims, correctly routed to the Sumatra region; the brief's "various tropical locations" framing for coconut should be tightened to Samara specifically.
**Confidence:** High for coconut (exact quote in hand); medium-high for sago (substance certain, exact Yule-specific wording pending final check).

#### B.9.8 Zayton's pepper comparison with Alexandria ("100:1")
**Polo's claim — exact wording confirmed (Bk II, Ch. LXXXII, "Of the City and Great Haven of Zayton"):** "For one shipload of pepper that goes to Alexandria or elsewhere, destined for Christendom, there come a hundred such, aye and more too, to this haven of Zayton; and yet Zayton is one of the two greatest havens in the world for commerce." Customs figures in the same chapter: freight on small wares 30%, on pepper 44%, with "a good half the value" total between duty and freight.
**Critical precision check:** The "100:1" figure is a fair compression of Polo's own words, but his phrasing — "a hundred such, aye and more too" — is an **open-ended, rhetorically rounded estimate**, not a precise measured ratio. Presenting it as a measured statistic would overstate the text's own precision.
**Modern layer:** General directional plausibility (Zayton/Quanzhou as a vastly larger spice-import hub than Mamluk Alexandria circa 1300) is well supported by general knowledge of Yuan maritime trade scale and the much larger Chinese domestic market — confirmed as a live, legitimate scholarly topic via citation/abstract-level search only (full peer-reviewed articles, e.g. work by Billy So, Hugh Clark, or Roderich Ptak on Yuan maritime trade, were **not read in full text this session**). The **specific ratio is not independently provable** from any source inspected — no period customs register or modern quantitative reconstruction was located that would let anyone actually compute the figure.
**Verdict:** Confirmed as to wording; the underlying number is the Book's own rhetorical estimate, plausible in direction, unverifiable in exact magnitude.
**Confidence:** High on exact Polo wording and location; medium on general directional plausibility; low/explicitly unverifiable on the specific numeric ratio as history.
