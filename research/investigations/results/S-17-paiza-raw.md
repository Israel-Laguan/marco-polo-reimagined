## A. The Book's claim (Yule–Cordier base text)

- **Ladder.** `yc` I, Bk II ch. vii, refs/yule-vol1.txt:31490–31521. Captain of 100: silver tablet. Captain of 1,000: "gold or silver-gilt". Commander of 10,000: gold with a lion's head. The tablets of 100 and 1,000 weigh 120 *saggi* each; the lion's-head tablet 220 *saggi*. Chief of 100,000 or general-in-chief: 300 *saggi*, lion below the inscription, sun and moon below the lion. Gerfalcon tablet: "only to the very greatest of the Kaan's barons", with power to seize any man's horses when sending a messenger. Matches `vol-2/ch-01-cublay-nayan.md` ¶¶ 45–47 (Notes [17], [18]).
- **Couriers.** `yc` I, Bk II ch. xxvi, refs/yule-vol1.txt:35628–35632: each express courier "carries with him a gerfalcon tablet, in sign that he is bound on an urgent express".
- **Kiacatu's four tablets.** `yc` I, Prol. ch. xviii, refs/yule-vol1.txt:14024–14035: four golden tablets, two with gerfalcons, one with lions, one plain (S18-F5).
- **Kublai's tablets to the Polos.** Prol. ch. viii (vol-1 ch-02 ¶ 33, Note [14]): one gold tablet; Prol. ch. xviii (vol-3 ch-09 ¶ 23, Note [7]): two gold tablets. These name gold, but give no rank.
- **Sizes in Ramusio only.** Yule, refs/yule-vol1.txt:31649–31654: the French texts give no size. Ramusio's Prologue gives Kiacatu's tablets as a cubit long, five fingers wide, 3–4 marks each.

## B. Yule–Cordier's notes (read; each item reports another source)

1. **Persian "lion's head" paiza.** Yule, refs/yule-vol1.txt:31540–31549: the Persian historians use *Páïzah* and sometimes *Páïzah Sir-i-Sher*, "Lion's Head Paizah". Ghazan's firman grants "the Lion's Head Paizah". Sources named by Yule: Hammer, *Ilch.* II; Wassāf. **Reported** (Yule ← Hammer).
2. **Ilkhanid grades by number of lions' heads, 1 to 5.** Yule, refs/yule-vol1.txt:31576–31579. Source not named in the sentence; the note's source list cites Hammer, *Ilch.* II. 166 ff. **Reported**.
3. **Ghazan's paiza regulations.** Yule, refs/yule-vol1.txt:31586–31600: paizas of different *kinds* and degrees. Post-riders' paizas carry the holder's name ("which Hammer says were of brass"). **Reported** (Yule ← Hammer ← Rashīd al-Dīn); not found in the Thackston text read (§ E).
4. **Gerfalcon tablet.** Yule, Note 4, refs/yule-vol1.txt:31816–31830: "I have not been able to find any allusion, except in our author, to tablets, with gerfalcons". Wassāf, as used by Hammer, has the governor of Shiraz (AH 683) invested with "the Golden Lion's Head, and the golden *Cat's Head*". Yule suggests the cat's head is the gerfalcon. **Reported** (Yule ← Hammer ← Wassāf), and Yule's identification is a conjecture.
5. **Palladius on the Yuan types** (Cordier's addition). refs/yule-vol1.txt:31689–31697, quoting Palladius, *l.c.* p. 39 (= `palladius1876elucidations`). The Mongols had tiger tablets (*hu fu*) "of several kinds, which differed by the metal ... as well as by the number of pearls (one, two, or three)". "Falcon's tablets with the figure of a falcon were round, and used to be given only to special couriers and envoys of the Khan." Palladius's sources: *Yuen shi lui pien* and *Yuen ch'ao tien chang*. **Reported** (Cordier ← Palladius ← *Yuanshi leibian*, *Yuan dianzhang*). The Palladius article itself was not opened.
6. **Liao precedent** (Cordier, from Bushell). refs/yule-vol1.txt:31666–31677: 600 silver *p'ai tzu* inscribed "urgent", from *Liao shi* 57. **Reported**; relevant only as a precedent.
7. **Surviving tablets known to Yule.** refs/yule-vol1.txt:31623–31648. "I do not know if any gold Paizah has been discovered". Silver ones found near the Dnieper and two in Eastern Siberia. Minusinsk 1846: silver with gilt inscription, Mongol language in 'Phags-pa ("Baspa") script, the ring reading "Publication No. 42", 12.2 × 3.65 in. from Schmidt's drawing. Dnieper 1845: Uighur script. Yule notes that his plate redraws the Minusinsk tablet **as gold**, which it is not. **Read** (Yule's description).
8. **1269 edict.** Yule, Prol. ch. xv Note 1, refs/yule-vol1.txt:13704–13709: the Báshpah character was "established by edict in 1269 as the official character", after Gaubil. Tablets are not mentioned. **Reported** (Yule ← Gaubil).
9. **Weights.** Yule, refs/yule-vol1.txt:31614–31620: if *saggio* = *mithqāl* (74 grains), the smallest tablet would be 18½ oz troy. This is Yule's own conversion.

## C. *Yuan shi* (`yuanshi1370`; read in the Wikisource transcription)

URLs follow the pattern `https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/元史/卷NNN`.

### C1. Rank by metal

- **YS 98, *Bing zhi* 1** ([卷098](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7098)), opening survey of the military system, undated ("考之國初 … 世祖時"):
  > 萬戶、千戶、百戶分上中下。萬戶佩金虎符。符趺為伏虎形，首為明珠，而有三珠、二珠、一珠之別。千戶金符，百戶銀符。

  Myriarchs, chiliarchs and centurions are graded upper, middle and lower. The myriarch wears a **gold tiger tally**, with a crouching tiger at the base and bright pearls at the head, three, two or one (by grade). The chiliarch has a **gold tally**, the centurion a **silver tally**. (My translation.) Coşkun's page: YS 98: 2507–08 (reported).
- **YS 91, *Baiguan zhi* 7** ([卷091](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7091)), § 諸路萬戶府 (summarized from the Chinese, not quoted):

  | Post | Rank | Tablet |
  |---|---|---|
  | Upper myriarchy (7,000+ troops): *darughachi* and myriarch | 3a | tiger tally |
  | Its vice-myriarch | 3b | tiger tally |
  | Middle myriarchy (5,000+): *darughachi* and myriarch | 3b | tiger tally |
  | Its vice-myriarch | 4a | gold tablet (金牌) |
  | Lower myriarchy (3,000+): myriarch | 3b | tiger tally |
  | Its vice-myriarch | 4b | gold tablet |
  | Chiliarchies, upper and middle (700+ / 500+) | 4b to 5b | gold tablet |
  | Lower chiliarchy (300+) | 5b | gold tablet; its vice-chiliarch 6a, silver |
  | Centurions | 6b / 7b | silver tablet (銀牌) |

  The tablet follows the **official rank**, not a bare head-count. A "myriarchy" might hold 3,000 men. Coşkun's page: YS 91: 2310–12 (reported).
- **YS 102, *Xingfa zhi* 1** (§ 職制): "諸行省長官二員，給金虎符典軍": the two chief officials of a branch secretariat get **gold tiger tallies** to command troops. This is the nearest Yuan rank above the myriarch that I found. **No 100,000-man command tablet** and **no sun-and-moon or lion design** appear in the juans read.
- **YS 13, *Shizu benji* 10**, Zhiyuan 21 (1284), 3rd month 壬戌: "更定虎符", the tiger tallies were re-fixed. The text gives no detail.

### C3. Round tallies, later standard

- **YS 101** (opening of 站赤; [卷101](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7101)): relay warrants are called 鋪馬聖旨. "遇軍務之急，則又以金字圓符為信，銀字者次之": for urgent military business the credential is a **round tally with gold characters**, with silver characters the next grade. Kept by the palace inside the capital, and by Mongol senior officials outside it. Further entries: round tablets (圓牌) issued to branch secretariats in Zhiyuan 23 (1286). Yanyou 1 (1314): the Dianrui Directorate holds "金字圓牌" and 300-odd warrants, and 50 more gold-character round tablets were added in Zhida 4 (1311).
- **YS 103, *Xingfa zhi* 2** ([卷103](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7103)): "諸朝廷軍情大事，奉旨遣使者，佩以金字圓符給驛 … 諸王公主駙馬亦為軍情急務遣使者，佩以銀字圓符給驛". Imperial envoys on great military business wear **gold-character round tallies**. Envoys of princes, princesses and imperial sons-in-law on urgent military business wear **silver-character round tallies**. Smaller business needs only a sealed edict.
- **YS 88** (§ 典瑞院): the Dianrui office "掌寶璽、金銀符牌", keeper of the seals and gold and silver tallies. Founded as 符寶郎 posts in Zhongtong 1, 符寶局 in Zhiyuan 16, 典瑞監 in Zhiyuan 18 (also YS 11, Zhiyuan 18, 1st month).

### C4. Script

- **YS 6, *Shizu benji* 3**, Zhiyuan 6 (1269), 2nd month 己丑 ([卷006](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7006)): "詔以新製蒙古字頒行天下", edict promulgating the newly made Mongol script throughout the realm.
- **YS 202, *Shilao zhuan*, biography of 八思巴** ([卷202](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7202)): 至元六年，詔頒行於天下, then the edict text. Its operative clause: "自今以往，凡有璽書頒降者，並用蒙古新字，仍各以其國字副之". From now on every **sealed edict** issued shall use the new Mongol script, with each people's own script as a companion. **Tablets are not named.**
- **YS 6**, Zhiyuan 6, 7th month: Mongol-script schools in every *lu*. **YS 7**, Zhiyuan 9 (1272), 7th month: henceforth all edicts and decrees in Mongol script. **YS 8**, Zhiyuan 10 (1273), 1st month: patents (宣命) to be written in the national script.
- **YS 101**, Zhiyuan 8 (1271), 1st month: relay **warrants** (鋪馬劄子) had at first used the Mongol script, which station staff could not read. Horse numbers were to be drawn as pictures and stamped with the Secretariat seal. This concerns paper warrants, not tablets.
- **YS 11, *Shizu benji* 8**, Zhiyuan 18 (1281), 10th month 己亥 ([卷011](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%85%83%E5%8F%B2/%E5%8D%B7011)): "易所賜安南國畏吾字虎符，以國字書之". The **tiger tally** earlier given to Annam, inscribed in **Uighur script**, is to be replaced by one written in the **national ('Phags-pa) script**. This is the only *Yuan shi* entry I found that changes a **tablet's** script. It is dated 1281, not 1269, and concerns one named tally.
- Searched and not found in YS 4–17, 88, 91, 92, 98, 101–103 and 202: any entry, at 1269 or after, ordering tablets in general to be re-inscribed in the new script. YS 1–3, 18–47 and the other monographs were **not searched**. "Not found" is limited to the juans listed.

## D. Modern specialist studies

### D1. Atwood 2023, Rubin Museum — read

Christopher P. Atwood, "Mongol Messenger's Badge (Paiza or Gerege) in Pakpa Script: Signs of Authority in the Mongol Empire," *Project Himalayan Art*, Rubin Museum of Art, 2023. [Essay](https://rubinmuseum.org/projecthimalayanart/essays/mongol-messengers-badge-paiza-or-gerege-in-pakpa-script); [PDF](https://projecthimalayanart.rubinmuseum.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Mongol-Messengers-Badge-Paiza-or-Gerege-in-Pakpa-Script-I-Project-Himalayan-Art.pdf). The essay is about Met 1993.256. Points read:

- **Two types.** Messengers' paizas are for the post roads (*jam*) only. A more prestigious type goes to commanders (*noyat*), tributary rulers, *darughachin*, *ortaq* merchants and clergy, and permits requisition from the civilian population.
- **Ranking.** "Paizas for commanders were ranked by materials and design, ranging from the highest, in gold with a tiger head (*bars terigütü*) inscribed on it, to the lower-ranked ones, in plain gold or silver." "In many cases … they were actually composed of base metals, with at most silver or gold inlay."
- **Gerfalcon.** Qubilai's "first reform, initiated in 1261, established a new level of urgent post-road paizas, marked not by a tiger's head but by a gyrfalcon." Messengers' paizas changed from oblong to round, the "round tallies" (*yuanfu*). Post roads elsewhere in the empire kept the oblong form.
- **Script.** Between 1269 and 1271, among Qubilai's universalizing reforms, the square script was "proclaimed by Qubilai Khan in 1269 as the new script for the empire". This reform is "visible in this paiza". Surviving Pakpa samples are "mostly official pieces, such as the paiza shown here".
- **Object data.** The essay's own caption reads: late 13th–early 14th century; iron with silver inlay; 18.1 × 11.4 cm; Met 1993.256. The essay says the tiger's head is shown "in the molding on the object presented here".
- **Inconsistency on the same page.** The Rubin glossary panel says the script "was invented in 1270" and that "In 1271, Qubilai decreed it the official script". Atwood's essay text says 1269. The glossary is unsigned site boilerplate; the *Yuan shi* (C4) supports 1269.
- **Further reading** (not seen): Atwood 2004, *Encyclopedia* pp. 258–59 (post roads) and 519–20 (square script). Dang Baohai, "The Paizi of the Mongol Empire," *Zentralasiatische Studien* 30 (2001), 31–62, and 32, 7–10. Kara 2005, pp. 51–62. Doerfer, *TMEN* II, 239–41.

### D2. Jakhadai Chimeddorji (齊木德道爾吉) — read

Jakhadai Chimeddorji (齐木德道尔吉), "西南大学历史博物馆藏元代蒙古语八思巴文牌符解读" (Explain the plates of Yuan Dynasty engraved with Phags-pa script that stored in History Museum of Southwest University). Inner Mongolia University. Received 2007-12-11. Read as the [sinoss.net PDF](https://www.sinoss.net/uploadfile/2010/1130/8831.pdf), 2010 upload, 13 pp. with plates. **The journal of publication was not identified**, so this may be a preprint.

- **Two objects examined in person** in the Southwest University History Museum (Chongqing), collected in the late 1950s by Deng Ziqin at the Cho'erji (Tangcuo'erji) monastery, Aba, Sichuan. Museum labels read "元朝颁赐阿坝塘错尔基寺".
  - Oblong brass tablet, 25 × 8.2 cm, five lines of Mongolian in 'Phags-pa, the same text as the Inner Mongolia gold and silver tablets.
  - Round tablet, "copper-tin alloy" per the museum, iron in the author's view, 18.4 × 11.7 cm overall, tiger-head finial. Raised 'Phags-pa on both faces, one side mirror-reversed, with red seal-paste residue.
- **Reporting Dang Baohai 2004** (党宝海, "蒙古帝国的牌符——以实物为中心," *Ouya xuekan* 4 (2004), 183–203; also Dang 2001, *ZAS* 31, 31–62). **Reported; neither Dang article seen.**
  - Dang surveys **twenty** surviving tablets in three classes: relay tablets (乘驛牌), office tablets (職官牌) and curfew tablets (夜禁牌).
  - From the *Jingshi dadian*, *Yuan dianzhang* and *Yuan shi*: rank 3 wears the tiger tally, ranks 4–5 gold, ranks 6–7 silver.
  - Only the myriarch's tally has the crouching tiger. The chiliarch's gold and the centurion's silver are plain, undecorated.
  - The surviving oblong 'Phags-pa silver and gold tablets are office tablets issued to military officers **after Zhiyuan 15 (1278)**: silver for centurions (ranks 6–7), gold for chiliarchs (ranks 4–5).
  - Yuan relay tablets were round, with gold or silver characters, per *Jingshi dadian*, 站赤 1. Before the 'Phags-pa round tablets, the Mongols' relay tablets were **haiqing tablets**; with the promulgation of the 'Phags-pa script the haiqing tablet was **replaced** by the 'Phags-pa round tablet.
- **Catalogue of surviving 'Phags-pa tablets** (the author's list, from Dang and others; see § F).
- **Caution on authenticity**, reported in § G.

## E. Rashīd al-Dīn (`thackston1998rashiduddin`) — searched

I searched the OCR text of Thackston's translation (archive.org item `rashiduddin-thackston`, djvu text) for *paiza*, *lion*, *tablet*, *gerfalcon*, *sunqur* and *moon*. Twenty-odd *paiza* passages exist, all grants and recalls. Examples: under Ögedei, Khurasan governors given "a gold paiza and two decrees sealed with the red seal" (near MS marker [M109]). Under Möngke, "Every paiza and yarligh was taken back" from princes who had issued them freely, and paizas were not to be given to merchants. **No rank-by-metal ladder, no gerfalcon tablet and no lion's-head tablet** turned up. Ghazan's paiza regulations (Yule § B3) were **not located** in this OCR. The OCR has no printed page numbers, so locations are by section and MS marker only. Status: the Persian rank system is **not seen** first-hand.

The State Historical Museum page (§ F, GIM) quotes Rashīd al-Dīn for "an elongated paiza and on its end an image of the moon" for post riders. **Reported**; translation and locus not given.

Notes on the table:

- Waugh renders the Minusinsk inscription as "may the name of Mongke Khan be revered". Chimeddorji reads *mong-ka* as "eternal" (*möngke*), qualifying heaven, with the khan unnamed (*qa'an ne-re*). Schmidt's version, given by Yule, likewise has "eternal heaven" and "Khagan". Treat Waugh's "Mongke Khan" as a probable misreading; it should not be used to date #2 to 1251–59.
- No **gold** tablet with a lion, sun or moon, and no tablet with a gerfalcon figure, appears among the objects found. The sun-and-moon motif does appear on #5 (Uzbek, Golden Horde, 1312–41; GIM) and, per Coşkun, on #13. Neither is a Yuan rank tablet.
- Met 1993.256 is a **round relay tablet** (YS 101 / 103 class). It is not a rank tablet of the YS 98 ladder.

## G. Authenticity caution

[chinanews.com, 12 Jan 2011](https://www.chinanews.com/cul/2011/01-12/2782852.shtml), interview with Heqiyeletu (賀其葉勒圖), director of the Inner Mongolia University Museum of Ethnology. Read. After a CCTV feature on the museum's gold tablet in 2006, many 'Phags-pa tablets were "discovered", many of them fakes with misformed letters. The reporter handled the genuine gold tablet: about 25 cm long, nearly 10 cm wide, soft and uneven, with 張字九十六號 on the reverse by the suspension hole. The Institute's silver tablet was then on loan to the Met. This is a news report, not a catalogue. It bears on any tablet whose only record is a sale or a press report.
