1. Ceylon shark-charmers (1856 vs. 1885)
Verdict: unresolved — the two dates belong to two separately under-cited claims; nothing I could inspect reconciles them.
Fernando (2022), "Seeing Like the Sea," Past & Present 254(1) — DIRECTLY INSPECTED (full open-access HTML at academic.oup.com/past/article/254/1/127/6373535). Section IV reads: "In a disavowal of this ritual practice, charmers were banned by the government in 1856 and did not feature in the twilight decades of the industry in Ceylon."[121]
Footnote 121 — seen only via two independent secondary extractions (a ResearchGate PDF scrape and the article's own PDF scrape), not re-verified by my own fetch of the footnote list (it fell past the character range I retrieved): "Ostroff and Eaton both give context to the role of the charmers at the fishery, which pre-dates the years studied here: Ostroff, 'Beds of Empire', 133."
Key finding: note 121 names no administrative record and gives no source specifically for "1856" — it cites Ostroff p.133 only for general background on the charmer figure, and says that background "pre-dates the years studied here" (Fernando's own period is 1800–1925). So the 1856 date is effectively unsourced in Fernando's own apparatus, as far as I can verify.
Kunz & Stevenson (1908), The Book of the Pearl — DIRECTLY INSPECTED via the full-text Project Gutenberg e-text (no. 63299, a transcription of the 1908 Century Co. edition). My own fetch located the passage between Gutenberg page-anchor markers 116 and 117 — i.e., printed pp. 115–116, matching the prompt's citation — in Ch. VI, "The Pearl Fisheries of Ceylon." It states shark-charmers (kadal-kotti, Tamil; hai-banda, Hindustani) worked the fishery "until 1885"; recounts Marco Polo's c.1294 account of their one-twentieth share of the catch; notes a decline under the Portuguese and again under the British; cites Cordiner's 1807 account; and states that government first limited charmers' pay to one oyster per diver per day, then barred them from demanding anything — closing: "And, finally, in 1885, the shark-charmers were done away with entirely, after having exacted their toll for upward of six centuries at least." No footnote or administrative citation accompanies the 1885 statement (confirmed directly: the nearby footnote markers, 144 and 145, attach to the one-twentieth figure and the Cordiner quotation, not to 1885).
What the two claims actually say (not inferred): Fernando frames 1856 as a government "ban." Kunz & Stevenson frame 1885 as charmers being "done away with" after a stepwise cut to their customary in-kind pay — not explicitly called a ban, no legal instrument named. These could be the same event under a garbled date, two distinct administrative actions (an 1856 prohibition later followed by a final 1885 termination), or one/both authors working without a verified primary date. I found no primary Ceylon administrative record (SLDNA, Ceylon Sessional Papers, Government Gazette) online establishing either date — see Inaccessible Sources. No reconciliation should be asserted.
Natasha Eaton (2016), "In Search of Pearlescence," DOI 10.1177/1359183515624453 — INACCESSIBLE (SAGE paywall; only abstract/bibliographic metadata found, confirmed by repeated "No full-text available" notices on ResearchGate). I cannot report what Eaton says about the charmers or any date.
Ostroff, "The Beds of Empire" (2016 Penn PhD thesis), p.133 — INACCESSIBLE at the cited page. I located front-matter/acknowledgments text of the open-access thesis (via a repository PDF) but did not reach p.133; whatever it says about the charmers is unverified by me.
Safe manuscript wording: "Accounts differ on when shark-charmers stopped working the Ceylon pearl fishery: one scholarly source gives 1856 as the year the government banned the practice; an older reference work gives 1885 as the year it was finally discontinued, after the government had progressively cut back the charmers' traditional pay. Neither source's own footnotes tie the date to a dated administrative record, so the date should not be stated as certain, and the two sources should not be presented as describing the same single event."
2. Historical Yunnan crocodilians (Wen Huan-ran et al. 1980)
Verdict: INACCESSIBLE — full text not located; no record-by-record mapping is possible.
Target source: Wen Huan-ran (文焕然), He Yeheng (何业恒), Huang Zhujian (黄祝坚) et al., "历史时期中国马来鳄分布的变迁及其原因的初步研究" [A preliminary study of changes in the historical distribution of the Malayan gharial/false gharial in China and its causes], Journal of East China Normal University (Natural Science) 1980, no. 3, pp. 109–121.
I confirmed the article's exact citation — title, authors, journal, year, issue, and pp. 109–121 — through several independent secondary bibliographies (a 2018 retrospective essay on Wen Huan-ran in a Chinese newspaper, a historical-geography bibliography index, and citation lists in later compilations edited by Wen's son Wen Rongsheng). These agree with each other and distinguish this crocodilian article from Wen's unrelated companion pieces (a 1981 Yangtze-alligator article, a 1981 rhinoceros article, and the unrelated 1980 forestry piece the prompt warns against). But I could not find the article's full text, a scan, or any reproduction of its content anywhere I could fetch. I therefore cannot map any individual record (date, place, textual/physical evidence, taxonomic identification) — doing so without the text would mean inventing content, which I have not done.
I located a later compiled volume (中国历史时期植物与动物变迁研究) that reprints some of Wen's shorter animal-geography essays, but I could not confirm whether it includes this specific 1980 journal article, so I cannot treat it as a substitute.
Per the prompt's caution: I made no attempt to infer historical crocodile range from modern Crocodylus/Tomistoma/Gavialis distribution or from fossil evidence — I simply have no primary content from this source to extrapolate from or to responsibly report.
Safe manuscript wording: "A 1980 Chinese academic study (Wen Huan-ran et al.) addressed the historical distribution of crocodilians in China, including in Yunnan, but its specific findings (dates, locations, textual or physical evidence) could not be verified for this retelling and should not be stated as fact without consulting the original article."
3. Kerman turquoise (Weisgerber 2004) and the 1390–1430 date
Verdict: INACCESSIBLE at the primary chapter; the Polo/1390–1430 claim traces only to a tertiary paraphrase, not to Weisgerber's own sentence.
Target source: Gerd Weisgerber (2004), "The turquoise mines at Nishapur," in T. Stöllner, R. Slotta & A. Vatandoust (eds.), Persian Antiques Splendor: Mining Crafts and Archaeology in Ancient Iran (Bochum: Deutsches Bergbau-Museum), pp. 502–509. INACCESSIBLE — this is a German mining-museum exhibition-catalogue chapter; searches turned up only citation listings (e.g., in the Ruhr-Universität Bochum archaeometallurgy group's own publication list, and in a UCL open-access repository metadata record), none reproducing the chapter's content.
The claim under audit — that the chapter names Shahr-i Babak, Marco Polo, and the date range 1390–1430 — is not something I found in Weisgerber's own text. I found it only in a 2021 GIA Gems & Gemology article, "Persian Turquoise: The Ancient Treasure of Neyshabur," which states, citing "(Weisgerber, 2004)": "...the Shahr-i Babak deposit in western Kerman Province, the latter of which was mentioned in Marco Polo's Book of the Wonders of the World between 1390 and 1430." I directly inspected this GIA 2021 article (confirmed via three independent re-postings of identical wording: ResearchGate PDF, Academia.edu PDF, and the GIA journal's own page), so the attribution to Weisgerber is confirmed as what the citing article says — but it is that article's paraphrase, not a quotation of Weisgerber's own sentence.
I cannot confirm from Weisgerber's own chapter: (a) whether it names "Shahr-i Babak" by that name, (b) whether it names Marco Polo at all, or (c) what "1390–1430" actually denotes there. The span is consistent with being a date range for a specific (unnamed) manuscript witness of Polo's text — composition in 1298 does not rule out a later manuscript bearing such a date, as the prompt notes — but without the chapter itself this is speculation about what Weisgerber meant, not a finding.
Shirdam et al. 2021 citing this chapter — I was not able to separately verify the Shirdam citation's own wording against Weisgerber; see Inaccessible Sources.
Safe manuscript wording: "A later mining-history source (Weisgerber, 2004, as cited by a 2021 GIA turquoise study) is said to connect the Shahr-i Babak turquoise deposit to a copy of Marco Polo's account dated 1390–1430 — a manuscript-era date, not the date of Polo's own composition (c.1298). Weisgerber's chapter itself was not accessible for direct verification, so this connection should not be presented in the retelling as independently confirmed."
4. Socotra whale hunt — Ramusio 1559 and the F/Z manuscripts (optional check)
Verdict: core wording confirmed via a modern critical edition; the 1559 print and the F/Z manuscripts themselves were NOT independently inspected by me. Two of the prompt's citation details are corrected below.
Directly inspected: Eugenio Burgio & Serena Fornasiero, "Il terzo libro" (2011) — a critical-apparatus article/edition of Marco Polo's text, retrieved as a PDF from the Università Ca' Foscari Venezia IRIS repository.
Location correction: the discussion of ch. III.34 §2 (Socotra) sits at the article's internal page 6, corresponding to roughly p.130 of the article (paginated 125–152) — not p.133 as the prompt states.
Confirmed readings:
• Ramusio's text (cited as "R"): "...con alcuni ferri ch'hanno le barbe che, ficcati nella balena, non si possono più cavare, alli quali è attaccata una corda lunghissima con una bottesella che va sopra il mare... e la conducono al lito, dove li cavano fuori del ventre l'ambracano e della testa assai botte d'olio." This confirms bottesella for the line float and botte for the oil, as the prompt claims (note: botte here means casks/barrels of rendered oil, not the float).
• The Z witness (quoted in note 20): "...A capite vero superiori ipsius pali est ligata una funis grossa... Et in quolibet capite .l. passuum ipsius funis ligata est una boticula et unus asser." This confirms boticula and asser appear in Z, as claimed — though the article's own citation is "Z 123, §3, 17, 19–20, 28–29" (a span), not a bare "Z 123.19–20"; 19–20 is part of, not the whole of, that span.
Citation correction: the prompt's "F CLXXXIX.3" does not match the article. It cites the F witness at "F CLXXXXIX.3" (four X's = 199, not 189), quoting "Il hi naist l'anbre en grant quantité." I report what the article actually prints.
What I did NOT verify: I did not independently inspect the 1559 Ramusio print (Navigationi et Viaggi, vol. II, Venice: Giunti) — I located, but did not fetch, a link to an open "Marco Polo digitale" edition of that text (arts.units.it) that could confirm its folio numbering; this remains inaccessible at the folio level for me in this pass. Nor did I independently inspect manuscripts F or Z themselves — my knowledge of their readings here comes entirely from Burgio & Fornasiero's quotations, a modern scholarly edition reporting those readings, not the manuscripts. Their Socotra collation is presented as their own analysis (citing Milanesi and Mascherpa only for other, unrelated points in the same note), so the comparative work appears to be theirs — but I have not cross-checked their transcriptions against the primary witnesses.
Nothing I inspected suggests the article treats this whale/ambergris hunt as Polo's own eyewitness observation rather than inherited or compiled material — consistent with the prompt's caution against treating the reported hunt as independent observation by Polo.
Safe manuscript wording: "The 1559 Ramusio edition of Polo's account of Socotra describes whale-hunting with harpoons, a float ('bottesella') on a long line, and rendered oil ('botte'); a related manuscript tradition (Z) uses 'boticula' and 'asser' for similar gear. These details are drawn from a modern critical edition's apparatus (Burgio & Fornasiero, 2011) comparing several manuscript witnesses, not from direct inspection of the 1559 print or the underlying manuscripts. The hunt should be presented as part of the inherited textual tradition the account draws on, not as Polo's own eyewitness observation."
