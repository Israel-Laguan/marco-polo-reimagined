# V-1 — Period visual evidence for 13 illustrated scenes (2026-11-17)

**Method and limits (read first).** All reading was done through a web search/extract tool that returns page text and highlights. **No image was opened or seen for any item.** "What the image shows" below is therefore taken from catalogue text, captions or scholarship, and is marked as such. Access level for every row is **extract read** or **catalog metadata only**, never "original image seen". No BibTeX is supplied: no entry was verified against a publisher record. Searches were one to three queries per scene. NOT FOUND means "not found in this pass", not "does not exist". Nothing here shows that Marco Polo was present anywhere; these are objects for illustrators, not testimony.

**Reliability flags.** Several sources were weak: Grokipedia (AI-written), Baidu Baike English, stock-photo captions (Getty, Bridgeman, Meisterdrucke), a dealer catalogue, hobby/tourism pages. They are used only where named and flagged. Anything resting only on them is "unconfirmed".

### 1. I-7 Kerman raiders — village buildings; Mongol-era raider dress and arms in Persia

**A. Raider dress/arms.** Claim: Persian painting of the 1330s shows Mongol-period warriors and their gear.
- Harvard Art Museums **1955.167**, *Iskandar's Iron Cavalry Battles King Fur of Hind*, folio from the Great Ilkhanid (Demotte) Shahnama, Tabriz, c. 1330–1340; ink, colours, gold, silver on paper, 27 × 29 cm. Date is stylistic (c.), not inscribed. Record text read via a secondary page quoting the Harvard record. Subject is a Persian epic battle; dress is contemporary Ilkhanid convention, not a Kerman event.
- Nelson-Atkins **55-103**, *Afrasiyab Killing Naudar*, Great Mongol Shahnama, c. 1335 (Wikimedia Commons metadata; Commons has detail crops titled "Mongol back-flap hat"). Metadata only.
- Verdict: **supported** for "Ilkhanid-court painters show Mongol hats, armour and bows, c. 1330s"; **not found** for Kerman or for 1260–1295. These are 35–70 years after Marco; a Shahnama is epic, not reportage.
- Not verified: the Edinburgh *Jami' al-tawarikh*, Or Ms 20 (University of Edinburgh Library). Its catalogue gives 714 AH/1314 but says the Edinburgh fragment is undated and that the dated fragment is in the Khalili Collection; Wikipedia gives 1306–07 from a later inscription. I did not identify which folio shows Mongol soldiers. Record: https://archives.collections.ed.ac.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/145535 (extract read). The catalogue also notes folio 42/47 was lent to the Venice *Worlds of Marco Polo* exhibition.

**B. Village buildings.**
- BnF Arabe **5847**, al-Wasiti, *Maqamat* of al-Hariri, copied 634 AH/1237, probably Baghdad (BnF says "Mesopotamian"). Maqama 43, "Discussion near a village": per an annotation quoted on 1001inventions.com (originally Susan Douglass, CHNM item 241), a palm, village mosque and individual mud-brick houses with open doors. Second-hand description. Gallica IDs from Wikidata: btv1b10884660g and btv1b8422965p (not opened). Wikidata: https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q16641327. Folio numbers for this miniature: **not found**.
- Verdict: Iraq, 1237, not Kerman. **Disputed** as a proxy; use only as "Mesopotamian mud-brick village, 13th c."
- Cautious wording: "A Mongol-era Persian painter would have drawn raiders in the fur-trimmed hat and short close coat of the Ilkhanid court; we have no Kerman image."

### 2. I-9 Badakhshan — highland dress; mining tools
NOT FOUND. One query run. Results were geology and Bronze Age mining only (Wyart, Bariand and Stockton, "Lapis-Lazuli from Sar-e-Sang", *Gems & Gemology* 17.4, 1981, DOI 10.5741/GEMS.17.4.184, abstract read; Thomalsky, DAI project report, extract read). A tourism-style gem page says miners broke rock "by heating it with fire and then dousing it with cold water, a method recorded by later travelers": unattributed, unconfirmed, and not 13th-century evidence. No dated dress or tool. Best safe fallback: Marco's own text for the method, and a generic Central Asian highland look flagged as reconstruction.

### 3. I-10 Pamir — camp, tents, herders' dress
NOT FOUND. No dedicated query returned anything. (The Bactrian-camel survey in *J. Camel Practice & Research*, Aug 2025, is Tang and earlier.) Fallback: Ilkhanid/Mongol felt-tent scenes in the *Jami' al-tawarikh* family, flagged as steppe, not Pamir.

### 4. I-11 Lop desert — camels, loads, bells, water
**Lead (Mogao caves 3 and 465): not confirmed as caravan evidence.**
- Cave 3 and 465 are Yuan. Per Baidu Baike (English, weak), cave 3's lower walls carry over sixty figure paintings of weaving, chicken farming, herding, pottery, tiger taming, leather processing, rice-hulling; cave 465 is Tibetan-style esoteric. No caravan is mentioned. UW Dunhuang exhibition pages list cave 3 as Yuan (1271–1368), with a Thousand-Armed Avalokiteshvara on north and south walls (catalogue metadata): https://dunhuang.ds.lib.uw.edu/Dunhuang-Exhibitions/items/browse?collection=1&page=5&sort_field=added&sort_dir=a
- The caravan murals named in a CSSN article are caves **296 (Northern Zhou), 302 and 420 (Sui)**, and **61 (Five Dynasties)** per a gallery page: all centuries too early. Source: http://english.cssn.cn/skw_culture/202412/t20241225_5826987.shtml (extract read).
- Verdict: **contradicted** as stated.

**Nearest dated alternative (not Lop).** BnF Arabe 5847 (1237): camel caravans, "caravan stopping", "herd of camels" and pilgrim departure. Folio numbers come only from stock-photo captions (Getty: "f.19r departure of pilgrims", Bridgeman: "f.10", "f.51", "f.101 herd of camels"); **unverified**. Probably dromedaries, not the Bactrian camels of the Lop route. Unchecked.
Fallback: "No securely dated 1200–1350 image of a Taklamakan caravan was found; use 1237 Mesopotamian caravan kit for loads, bells and water skins, noting the camel species may differ."

### 5. II-5 paper-money workshop — blocks, plates, workers
- **CUHK lead: confirmed with a caveat.** Art Museum, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, accession **1988.0028**, two sets of bronze blocks and seals for *Zhiyuan tongxing baochao* notes (200 and 500 wen), "Yuan dynasty (late 13th–early 14th century)", gift of Bei Shan Tang. Large 200-wen block 21.7 × 14.9 × 0.8 cm, 1.51 kg; 500-wen block 25.2 × 17.2 × 1.1 cm, 2.91 kg. Dating is by type (Zhiyuan notes issued 1287), not by an inscription on the object. The museum's own text says these **might be illegal blocks used for forgeries** rather than official ones. Shows: heading in a row between two flaming pearls, floral ground, coin strings, Phags-pa legend, 79-character edict, seals. Record: https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/two-sets-of-blocks-and-seals-for-paper-notes/QwGBRgJAh47r9g (extract read).
- National Museum of China lists a "Copperplate for Printing *Zhiyuan Tongxing Baochao*" (Yuan) on its gallery page; no inventory number seen: https://www.chnmuseum.cn/portals/0/web/zt/gudai/en/detail7.html (catalogue metadata only).
- A bronze block (c. 1287) published by L. C. Goodrich, "A Bronze Block for the Printing of Chinese Paper Currency (ca. 1287)", *Museum Notes (American Numismatic Society)* 4 (1950): 127–130, is cited by a Harvard student Omeka exhibit (secondary). Holding institution and number not given there. Tokyo Currency Museum note-and-plate image appears only as a Wikimedia/Tontine caption.
- A stone "block" offered by Douglas Stewart Fine Books (ex Pelliot, "c. 1287") is a dealer claim, not evidence.
- Printing method: sources say woodblock then bronze (secondary pages give 1275; another gives 1272 for a 1-to-5 conversion). **Disputed/unverified.**
- **Workshop, workers: NOT FOUND.** No image of a workshop, and Marco's text gives only officials signing and sealing.

### 6. II-6 Pulisanghin (Lugou) bridge — 13th-century state
NOT FOUND (no image or description from 1200–1350 in this pass). Secondary text only: building began 1189, finished 1192 under the Jin; rebuilt 1698 under Kangxi; 11 arches, 266.5 m (Wikipedia; Visit Beijing). A mirror of an older Wikipedia text says some lions are Yuan and a few Jin. **Unconfirmed**; counts and attributions vary. Fallback: depict an 11-arch stone bridge with lions and no Qing stelae, and flag that the Jin/Yuan parapet detail is unattested here.

### 7. II-7 Kham dress
NOT FOUND. A lead only: the Metropolitan Museum's *Sacred Visions* (Kossak and Singer, 1998) page lists a "Mahasiddha Jnanatapa, Eastern Tibet, Kham, Riwoche monastery, ca. 1350" (catalogue metadata; accession number not seen; subject is a siddha, so it shows monastic/saintly dress, not lay Kham dress). Yuan-court donors in Mongol dress (e.g. the Met Yamantaka tapestry c. 1330–32) are not Kham. Source: https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/sacred-visions-early-paintings-from-central-tibet

### 8. II-8 Vochan 1277 — Pagan elephants and howdahs
NOT FOUND. Dagon University paper (Aye Aye Aung, *Research Journal* 9.1) describes elephant-head plinths on Pagan monuments, "estimated" 13th century, and says elephants appear in murals; it shows no howdahs. Gubyaukgyi (1113) is too early. The Harvard Shahnama folio above has Indian elephants, but a Persian painter's imagination, not Pagan. Do not use it for Burmese howdahs.

### 9. II-10 Song surrender — Southern Song officials' dress
**Lead corrected.** Huang Sheng's tomb (Fuzhou, buried 1243, found 1975) yielded 436 objects, 354 textiles, but she was a 17-year-old woman; her clothes are women's dress. Holder: Fujian Museum (no inventory numbers seen). Source: Baidu Baike (weak) and Zenodo conservation record https://zenodo.org/records/6228334 (abstract read). A 2024 *SCMP* item reports a digital reconstruction by Liu Anding's team.
**Better match for officials:** the tomb of **Zhao Boyun** (1155–1216), Huangyan, Zhejiang, excavated 2016: an eighth-rank official's burial dress, 16 garments in 8 layers, 76 costume items in all. Source: Feng et al., "Study and Digital Restoration of Costumes Unearthed from the Tomb of Zhao Boyun", *Fibres & Textiles in Eastern Europe* 31(5), 2023, 56–65, DOI 10.2478/ftee-2023-0045 (full text read via ResearchGate extract). Caveat: burial date 1216 is 60 years before the 1276 surrender; "buried at the rank of eighth-rank official" is the paper's statement. Holding institution/inventory not seen; exhibited at Huangyan Museum and China National Silk Museum.
Verdict: **supported** for Southern Song official/gentry dress c. 1216–1243; **not found** for 1270s court dress.

### 10. II-12 Yangzi trackers — river junk
**Lead refuted.** Freer **F1954.21** is Cheng Qi (attrib.), *Tilling Rice, after Lou Shou*, handscroll, ink and colour on paper, 32.7 × 1049.8 cm, Yuan, mid-to-late 13th c. It shows rice cultivation, not boats or trackers. Documentation PDF: https://asia.si.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/song-yuan-f1954-21_documentation.pdf (extract read).
**Related, unconfirmed as trackers:** Freer **F1916.539**, *The Shu River*, trad. Li Gonglin, Southern Song, 13th c., ink on paper; a place-name scroll of the Min and Yangzi (Zhongzhou to Diaoshi Rapids). Whether it shows boats or trackers: not stated in what I read. https://archive.asia.si.edu/SongYuan/F1916.539/F1916-539.Documentation.pdf
Also named in a review article (Kim, *The houseboat in pre-modern China*): Xia Gui (attrib.), *Ten-thousand Li of the Yangzi*, NPM Taipei, which shows a boat leaving the gorges, and Zhang Zeduan's *Qingming* (12th c.). Not read or dated by me.
Hull evidence: the **Quanzhou ship**, found 1974 at Houzhu, Quanzhou Bay; Song, per the Quanzhou Maritime Museum (a seagoing ship, not a Yangzi junk); length 24.2 m and width 9.15 m remain, watertight bulkheads, multi-layer planking (Baidu Baike). "Late 13th century" is from a merchandising blog. Wreck-date coins: not seen. **Disputed** as a river-junk model.

### 11. III-3 Sumatra — dress, buildings, boats
- National Museum of Indonesia (Jakarta), **D.198A** (inscribed base) and **D.198-6469** (statue), Amoghapasa Lokesvara, Padang Roco inscription dated Saka 1208 = **1286**, andesite, 163 cm; second inscription added 1347 by Adityavarman. Inventory numbers from Grokipedia (weak) and Wikipedia's *Amoghapasa inscription* (extract; confirms D.198-6469). **Made in Java and shipped to Sumatra**: not "Sumatran" evidence.
- Bhairava statue (Adityavarman), Padang Roco, 14th c., National Museum: a photo site lists it with a "detailed clothing pattern". Reign after about 1347; inland Dharmasraya, not the Samudra/Basma coast Marco names.
- Gravestone of Malik al-Salih, AH 696/1297 (Archnet, infoaceh, Wikipedia): dated and Sumatran-sited, but a Gujarati-style import, with no dress, building or boat.
- Verdict: **partly answered**. Boats, houses: NOT FOUND.

### 12. III-4 Adam's Peak — pilgrim dress; chains
- Dress: NOT FOUND. Gadaladeniya (founded 1344 per its inscription) door paintings show kinnaras and "flower maidens", not pilgrims (tourism site, weak).
- Chains: Sri Lanka's UNESCO tentative-list text (https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5531/, extract read) says Parakramabahu II's minister Devaprathiraja installed iron chains (reign 1236–1271) and quotes Ibn Battuta on the chains. **Text, not object**; the same page says remains "can be dated to a period not before the twelfth century". It also says Marco Polo visited the peak in 1286 and saw chains; that statement is **not supported** and should not be repeated.
- Cautious wording: "By the 14th century iron chains on stanchions aided the climb (Ibn Battuta); the 13th-century origin is a Sinhalese tradition."

### 13. III-6 Malabar corsairs — west-Indian coast ships
BnF Arabe 5847, f. **119v** (Maqama 39), "Abu Zayd and al-Harith sailing from Basra to Oman", 1237. Folio attribution is consistent across Getty, Bridgeman and a book extract titled *Classic Ships of Islam: From Mesopotamia to the Indian Ocean* (the extract I read did not show author or publisher; it captions the plate "Baghdad 635/1237, fol. 119v", while Wikipedia gives the colophon as 634 AH, so the AH year is **disputed**). Shows a Gulf/Indian Ocean sailing vessel with crew, steering, anchor and fish. Not Malabar, not a pirate ship; the planking/sewing question is unresolved. NOT FOUND for any securely Malabar ship.

## Disagreements and where evidence stops
- **Dates of the Edinburgh *Jami' al-tawarikh***: 1306–07 (later inscription) versus 1314 (EUL catalogue); the Edinburgh fragment itself is undated.
- **Block printing history**: 1272, 1275 and 1287 appear in secondary sources for the switch to bronze or for specific issues.
- **CUHK blocks**: museum text itself raises a forgery possibility.
- Evidence stops at: no image seen; Arabe 5847 folio numbers and Gallica IDs only from aggregators; no inventory numbers for Zhao Boyun, Huang Sheng or the Quanzhou ship.

## Unresolved
1. Open the Gallica Arabe 5847 images (ff. 10, 19r, 101, 119v, and Maqama 43) and read the BnF notice.
2. Check the Met *Legacy of Genghis Khan* catalogue for Ilkhanid arms and dress dated 1260–1300.
3. Look for 13th-century Pagan murals with elephants (Luce, *Old Burma–Early Pagan*) and the Hamburg Pagan paintings.
4. Verify the Goodrich 1950 block and the Tokyo Currency Museum plate record.
5. Identify real Yangzi tracking images (Xia Gui scroll at NPM Taipei; Li Song, *Bachuan chuxia tu*, cited by Kim).
6. Confirm Marco did not climb Adam's Peak (he describes it from report); check the *Milione* before using the chain item.

## Best safe fallback
Use dated Mesopotamian and Ilkhanid paintings (Arabe 5847; Great Mongol Shahnama) for caravans, ships and Mongol dress, the CUHK/NMC bronze blocks for scene 5, Zhao Boyun's tomb for officials' dress, and label everything else as reconstruction rather than period evidence.
