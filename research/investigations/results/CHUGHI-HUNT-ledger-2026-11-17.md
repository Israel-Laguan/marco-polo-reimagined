# Chughi ascetics (Polo III.20) and Yuan hunting seasons — source ledger (2026-11-17)

**Method and limits (read first).** Everything below was read through a web search/extract tool and `curl` downloads of plain-text transcriptions. **No manuscript image, printed page or physical object was seen.** Access levels are therefore only "edition/transcription or full text read", "extract/OCR read", "abstract/review read" or "NOT SEEN". The Gutenberg site was down for direct download, so Yule–Cordier was read from tool-returned extracts and from a full-text mirror (fullbooks.com, Vol. II, part 10). Page numbers appear only where a mirror printed them (Yule–Cordier II, printed pp. 363–369 via a bookrags mirror of the Gutenberg text); for everything else the locator is a chapter, juan or heading, not a page. No BibTeX is given for anything I did not see title-page data for. NOT FOUND / NOT SEEN means "not found in this pass", not "does not exist". Nothing here shows Marco Polo was present at any scene; Polo's Lar/Chughi passage is, by its own wording, report ("they say"), and Yule himself calls the Lar material "very obscure … through erroneous notions".

## Q1(a). Mercury–sulphur elixirs c. 1300

**Polo's claim (the thing being checked).** Yule–Cordier, *Travels of Marco Polo*, Bk III ch. XX, "Concerning the Province of Lar whence the Brahmins come" (printed p. 364 in the mirror): the Chughi, "properly Abraiaman", live 150–200 years on rice and milk, and drink "a potion of sulphur and quicksilver mixt together … twice every month … from their childhood." Access: **full text read** (extract of Gutenberg #12410; mirror fullbooks.com Vol. II part 10). Type: manuscript/translation reading, not an eyewitness claim. Polo does not say he saw the drink taken. The F-family text read by Yule has "they say".

### F1. Mercury is ingested for long life in Sanskrit Siddha/alchemical texts by c. 1200
- **Verdict: supported** (for the practice; **not** for the exact Polo recipe).
- **Source.** David Gordon White, *The Alchemical Body: Siddha Traditions in Medieval India* (Chicago and London: University of Chicago Press, 1996; ISBN 0-226-89497-5 cloth, 0-226-89499-1 paper), read as search extracts from epdf.pub and vdoc.pub copies (not the publisher; these are unauthorised mirrors). Locators: ch. 1 §2 "Tāntrikas, Siddhas, and Yogis"; chapter on "Tantric and Siddha Alchemical Literature"; passages on *Rasārṇava* (RA) ch. 18 and *rasāyana*. Printed page numbers **not visible** in extracts.
- **What it says.** In alchemical usage *rasāyana* means a mercurial elixir; mercury is Śiva's seed and sulphur/mica the Goddess's blood; the RA's final chapters prescribe ingesting processed ("killed", ash-like) mercury, in pill form (*guṭikā*) held in the mouth, for immortality. The *Khecarīvidyā* 4.13 (cited by White, MS NNA 5–6568, extract) recommends at daybreak "a decoction of mercury, the sap of the silk-cotton tree, and sulfur" with honey, sugar and butter, so that "in the space of six months one becomes unaging and immortal." Date of the *Khecarīvidyā*: **not given in the extract.**
- **Corroboration of dates.** *Rasārṇava* dated c. 10th–12th c., "about the twelfth century" (Meulenbeld's dating as reported in the *Asian Medicine* 8.1 article below); quoted in the *Raseśvara-darśana* of the *Sarvadarśanasaṅgraha* (Mādhava, 14th c.) per a Sanskrit-sources survey (tantriklaboratories.com PDF, extract). *Rasendracūḍāmaṇi* (Somadeva) "probably 12th or 13th c."; *Rasaprakāśasudhākara* (Yaśodhara) 13th c. (same survey). **Disputed:** the *Rasaratnākara* of Nityanātha is 13th c. in some lists, 15th c. in an annotated bibliography (academia.edu "Rasa and Rasaśāstra", extract); *Rasaratnasamuccaya* is "c. 13th c." there but "about the sixteenth century" in the Brill article. Use none of these as a firm pre-1300 date for a specific text without checking Meulenbeld's *History of Indian Medical Literature* (NOT SEEN).
- **Access.** Extract/OCR read. **Limitations.** White is a modern interpreter; he reads Polo and Bernier as *evidence* that yogis were alchemists, and his notes put Polo's Chughi "on the Malabar coast", which is more specific than Polo's text (Polo puts Lar "towards the west" of St Thomas's tomb; Yule: "very obscure"). The RA processes mercury; Polo's "quicksilver and sulphur mixed … a drink" sounds like crude raw mercury plus sulphur, which White says is not what the texts prescribe.
- **Cautious wording.** "Indian alchemical and Siddha texts of roughly the 12th–13th centuries prescribe elixirs of processed mercury, usually bound with sulphur; Polo's account of a mercury-and-sulphur drink is a garbled but recognisable echo."

### F2. Mercury–sulphur preparations in 13th-c. medical (not alchemical) literature
- **Verdict: supported.**
- **Source.** "Perfect Medicine: …" on mercury in classical South Asian medicine, *Asian Medicine* 8.1 (2013), pp. 15–?, Brill, https://brill.com/view/journals/asme/8/1/article-p15_2.xml. Author name and end page **not shown in the extract**; abstract and body extract read.
- **What it says.** Abstract: "with one notable exception, it was only from the thirteenth century onwards that ways of processing mercury were developed or adopted from alchemical sources in ayurvedic medicine." On the *Śārṅgadharasaṃhitā* (13th c.): "A common denominator of all recipes is the occurrence of sulphur as one of the ingredients. Mercury is ingested mixed with honey or ghee, as a beverage, or in the form of pills." Same article says the *Rasārṇava* is "about the twelfth century" (n. 59, citing Meulenbeld 1999–2002, IIA, p. 684).
- **Access.** Abstract + extract read. **Limitation.** Medical tonics and aphrodisiacs, not specifically a yogi longevity regime; no "twice a month".
- **Wording.** "By the thirteenth century mercury-and-sulphur preparations were being taken by mouth in Indian medicine."

### F3. Sanskrit commentary on Patañjali treats mercury as a rasāyana
- **Verdict: supported (pre-1300, but late classical commentary).**
- **Source.** Pines and Gelblum, "Al-Bīrūnī's Arabic Version of Patañjali's *Yogasūtra*: a translation of the fourth chapter and a comparison with related texts", *BSOAS* 52.2 (1989 issue; the search record shows an online date of 2009), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/abs/albirunis-arabic-version-of-patanjalis-yogasutra-a-translation-of-the-fourth-chapter-and-a-comparison-with-related-texts/A1A057F788DC5DA85EA22703A242178D. Extract read. (I did not confirm the volume year; the article text refers to earlier instalments in *BSOAS* xxix and the other bibliographic details come from the same extract.)
- **Passage seen.** Bhoja on YS 4.1: *oṣadhi-siddhayo yathā pāradādi-rasāyanādy-upayogāt* — "the perfections of herbs [arise] as, for instance, by the use of … the elixir of life consisting of e.g. quicksilver." Vyāsa's own comment on *oṣadhi* is also read as *rasāyana*, glossed there as the "elixir of life" offered by demons' daughters. Bhoja's date is not given in the extract (NOT SEEN).
- **Limitation.** Commentarial gloss, not evidence of what any 1300 ascetic drank.

### F4. al-Bīrūnī (c. 1030)
- **Verdict: not found for mercury–sulphur; supported only for "Indians have an alchemy-like art, rasāyana".**
- **Source.** Sachau, *Alberuni's India* (London, 1888/1910), I, ch. on Patañjali etc., as quoted in the Pines–Gelblum extract and in Ray's *History of Hindu Chemistry* (archive.org, extract). Quoted: "They have a science similar to alchemy which is quite peculiar to them. They call it Rasāyana, a word composed with rasa, i.e. gold. It means an art which is restricted to certain operations, drugs, and compound medicines, most of which are taken from plants." Also, from the Arabic *Kitāb Bātanjal*, rasāyana is "a fourth part, based on false beliefs … procedures involving drugs similar to those used in alchemy." The *Iranica* entry says Bīrūnī "devalues" it as alchemy.
- **What it does not say.** I saw no mention of mercury or sulphur. The 2018 *HSSA* introduction (oro.open.ac.uk/54123) notes that his initial account "seems rather to describe the rasāyana of the Sanskrit medical classics than … the alchemical works" and that he then retells stories of gold-making.
- **Access.** Extract read, not the text itself. **Wording.** "al-Bīrūnī (c. 1030) knew rasāyana as an Indian alchemy-like art, but I could not find him naming mercury and sulphur."

### F5. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa (c. 1333–47; Rihla compiled 1355)
- **Verdict: not found for mercury; supported for "yogis make pills".**
- **Source.** Quoted in a blog essay (qcurtius.com, "The Indian Rope Trick", extract; translation not named): "The people say that they make up pills, one of which they take for a given number of days or months, and during that time they require no food or drink." White's note on this (vdoc.pub extract, truncated) says Ibn Baṭṭūṭa "describes yogis, encountered near Gwalior and in coastal Tamil Nadu, who specialized in the preparation of mineral pills" and cites a 3-vol. *Voyages* translation (rest NOT SEEN).
- **Access.** Secondary quotation; the Arabic and a full translation **not seen**. **Limitation.** "The people say" is hearsay inside the Rihla; the Rihla itself is a compiled text (Ibn Juzayy) of c. 1355. The same essay has the yogis removing their hair "with ashes" — a depilatory, not ash-daubing; do not use it for Q1(b).
- **Wording.** "Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, writing of the 1330s, reports that yogis were said to take pills that replaced food, without saying what was in them (in what I could read)."

### F6. Arghun Khan and the Indian bakhshis (1290–91)
- **Verdict: supported as Yule's report; Rashīd al-Dīn's own passage NOT SEEN.**
- **Source.** Yule–Cordier II, Bk III ch. XX, Note 5 (printed p. 368): "Arghun Khan of Persia (see Prologue, ch. xvii.), who was much given to alchemy … had asked of the Indian Bakhshis how they prolonged their lives to such an extent. They assured him that a mixture of sulphur and mercury was the Elixir of Longevity. Arghun accordingly took this precious potion for eight months; and died shortly after!" Yule does not give a Rashīd locator in the text I read. Wikipedia's "Arghun" and a student web page repeat it as Rashīd al-Dīn's account; Wikipedia's extract names no folio or edition. Treat as **second-hand through Yule**.
- **Independent witness?** Partly. Rashīd (if confirmed) is a different author writing in Persian c. 1307–16, so it is independent of Polo's text, but it concerns a Mongol court in Iran, not South India, and "Indian bakhshis" there is Yule's reading.
- **Wording.** "Yule notes that the Persian historian Rashīd al-Dīn is said to describe the Ilkhan Arghun taking a sulphur-and-mercury elixir from Indian ascetics before his death in 1291 (not checked in Rashīd)."

### F7. Later Indian-vernacular and European echoes (not 1300 evidence)
- Bernier (17th c.) on jogis preparing mercury: cited by Yule Note 5 and White. Ma Huan (Cochin, early 15th c.) on "Chokis" who go unclothed, with a conch and plaited hair: quoted in Yule–Cordier Note 5 addendum from *JRAS* 1896 p. 343. Neither is c. 1300.

### Q1(a) unresolved
- No Indian text or traveller I could open describes a **twice-monthly** mercury-and-sulphur drink taken **from childhood**; those details rest on Polo.
- Is Polo's "Lar" Gujarat, Tamil country or elsewhere? Yule: "very obscure". Do not place the Chughi in "South India" without hedging.
- Not checked: Meulenbeld, *History of Indian Medical Literature* (for dates); the Persian text of Rashīd al-Dīn on Arghun; Gibb/Defrémery–Sanguinetti for Ibn Baṭṭūṭa; Ma Huan in Mills/Phillips.
- **Safe fallback:** "Polo reports, at second hand, that some long-lived Indian ascetics drank a mixture of quicksilver and sulphur; mercury elixirs were a real feature of medieval Indian Siddha alchemy."

## Q1(b). The naked, ox-worshipping, ash-daubed ascetics

**Polo's text (seen).** Yule–Cordier II, Bk III ch. XX (fullbooks mirror): "certain members of this Order who lead the most ascetic life in the world, going stark naked; and these worship the Ox. Most of them have a small ox of brass or pewter or gold which they wear tied over the forehead. Moreover they take cow-dung and burn it, and make a powder thereof; and make an ointment of it, and daub themselves withal, doing this with as great devotion as Christians do show in using Holy Water." They kill no creature, "not even a fly, or a flea, or a louse"; eat nothing green, only dried; eat from dry leaves; sleep naked on the ground; fast and drink water. Asked why they go naked, they say they came naked into the world and "have no sin of the flesh". **Text variants:** Yule (II, Note 11, bookrags p. 351) says the Geographic Text has "losci dou buef" which Pauthier turned into "suif de buef", and that Ramusio makes the Jogis daub themselves with powder of ox-**bones** (*l'ossa*). So "cow-dung ash" is one reading among several. Access: full text read.

### G1. Pāśupata (and Kālāmukha) ash-smearing Śaiva ascetics — ash yes, ox and cow-dung not shown
- **Verdict: disputed (best fit for the ash; no source I read equates them with Polo's group).**
- **Sources.** (i) David N. Lorenzen, "The Kapalikas and Kalamukhas: Two Lost Saivite Sects", PhD thesis, Australian National University, March 1968, https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/637fe273-0991-4113-9c0a-226f0693e705/content (extract read; page numbers not visible). Quoted: "The bath in ashes is one of the central rituals prescribed in the Pasupata-sutra"; "most Saivite ascetics smear their bodies with ashes"; the Kālāmukhas were "an offshoot of the Pasupatas"; their grants are mostly 11th–12th c., in Kannada, in what is now Karnataka, with a 13th-c. (1252–53) Kālāmukha described as wearing "a small piece of cloth around the loins" and an antelope skin. (ii) S. N. Dasgupta, *A History of Indian Philosophy*, vol. 5 (via wisdomlib.org extract of "The Doctrine of the Pāśupata-sūtras"): ashes used for bathing instead of water; "higher state" ascetics "go about naked … smear their bodies with ashes three times, and meditate upon Śiva" (this passage is Dasgupta reporting Guṇaratna's commentary on Haribhadra's *Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya*; its Naiyāyika/Yoga attribution is as printed).
- **Does it fit?** Ash: yes. Nakedness: yes in a late stage. A bull emblem on the forehead: **not seen** in these sources. Ash *from burnt cow-dung*: **not seen**. Non-killing of lice and "green leaves have souls": **not seen**. The Pāśupata sūtras address Brahmins (wisdomlib summary), which matches Polo's "properly Abraiaman" framing.
- **Limitation.** Lorenzen's thesis is a primary-research thesis of 1968; the extract is partial. These were Śaiva, not necessarily the Tamil/Gujarat people Polo met.

### G2. Jaṅgamas / Liṅgāyat-type Śaiva wearers of an emblem on the forehead — emblem analogue only
- **Verdict: supported as an analogy by Yule; not as an identification.**
- **Source.** Yule–Cordier II, Note 5 (fullbooks mirror): Rev. Dr Caldwell, "The worship of the ox is still common enough, but I can find no trace of the use of the effigy worn on the forehead … no trace of the custom in Tamil literature", yet the Tamil pundits thought it "so truly Hindu in character" that it probably existed. Yule adds: "the Jangams, a Linga-worshipping sect of Southern India, wear a copper or silver linga either round the neck or on the forehead" (citing Wilson, *Mackenzie Collection* II.3; *JRAS* N.S. V. 142 ff.).
- **Limitation.** Doesn't give nakedness, ash, or non-killing. Caldwell's note is a manuscript opinion, not a text.

### G3. Digambara Jains — nakedness and non-injury fit; ash and ox-worship do not
- **Verdict: disputed.**
- **Source.** Wikipedia "Digambara" (extract, **weak source**; use only as a pointer to check Jain primary texts and, for example, Jaini, *The Jaina Path of Purification*, NOT SEEN): monks are "sky-clad", vow *ahimsa* to all living beings "whether tiny or large", never bathe, sleep on hard ground, eat once a day, pluck their hair.
- **Fit.** Nakedness, not killing flies/fleas/lice, sleeping on the ground, fasting: yes. Smearing with ash: no (Digambara monks do not bathe or daub). Forehead ox: no. A **bull** is the traditional emblem of the first Tīrthaṅkara Ṛṣabha; I believe this but **did not verify it in any source here**, so do not cite it.
- **Limitation.** Polo's "eat nothing green" and "green leaves have souls" read as Jain-like to a modern reader; no scholar I opened makes the identification.

### G4. Kāpālika / Aghorī, 7th-c. Xuanzang — ash yes, but not cow-dung and not Polo's period
- Lorenzen's thesis quotes Xuanzang (via Beal) for "naked ascetics, and others who cover themselves with ashes, and some who make chaplets of bones" and has Beal identify the heretics as Digambaras, Pāśupatas and Kāpālikas. That is 7th-c., north-west India. Wikipedia "Aghori" (weak) repeats it. Not evidence for 1290s.

### Q1(b) evidence status
- **Manuscript/text reading:** Polo's text is as above; variants (dung/bones) exist.
- **Independent contemporary witness:** none found for this exact bundle. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's jogis are ash-*depilated*; Ma Huan's "Chokis" are 15th-c., married, unclothed, conch-blowing, no ox, no ash (via Yule).
- **Physical evidence:** none sought or found.
- **Modern interpretation:** Yule (Jangam analogy; "Jogi used loosely for any Hindu ascetic"); White (treats them as Siddha/yogi alchemists); the sources above for Śaiva/Jain parallels.
- **Best safe fallback:** "naked holy men who revere the ox, smear themselves with ash made from burnt cow-dung (or, in another manuscript, ox-bones), and will kill no living thing. Polo calls them Chughi, i.e. yogis. Scholars see Śaiva ash-smearing ascetics and Jain-like scruples; the exact group can't be named."

## Q2. Yuan hunting closed season

**Overall verdict.** The Ministry of War section of the *Yuan dianzhang* (juan 38) and the *Tongzhi tiaoge* juan 28 *Zaling* both state the season, in imperial edicts quoted in Mongolian-calqued Chinese. The closed season is **from the 1st day of the 1st month to the 20th day of the 7th month**. Area: the quoted edicts say "不揀是誰" ("whoever"), i.e. no territorial limit is stated in the lines I could read. Related articles name zones (500 li, 800 li).

### H1. 元典章 juan 38 (兵部卷之五) 典章三十八 捕獵 — article "禁治打捕月日"
- **Verdict: supported.** Access: **extract/OCR read**; the text is a machine-OCR of a woodblock-based edition and has many errors. I quote only the lines where independent readings agree (the OCR on shidianguji and the quotations in a modern paper); characters I'm unsure of are marked.
- **Source and locator.** 《大元聖政國朝典章》 (Yuan dianzhang), 兵部卷之五, 典章三十八, 捕獵; article heading read in the OCR as "禁治打捕月日" (the Tianjin Guji edition's table of contents, via Douban, prints it as "禁洽打捕月日", a typo for 治). OCR text: https://www.shidianguji.com/book/CADAL02052896/chapter/1l1thuhsovtpe (CADAL scan). Table of contents: https://book.douban.com/subject/6802572/ (Tianjin guji 2011, ed. 陳高華 張帆 劉曉 黨寶海; ISBN 9787806968970; catalogue metadata only). A modern secondary footnote cites "《元典章·兵部五·捕獵》第564頁" (economy.guoxue.com, edition not named), so **a printed page of ~564 is reported, not seen.**
- **Passage (Dade 1/2/18 edict; 1297).** Chinese as read, with uncertain OCR characters bracketed:

  > 大德元年二月十八日，欽奉聖旨節該：「昔兒哈剌教奏：在前春裏、夏裏，不揀是誰，休打捕者。么道，薛禪、今帝行了聖旨來。…… 在前正月爲懷羔兒時分，至七月二十日，休打捕者。打捕呵，肉瘦，皮子不成用，可惜了性命，野物出了，踐踏田禾。么道，依在先行了的聖旨體例，如今正月初一日爲頭，至七月二十日，不揀是誰，休打捕者。打捕的人每，有罪過者。」

  Translation: "On the 18th day of the 2nd month of Dade 1, we respectfully received an imperial edict … 'Previously, in spring and summer, whoever it was, they were not to hunt. … Formerly, from the 1st month, when the lambs are in the womb, to the 20th of the 7th month, there was to be no hunting; if they hunt, the meat is lean, the skins useless, a waste of lives, and the game, once out, tramples the crops. So, following the precedent of the edict issued earlier, now from the 1st day of the 1st month to the 20th day of the 7th month, whoever it is, they are not to hunt. Those who hunt will be guilty.'" The same wording is quoted in a modern paper on animal protection (ulunwen.com, "《元典章》中關於動物保護的內容", unnamed author; **weak source**) and the OCR agrees on "至七月二十日" three times. The same OCR repeats "正月初一日爲頭至七月二十日不揀是誰休打捕野物" in the Dade 7 (1303) case about the Taoyuan county men (淮東道), as a precedent.
- **Penalty (Yanyou 3/1, 1316, under the heading read as 漢兒人不得懸帶弓箭圍獵).** OCR: "薛禪皇帝時分，自正月初一日爲頭，……不揀甚麼走獸，放鷹犬的人每禁斷有來 … 當日騎的鞍馬、穿的衣服、弓箭、鷹犬他每的要了，打六十七下". Translation: from the 1st day of the 1st month, no one may hunt any beast with hawk or hound; offenders forfeit that day's horse, clothes, bow and hounds and receive 67 blows. Heading and wording are **OCR-inferred**.
- **Area.** The Dade 1 edict gives no territory in the lines read. Nearby articles: "禁治打捕兔兒" (Dade 1/5/18): hares not to be hunted within 800 li of Dadu (OCR "自大都八百里以裏休打捕兔兒者"); "中統三年十月" (1262) edict, quoted in 通制條格, forbids flying hawks or hunting within 500 li of Zhongdu except by registered hunter households. These are separate rules.
- **Limitations.** (1) OCR, not an image. (2) A modern book excerpt on guoxue.com gives "七月二十八日" for the end date; the primary-text readings I saw all say 二十日, so "二十八" looks like a slip, but I have not seen the page. (3) The Dianzhang gives Dade 1 *second* month; the *Yuan shi* (below) has the ban recorded under the *third* month.
- **Access level.** Extract/OCR read (no image).

### H2. 通制條格 juan 28 雜令 (圍獵) — closed season and a Sept–Nov hunting window
- **Verdict: supported.** Access: **edition/transcription read** (a modern punctuated transcription at daizhige.org, 史藏 > 政書 > 通制條格; footnote style such as "據《元典章》卷三十八改" suggests a collated critical edition, but the page does not name it). URL: https://daizhige.org/%E5%8F%B2%E8%97%8F/%E6%94%BF%E4%B9%A6/%E9%80%9A%E5%88%B6%E6%9D%A1%E6%A0%BC.html. Saved locally; article numbers **not given** in the transcription, so I give section and date.
- **(a) Juan 28 雜令, section 圍獵, entry dated 至元二十八年八月二十七日 (1291).**
  > 至元二十八年八月二十七日，中書省奏：「咸平府那裏每這幾年田禾不曾收來。百姓每生受的其間，野物的不教禁約，教養百姓每喉嗉急呵，怎生？」么道，那裏的宣慰司官人每說將來有。俺商量得，依他每言語不教禁約呵，怎生？商量來，么道。奏呵，那裏的怎生禁約來？自正月至七月，爲野物的皮子肉歹，更爲懷羔兒的上頭，普例禁約有。除那的外，教采打食者。……聖旨了也。

  Translation: "On 27 August … the Secretariat reported: 'In Xianping Prefecture the crops have not been harvested for several years and the people are suffering. Shall we not forbid hunting wild game, so that the people can feed themselves?' … We asked how it had been prohibited there; [they said:] from the 1st month to the 7th month, because the skins and meat of game are poor and also because the animals are carrying young, it is prohibited by general rule. Apart from that, let them hunt and eat. … [The emperor] so ruled." Area stated: **a general rule ("普例")**, case from 咸平府 (Xianping, Liaodong).
- **(b) Same section, entry 至元二十八年十一月二十二日 (1291).** The Censorate reported disputes over the Luan River (滦河) boundary and the text continues with: "依在先聖旨體例裏，正月爲頭懷羔時分，河西每、憨哈納思、阿陳每、漢兒人每，不揀誰休圍場者。那其間裏圍場呵，肉瘦，皮子蟲蛀，可惜了，性命無濟有，野物呵也盡了去也。……九月十月十一月，這三個月圍場者，除這三個月外休圍場者。" Translation: "According to the earlier imperial precedent, from the 1st month, the time of lambing, Hexi people, the Qianhanasi, the Achen and Han people alike were not to run enclosed drives. … Hold drives in the 9th, 10th and 11th months; outside those three months, do not." **Caveat:** the daizhige text has a break in this entry ("直至蠻子田地哏寬有……" follows a gap), so the reference of "那裏" is uncertain; I cannot say which area the Sept–Nov permission covers beyond "the groups named".
- **(c) Same section: Zhida 4 (1311) edict.** "百姓於禁地內打捕野物者，仰管圍場官與各處有司一同斷罪，毋得似前斷沒家產" — people who hunt in preserve ("禁地") land are tried by the preserve officer with the local authority; confiscating the family's property is no longer permitted.
- **(d) Huangqing 1/5/7 (1312), same section.** After the Censorate complained of a prince's hunting during the sowing season in Jinning: "今後農種時月裏休圍獵者 … 今後圍獵呵，十月初頭圍獵者" — no hunting during sowing; hunt from early 10th month.
- **Limitation.** The *Tongzhi tiaoge* is a 1323 compilation of Yuan law; juan 28 survives; the transcription is a web text, not the printed edition.

### H3. 元史 — months stated in a basic annal
- **Verdict: supported.** *Yuan shi*, Chengzong ji 2 (成宗二; **juan number not shown** in the extract; I believe juan 19, not verified), Dade 1 (1297), third month, 丁亥: "禁正月至七月捕獵，大都八百里內亦如之" — "forbade hunting from the 1st month to the 7th; likewise within 800 li of Dadu." Read as quoted in 《欽定古今圖書集成·經濟彙編·祥刑典》juan 24 (律令部彙考十), zh.wikisource.org (extract read), which quotes the *Yuan shi* annal: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%AC%BD%E5%AE%9A%E5%8F%A4%E4%BB%8A%E5%9C%96%E6%9B%B8%E9%9B%86%E6%88%90/%E7%B6%93%E6%BF%9F%E5%BD%99%E7%B7%A8/%E7%A5%A5%E5%88%91%E5%85%B8/%E7%AC%AC024%E5%8D%B7. This is a quotation by the Qing encyclopedia, not the *Yuan shi* itself. It also lists earlier local bans (Zhiyuan 2/5 Beijing and Pinglan hunting; Zhiyuan 19/3 Yidu and Dongping on the Huai), which show the ban was repeated locally before 1297.
- **Note on the user's premise.** *Yuan shi* juan 101 (the "地有禁，取有時" line) gives no months. The annals do. I did not read juan 101 itself.
- **Area.** "大都八百里內亦如之" (same rule inside 800 li of Dadu).

### H4. Polo's own count — conflicts
- Modern Chinese paper (economy.guoxue.com, a Chinese translation by Liang Shengzhi, 1998, quoted second-hand): Polo says Kublai forbade killing hares, roe, fallow deer, red deer and birds "from March to October". Polo's own passage **not located** in Yule–Cordier in this pass. If the quotation is right, Polo's months (March–October) disagree with the edicts (Jan 1–Jul 20). Do not use "March to October" and "January to July" interchangeably.
