## Part 1

I read the OCR text of Wood's book at the archive.org URL you gave. It is a full text file, not images, so quotes may carry OCR errors. The title page says "A Member of the Perseus Books Group", so it is the US edition. I did not check which year. Page numbers below come from running heads in the OCR and are approximate.

Wood's own position matters for how you word the retelling. The ledger entries for claims 1 and 2 change as follows.

**Claim 1 (Great Wall) — still supported, with two details added**
- **Chapter:** "He missed the biggest wall", chapter 11, pp. ~95–101.
- **Wood's conclusion:** She writes: "the omission of the Wall in the Description of the World is telling." De Rachewiltz quotes her accurately.
- **She does not claim a continuous Wall:** She calls the issue "complicated by the question of what the Great Wall might have looked like in the thirteenth century." She concedes the brick-facing "was done after the Polos travelled east."
- **She already cites Waldron:** She quotes Waldron ("The Mongols encountered no Great Wall when they conquered China") and discusses his argument in chapter 11, with notes citing his "The problem of the Great Wall". His point is therefore not new to her.
- **Her counter-argument:** Her argument is that earlier tamped-earth walls "would have been much of the tamped earth wall surviving in the thirteenth century".
- **Safe wording:** "de Rachewiltz replies, using Waldron, that no continuous Wall stood in Polo's day. Wood herself grants the Wall's thirteenth-century form is uncertain but argues earlier earthen walls would still have been visible."
- **Scope:** This is what the scan shows. It does not settle which reading is right.

**Claim 2 (Yangzhou) — de Rachewiltz oversimplifies Wood's point 7**
- **What he attributes to her:** The ANU summary says Wood argues "no gazetteer of Yang-chou mentions him; therefore… unsubstantiated".
- **What Wood actually says:**
  - Chapter 8: "according to one manuscript he governed for three years" (p. ~94).
  - Chapter 15 ("A significant absence", pp. ~132 ff.):
    - Pelliot suggests Polo was an official in the Salt Administration. The Yuan shi lists a "Boluo" as Salt Administrator of Yangzhou.
    - A scholar she cites in the OCR (the name is not visible in my excerpt) argues "governed" was a copyist's error for "sejourna". The text then says "Taking this more plausible reading, no one else need plough through lists of Governors…".
    - She also says that even if Polo "was a sojourner in Yangzhou, rather than a governor, it is likely that a visitor of exotic foreign origin would have been included" in the gazetteers.
- **Safe wording:** "Wood notes the governorship may be a copyist's error but still argues that a foreign resident would likely appear in the local gazetteers. De Rachewiltz says Polo held no official position, so none would be expected."
- **Where they differ:** On whether a sojourning foreigner would be recorded. They agree that "governor" is doubtful.

**Other checks**
- She mentions Professor Yang Zhijiu's defence of Polo and says Yang considered omissions such as tea and the Great Wall "not sufficient to dismiss the whole book" (chapter 15).
- Her Shangdu passage (chapter 12) concerns Europeans at the Khan's summer palace in 1260–61. It does not describe the palace or the cane palace, so it adds nothing to claim 8.
- I found no mention of Dutschke or Atwood in the lines I searched.

## Part 2

Atwood's Shangdu entry is readable, so claim 8 can be answered, though only from an OCR scan.

**Claim 8 — answered. The entry mentions the cane palace but says nothing about Polo.**
- **Source:** Atwood, *Encyclopedia of Mongolia and the Mongol Empire* (Facts on File, 2004), entry "Shangdu (Shang-tu, K'ai-p'ing, Xanadu)", in the alphabetical run before "Shangdzodba, Erdeni".
- **Access level:** full-text OCR read from an archive.org scan, identifier `encyclopedia-of-mongolia-and-the-mongol-empire`. The OCR has no reliable page marker around the entry, so I give no page number. The scan looks user-uploaded, so check the printed page in a library copy.
- **What it says about the palace:** The entry is a single paragraph. In 1256 Prince Qubilai built Kaiping (Inner Mongolia, near modern Zhenglan Qi), and was elected great khan there in 1260. It describes a three-part city: an outer city of about 2,200 m square, an imperial city, and a smaller palace city. Buddhist monasteries mark each corner of the imperial city.
- **What it says about the cane palace:** The outer city's wall enclosed a well-watered hunting park to the north, "where Qubilai built a collapsible cane palace", which Atwood links to Coleridge's "stately pleasure dome".
- **Other details:** Kaiping was renamed Shangdu in 1263. The khan and a skeleton staff stayed there May to September, holding audiences at the Da'an Hall. Quriltai assemblies met there through 1328. The Ming garrisoned it, and it was abandoned around 1430.
- **What it says about Polo:** Nothing. The words "Polo" and "Marco" do not appear in the entry's lines. So the entry cannot support any claim that Polo saw this palace.
- **Verdict:** supports, in part. It supports "Shangdu was Qubilai's summer city, with a collapsible cane palace". It does not support anything about Polo's visit or eyewitness status.
- **Safe wording:** "Atwood describes Shangdu as Qubilai's summer capital, with a collapsible cane palace in a northern hunting park." Do not attach Polo to this citation.
- **Limitations:** Two cross-references in the entry, to Kaiping and to Xanadu, point back to it. The OCR text may have small errors, so check wording against the printed page before quoting.
- **One internal slip in the book:** The entry says rebels burned Shangdu "in winter 1258–59". Given the Yuan context this looks like a printing or OCR slip for 1358–59. I did not check it. Don't repeat the date without a second source.

**Dutschke (claims 6–7) is still NOT SEEN.** I found no open-access copy of the dissertation. It is available through UMI/ProQuest and library holdings.
