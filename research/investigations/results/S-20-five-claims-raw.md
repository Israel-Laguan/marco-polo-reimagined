# Five Marco Polo claims checked against the sources

This report synthesizes five independent research passes undertaken to verify specific, disputed claims in a draft chapter on Marco Polo's influence on Columbus, cartography, the "Il Milione" nickname tradition, and Chinese scholarship on Polo's departure from China. Across all five questions, the research converges on a single methodological finding: several claims in the draft are not supported by any source the researchers could locate and read, several others rest on secondary or tertiary paraphrase rather than primary-text confirmation, and at least two citation errors in the draft's own apparatus have been identified and corrected. Nothing below should be read as resolving a genuine scholarly dispute in either direction; where the researchers could not get past an abstract, a vendor description, or a search engine's silence, that limitation is stated as such rather than papered over. The report is organized around the five disputed points in turn, each with an evidence table and a recommended fallback sentence, followed by a consolidated works-consulted list and a summary of corrections for the chapter.

## 1. Columbus's annotated Seville copy of Polo and the 1492 voyage

The single most important corrective finding across this entire research effort concerns timing: every source the researcher could access that discusses when Columbus annotated his copy of Polo's book places those annotations **after** 1492, not before it. The draft's framing that the Seville (Biblioteca Colombina) copy "fed" the 1492 voyage is not supported by anything found in this pass.

## 2. The Toscanelli correspondence: authenticity and its link to Polo

The Toscanelli dossier splits, in the literature the researcher could access, into at least three named positions, and the chapter's premise about the "2023" Apfelstadt article is factually wrong — it is a 1992 piece.

**Recommended fallback wording:** "Historians continue to dispute how much of the Toscanelli correspondence is genuine: some reject the entire dossier — the 1474 letter to the Portuguese canon Fernão Martins, its accompanying map, and the later letters supposedly sent directly to Columbus — as a later fabrication (one classic theory, Henry Vignaud's, points to Columbus's brother Bartholomew as a possible author, though Vignaud calls this a presumption, not proof); others accept the original Martins letter but remain skeptical specifically of the letters addressed to Columbus himself. Because even the 'accepted' Martins letter survives only via its later, more disputed transmission to Columbus, the extent to which Toscanelli actually shaped Columbus's plan — as opposed to Columbus's own direct reading of Marco Polo — remains an open and contested question rather than settled fact."

## 4. "Il Milione": the nickname and the "million lies" insult tradition

Two distinct, dated strands of evidence exist and should not be merged: a documented, early wealth-based reading of "Milion," and a vaguer, undated tradition of skepticism about Polo's veracity. The specific "million lies" pun cannot be dated at all from what was found, and the "pelorce" etymology is unsupported.

## 5. Yang Zhijiu, the Chinese record, and the Polos' departure from China

## Corrections to the chapter draft

Three specific, confirmed errors in the chapter's current apparatus should be corrected: the Apfelstadt article on Toscanelli, Fernão de Roriz, and the correspondence's authenticity is dated **1992**, not "2023-or-so" — no 2023 Apfelstadt piece on this topic exists in the sources checked. The doctoral thesis surveying the "Il Milione" nickname theories is by **Francisco Sangorrín Guallar**, not "Dolores" Sangorrín Guallar. And the "pelorce" nickname etymology has no support in any source found across this research and should be removed from the chapter entirely rather than hedged.

Beyond these three corrections, the chapter's substantive claims need adjustment on all five points: the Seville copy's annotations should not be presented as feeding the 1492 voyage (all dated evidence points to 1497–98, tied to the third voyage), and no individually locatable postil, nor the Shawcross 2023 footnote's actual content, nor the John Day letter's identification with the Colombina copy, could be verified in this pass — these should be flagged as open rather than settled. The Toscanelli correspondence should be presented as genuinely disputed along the Vignaud-vs-Apfelstadt lines described above, with Polo's influence on Columbus kept analytically separate from (and more secure than) the contested Toscanelli channel. The Catalan Atlas's "Chipangu" label should be cut outright — no source found supports it — while its genuine, narrower Polo borrowings (Qubilai Khan, Chanbalech, southwestern China) can be kept with attribution to Lišćák (2018); the Fra Mauro/Lisbon map relationship should be presented as unresolved rather than asserting the Venice map's priority. The "million lies" insult tradition cannot be dated as either early or late on the evidence found — only generic early skepticism about Polo's veracity is dated — and any deathbed story used should reflect Polo's refusal to recant, not a recantation. Finally, Yang Zhijiu's argument should be presented with the Yang/Cleaves division made explicit, the Yuanshi-vs-Yongle-Dadian location left hedged as genuinely disputed in the secondary literature, and the clear, well-corroborated statement that neither the Chinese nor the Persian primary sources name the Polos retained as one of the more solid findings of this whole research effort.
