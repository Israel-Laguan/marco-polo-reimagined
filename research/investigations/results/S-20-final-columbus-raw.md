# **Columbus's Annotated Polo**: What the Sources Actually Support

The supplied corpus supports a **cautious, well-attributed account of the Seville copy's marginalia** and a **qualified statement on the Toscanelli letters**, but it contains **no evidence** on the Catalan Atlas's Chipangu label, the *Il Milione* nickname etymology, or Yang Zhijiu's embassy-notice argument. The strongest new finding is that the surviving copy is **heavily annotated by Columbus and two others in his circle**, with the notes overwhelmingly concerned with **material things** rather than topography — which reframes, rather than confirms, the "Polo fed the first voyage" implication.

## What Can Responsibly Be Said About 1492

The surviving copy is a **Pipino Latin incunable** bearing roughly **366 postils in at least three hands** — Columbus, his son Hernando, and one other associate — with a residue of indeterminate origin (Shawcross 2023, 376-424). The notes are dominated by **objects and resources** — gold, pearls, spices, minerals — organised hierarchically by potential value to Europeans, a reading practice Shawcross ties to a shift in the relationship between materiality and cosmography (Shawcross 2023, 376-424). That is a claim about **how Columbus read**, not about **when** he read this copy.

On timing, Shawcross explicitly leaves open that Columbus "may not have yet had the opportunity" to access the *Devisament* or any recension directly during his research, and that his knowledge "may have been acquired at one or more removes" (Shawcross 2023, 376-424). What he demonstrably had before 1492 were **maps and Toscanelli's letters**, not necessarily the book (Shawcross 2023, 376-424). Wood's conclusion that Columbus "had a copy of Polo's *Description of the World* with him on his epic voyage" is a broader claim than the marginalia evidence supports and should not be used to date the notes (Wood 2018, 140-151). Beal's 1993 survey confirms the volumes "still exist in the Columbian Library in Seville" with Columbus's handwritten annotations, but adds no dating (Beal-Jr. 1993, 151-160).

**Recommended caption:** "Seville, Biblioteca Capitular y Colombina, 10-3-2 (formerly V-11713): Pipino's Latin *De consuetudinibus*, annotated with some 366 postils in the hands of Christopher Columbus, his son Hernando, and another associate; the dates of individual notes are uncertain and do not establish use before 1492." This follows Shawcross's own hand-attribution and her caution about pre-voyage access (Shawcross 2023, 376-424).

## Toscanelli: A Contested Correspondence

Apfelstadt's abstract is the clearest statement of the dispute: Toscanelli is thought to have sent three letters to Portugal between 1474 and 1482 — one with a map to the Lisbon canon Fernando Martins, and two to Columbus — and while "l'autenticità delle tre lettere sia stata accettata dalla maggior parte degli studiosi colombiani," the whole correspondence has been denied by some and the two Columbus letters by others (Apfelstadt 1992, 69-80). His new documents place Roriz in Florence in 1459 and 1466 alongside Toscanelli, but that **context does not authenticate the letters** (Apfelstadt 1992, 69-80). Sousa Costa likewise notes the correspondence's "authenticity is denied by some historians" (De Sousa Costa 1990, 187-276), and Apfelstadt warns that "in giuoco almeno una parte della stima per la scoperta del Mondo Nuovo" (Apfelstadt 1992, 69-80).

**One accurate sentence for a general reader:** "Most Columbus scholars accept Toscanelli's 1474 letter to Fernão Martins and his letters to Columbus as genuine, but a minority rejects the whole correspondence or the Columbus letters specifically, so the link should be presented as disputed." The Markham translation of the letters themselves is available as a primary text (Columbus and Markham 2010, 1-12), but it does not settle authenticity. **Fallback:** if the underlying argument stays inaccessible, omit the causal claim that Toscanelli derived his project from Polo and attribute the correspondence as "contested."

## Catalan Atlas, *Il Milione*, and Chinese Scholarship: Gaps

No supplied sentence states that the 1375 Catalan Atlas carries a **Chipangu** label or a Japan-like island. What the corpus does support is that the Atlas drew on Polo, Mandeville, Odoric and Ibn Battutah (Lišèák 2018, 1-8; Jung and Jung 2015, 1-14), that its eastern names include **Caulij** (probably Kauli/Koryo) for Korea (Knox 2016, 93-94; Jung and Jung 2015, 1-14), and that its sources were pluricultural, with Islamic input generally underestimated (Vagnon 2021, 160-188). Vagnon confirms the Atlas was completed c. 1375 and in Charles V's library by 1380 (Vagnon 2021, 160-188). **Recommendation:** cut the specific Chipangu sentence and substitute an independently supported one — e.g. "The 1375 Catalan Atlas, completed c. 1375 and in Charles V's library by 1380, drew on Polo, Mandeville and Odoric for its Asian names, including *Caulij* for Korea" (Vagnon 2021, 160-188; Lišèák 2018, 1-8; Knox 2016, 93-94). On Fra Mauro, the surviving Marciana map was made at San Michele in Isola between 1448 and 1453 ; the corpus does not resolve its relationship to the Portuguese-commissioned map, so the qualification must stand.

For *Il Milione*, the corpus offers only **competing attributions**, not a chronology. Cruse notes the title "appears to derive from a nickname for the Polo family" but is "traditionally said to refer to the text's descriptions of the great wealth of Kubilai Khan" (Cruse 2022, 233-240). Garkavets claims to have "confirmed the hypothesis" linking the nickname to the family estate Vilione/Milione (Garkavets 2017, 522-565), while Wirz-Ridolfi says the addition "ist nicht unbedingt ein Ausdruck der Übertreibungen, sondern ein Hinweis auf den Reichtum der Familie Polo," noting Niccolò was known as "Milion lo grande" (Wirz-Ridolfi 2013, 6-7). **No dated "million lies" telling appears in the corpus**; the claim that the insult is late cannot be established and should be dropped, with the explanations presented as attributed interpretations. The deathbed quotation ("Ich habe nicht die Hälfte dessen erzählt, was ich gesehen habe") is reported by Wirz-Ridolfi without a source and should not be used as authenticated last words (Wirz-Ridolfi 2013, 6-7).

**Yang Zhijiu (1941) and the Chinese embassy notice:** no supplied sentence mentions Yang, his publications, or the embassy notice. The search for Chinese scholarship did not return usable results. **Fallback:** use Ma and Jackson's qualified accounts as already drafted, and present the embassy notice as a proposed identification whose Chinese and Persian sources do not themselves name the Polos — without the uncertain Yang citation.
