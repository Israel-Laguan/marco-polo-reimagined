# Historiographical Synthesis: **War of Saint Sabas** and Sultan **Baybars**

Modern scholarship refines the chronologies of the War of Saint Sabas and the reign of Sultan Baybars, correcting nineteenth-century accounts regarding the spolia of Saint Mark’s and Mamluk dynastic numbering (Stern et al., 2024; Sublet, 1998; Perry, 1977).

## Chronological and Historiographical Clarifications

Regarding the War of Saint Sabas, modern excavations at Acre show clear physical evidence of the conflict in the Genoese quarter, including blocked gates and fortified towers built of bossed ashlars with arrow slits abandoned following the commune's expulsion (Stern et al., 2024). While earlier historiography occasionally pointed to 1255, the definitive outbreak of inter-communal armed hostilities in Acre is placed in 1256, continuing intensely until the Genoese fleet was defeated off Acre in 1258 and Genoese operations relocated to Tyre (Stern et al., 2024; Falk, 2018; Irwin, 2021). The cessation date remains nuanced: while the local conflict in Acre was extinguished by 1258–1260 and formalized under truces surrounding the Eighth Crusade in 1270, broader Venetian–Genoese naval warfare persisted intermittently across the Levant and Mediterranean until the late fourteenth century (Falk, 2018; Katele, 1988).

The famous porphyry sculpture embedded in the corner of Saint Mark's Basilica in Venice did not originate from the monastery of Saint Sabas in Acre (Perry, 1977; Arce, 2023). Nineteenth-century antiquarians confused local Acre spoils with Constantinopolitan spolia brought to Venice after the Fourth Crusade in 1204 (Perry, 1977; Arce, 2023; Katele, 1988). The porphyry sculpture depicts Late Roman rulers—traditionally the Tetrarchs, though argued by some to be the Constantinian dynasty—transported from the Philadelpheion of Constantinople (Arce, 2023). Conversely, the freestanding carved pillars outside Saint Mark's (*pilastri acritani*) are separate monuments historically linked to the Syrian Levant, though frequently conflated in early commentaries (Perry, 1977; Arce, 2023).

For the biography of Sultan Baybars, his accession followed the assassination of Sultan Quṭuz in October 1260 shortly after the victory over the Mongols at ʿAyn Jālūt (Mahamid, 2023; Irvine, 1990; Aigle, 2006). His reign ended with his death in 1277 in Damascus (Mahamid, 2023; Irvine, 1990). Calling him the fourth Bahri sovereign aligns with the direct sequence of ruling sultans in Cairo (Aybak, al-Manṣūr ʿAlī, Quṭuz, Baybars) (Sublet, 1998; Cook, 2020). Primary Arabic biographers (such as Ibn ʿAbd al-Ẓāhir and Ibn Shaddād) and contemporary chronicles document his campaigns into Little Armenia and Damascus, while his sudden demise in 1277 is preserved in historical accounts across varying narratives of acute illness and poisoning (Mahamid, 2023; Sublet, 1998; Irvine, 1990; Spivak, 2021).

**Short prose on disagreements**

On the War of St Sabas start, Yule’s 1255 reflects the simmering property dispute; modern accounts (Jacoby and others) place the decisive Genoese seizure and open violence in 1256, with full Venetian naval intervention 1257–58. The 1270 Cremona settlement is the accepted formal end of the named war, even though the wider Venetian–Genoese rivalry continued and the Genoese only recovered their Acre quarter in 1288.  

On the sculptures, the older Venetian tradition (and Yule) linked the porphyry figures to the 1258 Acre victory; excavation evidence and art-historical consensus since the mid-20th century place the Tetrarchs firmly in Constantinople (1204 spolia). The Pilastri Acritani share the same reattribution trajectory.  

On Baybars’s death, the poisoned-kumiz story (accidental or self-inflicted) is the majority medieval and modern narrative; pure illness or wound accounts exist but are secondary. Day-of-death variance (30 June vs 1 July) is minor and common in conversion of 28 Muharram 676. Birth year remains approximate.

**Explanatory notes**

On the War of St Sabas, Yule’s narrative is essentially sound but the opening year is better given as 1256. The decisive phase was 1256–58; the formal peace of 1270 closed a longer period of intermittent conflict. The sculptural attribution is the point that requires correction: both the Tetrarchs and the Pilastri Acritani are now securely linked to Constantinople (pre-1204 spolia), not to the 1258 Acre victory.

On Baybars, the accession date is fixed to the immediate aftermath of Qutuz’s murder in 1260 (October). The death date is 30 June / 1 July 1277 (27 Muḥarram 676). Cause remains debated in the sources themselves; a cautious note should acknowledge both the illness narrative and the poisoned-kumiz tradition without privileging Yule’s version.

**Unresolved questions**
- Precise day of Baybars’s formal investiture (24 vs 26 October 1260) varies slightly by source.
- Whether any residual fighting in the Levant after 1270 is still counted by some historians under the “War of St Sabas” label.
- Exact relationship (if any) between the Venetian chronicles’ claim of trophies from Acre and the actual Constantinopolitan origin of the sculptures now on the south façade of San Marco.

For the retelling, print the War of St Sabas as 1256–1270 (intense phase 1256–58, formal end 1270), correct the sculptural origin note, and give Baybars as sultan 1260–1277 (accession October 1260 after Qutuz’s murder; death 30 June/1 July 1277 at Damascus).
