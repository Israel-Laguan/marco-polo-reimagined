## Return A — shark-charmers

The best-supported answer is that Ceylon’s shark-charmers (*kadal-kotti* / “binders of sharks”) were **finally done away with in 1885**. That was an abolition of their official employment and government remuneration—not merely a payroll adjustment—and it followed earlier rules that had already barred them from taking oysters or other payment directly from divers. [readingroo](https://readingroo.ms/6/3/2/9/63299/63299-h/63299-h.htm)

## What changed, and when

| Event | What the sources show |
|---|---|
| Earlier regulation | The government first limited charmers to **one oyster per day from each diver**, to curb “extravagant misuse” of their influence.  [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/63299/pg63299-images.html) |
| Later arrangement | They were then **paid by the government** and expressly forbidden to demand or receive oysters or any other compensation from boatmen, divers, or anyone else.  [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/63299/pg63299-images.html) |
| **1885** | The shark-charmers were **“done away with entirely.”** This is the clear endpoint of both their paid employment and their official role in the fishery.  [readingroo](https://readingroo.ms/6/3/2/9/63299/63299-h/63299-h.htm) |

So, the 1885 measure was **more than a payroll change**: it ended the charmers’ government-sponsored function altogether. But the accessible wording does not by itself establish a sweeping prohibition on *private* hiring of charmers outside the government fishery; it establishes that they were eliminated from the official Ceylon fishery system. [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/63299/pg63299-images.html)

## The 1856 claim

A modern history of the fishery states that “charmers were banned by the government in 1856” and therefore did not appear in the industry’s final decades.  That statement is difficult to reconcile with the stronger, more specific contemporary-derived account that charmers remained a feature of the fishery **until 1885**. [readingroo](https://readingroo.ms/6/3/2/9/63299/63299-h/63299-h.htm)

On the evidence available, **1856 should not be treated as the year they stopped being paid or employed**. It may reflect a particular restriction, administrative decision, or a misdated claim, but it is not supported by the detailed 1885 abolition account. [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/63299/pg63299-images.html)

## What 1859 refers to

**1859 does not appear to concern the charmers.** In the Ceylon Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society material, 1858–59 is discussed in connection with cholera outbreaks that prematurely closed the pearl fisheries; the 1859 fishery reportedly yielded £48,000 rather than a projected £70,000 because of the epidemic and the resulting panic and desertion of Salapatturai. [upload.wikimedia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6b/Journal_of_the_Ceylon_Branch_of_the_Royal_Asiatic_Society_(IA_journalofceylonb10roya).pdf)

Thus, 1859 is relevant to the **fishery’s operation and mortality**, not to a documented change in charmer employment or payment. [upload.wikimedia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6b/Journal_of_the_Ceylon_Branch_of_the_Royal_Asiatic_Society_(IA_journalofceylonb10roya).pdf)

## What Twynam cites

The relevant Twynam work is **Sir William Crofton Twynam, *Report on the Ceylon Pearl Fisheries, 1899***, published at Colombo in 1902; Twynam was Superintendent of the Fisheries from 1862 to 1896. [searcharchives.bl](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/041-003202679)

Kunz and Stevenson’s *The Book of the Pearl* presents the 1885 abolition in language closely aligned with Twynam’s account and identifies Twynam as an authority on Ceylon pearl fishing.  However, the searchable text available here does **not** expose the precise archival document, minute, ordinance, or administrative order that Twynam footnotes for the 1885 abolition. The defensible formulation is therefore: [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/63299/pg63299-images.html)

> **Twynam is the proximate authority for the 1885 end of the shark-charmers, but the exact document he cites for that date could not be verified from the accessible text.**

## Bottom line

- **Year charmers stopped being employed/paid:** **1885**. [readingroo](https://readingroo.ms/6/3/2/9/63299/63299-h/63299-h.htm)
- **Nature of the 1885 measure:** an order/measure abolishing them from the official fishery, following a prior rule that they be government-paid and barred from private exactions; not merely a payroll change. [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/63299/pg63299-images.html)
- **1856:** a claim of a government ban appears in one modern account, but it conflicts with the 1885 endpoint and should be treated as unverified for this specific question. [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/63299/pg63299-images.html)
- **1859:** concerns cholera and the premature closure of the fisheries, not the charmers. [upload.wikimedia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6b/Journal_of_the_Ceylon_Branch_of_the_Royal_Asiatic_Society_(IA_journalofceylonb10roya).pdf)
- **Twynam’s cited document:** not identifiable from the accessible sources; Twynam’s 1902 *Report on the Ceylon Pearl Fisheries* is the work to check for the underlying citation. [searcharchives.bl](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/041-003202679)

## Return B — Wen et al. 1980, Yunnan

Yes—Wen, He, Huang et al. (1980) appear to include a Yunnan record for the “Malay gharial” (马来鳄), but it is a **historical written record**, not a fossil or other physical remain. The paper is **Wen Huanran (文焕然), He Yeheng (何业恒), Huang Zhujian (黄祝坚) et al., “历史时期中国马来鳄分布的变迁及其原因的初步研究,” *华东师范大学学报（自然科学版）* 1980(3): 109–121**. [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555)

## The Yunnan record

The Yunnan locality is associated with the historical **金齿 / 永昌** area—i.e., western Yunnan around present-day Baoshan, southwest of Dali—rather than with a dated excavation or museum specimen. In Yuan times, the relevant administrative unit was the **大理金齿等处宣抚司**, whose seat was at Yongchang (永昌府, modern Baoshan); “金齿” thus functions here as a historical regional/ethnic-geographic designation, not as a crocodilian taxon. [zh.wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%87%91%E9%BD%BF%E7%AD%89%E5%A4%84%E5%AE%A3%E6%8A%9A%E5%8F%B8)

I could not retrieve the exact primary quotation and precise dynastic year from the accessible versions of the 1980 article. On the evidence available, the record should be treated as a **pre-modern textual occurrence** from the Yongchang/Jinchi region of Yunnan, cited by Wen, He, Huang and colleagues in their reconstruction of the species’ former Chinese range. [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555)

## Written record or remains?

| Evidence type | Status for the Yunnan record |
|---|---|
| Written historical source | **Yes** — this is the basis of the record in Wen et al. (1980).  [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555) |
| Fossil / subfossil bones | **No evidence found** that the Yunnan record rests on physical remains.  [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555) |
| Modern specimen | **No evidence found** of a Yunnan voucher specimen.  [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555) |

This matters because the physical evidence for a gharial-like crocodilian in southern China is instead associated with Guangdong: Bronze Age subfossils from Jiangmen and Foshan were later described as *Hanyusuchus sinensis*, not as *Tomistoma*. [ncbi.nlm.nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8905159/)

## Species identification

Wen, He, Huang et al. identify the animal as **马来鳄**, the **Malay gharial / false gharial**, *Tomistoma schlegelii* (Müller, 1838)—not the Indian gharial *Gavialis gangeticus*. [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555)

Their identification is therefore a **historical-zoogeographical inference from written sources**, using the recorded animal’s description and reported range to assign it to *Tomistoma*. It is not based on a Yunnan skull, skeleton, or other directly examined specimen. [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555)

## Important qualification

The identification should be regarded as **provisional and historically argued**, not anatomically confirmed for Yunnan. Modern *Tomistoma schlegelii* is documented from Malaysia, Sumatra, Borneo, Java, Sulawesi, and southern Thailand; its present native range does not include Yunnan.  Thus, the Yunnan entry is best described as: **a written, pre-modern record from the Jinchi/Yongchang area, assigned by Wen et al. (1980) to *Tomistoma schlegelii*, with no known physical specimen supporting that particular locality.** [progressingeography](https://www.progressingeography.com/CN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=12555)

## Return C — Kerman turquoise

Marco Polo did **not** visit Shahr-i Babak in 1390–1430: he died in 1324. Those dates refer to the period in which surviving manuscripts/copies of his *Travels* were produced or circulated, including the passage that mentions the turquoise mine at **Shahr-i Babak** in western Kerman, Iran. [gia](https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-2021-persian-turquoise-ancient-treasure-neyshabur)

## What the dates mean

Marco Polo traveled through Asia mainly in 1271–95 and dictated *Il Milione* (better known as *The Travels of Marco Polo*) after returning to Venice.  The reference to “1390–1430” is therefore a **manuscript-transmission date**, not the date of Polo’s journey or visit. [gia](https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-2021-persian-turquoise-ancient-treasure-neyshabur)

The Gemological Institute of America specifically notes that the Shahr-i Babak turquoise deposit was mentioned in Marco Polo’s *Book of the Wonders of the World* “between 1390 and 1430”—meaning a manuscript tradition from that window preserves the account. [gia](https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-2021-persian-turquoise-ancient-treasure-neyshabur)

## Shahr-i Babak

Shahr-i Babak is in western Kerman Province, Iran, and is associated with a historically important turquoise deposit. Polo’s text is cited as an early European description of the mine, even though it was written down long after his actual travels. [gia](https://www.gia.edu/gems-gemology/fall-2021-persian-turquoise-ancient-treasure-neyshabur)
