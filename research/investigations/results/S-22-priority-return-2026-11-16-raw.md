# Verified visual sources for a 1260–1295 Marco Polo retelling: a category-by-category ledger

This report answers a narrow, practical question — which period (c.1260–1295) visual or physical sources can an illustrator actually stand on, scene by scene — and the honest answer is: very few, and almost none of them have been *seen* as images by the researchers who checked them. Across armour, ships, Venice/Genoa/Ayas, the Yuan cities, and Sumatra/South India, the pattern repeats: archaeological finds exist and are real (Takashima helmets and armor fragments, the Quanzhou ship, Kota Cina boat timbers, Shangdu's wall lines), dated paintings exist and are real (Liu Guandao's 1280 "Khubilai Khan Hunting," the Porta Sant'Alipio mosaic c.1270–75), but in every one of these cases the researcher read a museum page, an encyclopedia article, or a scholarly abstract about the object — not the object or image itself — so no first-hand drawing cue can responsibly be offered. Several widely-assumed sources fail on inspection: the Met's "Yuan-style" ceremonial armor is dated by the Met itself to the **18th century**; the Qingming Shanghe Tu depicts **Kaifeng**, not Hangzhou, and is Northern Song, a different city in a different dynasty's capital; the Borobudur ship relief is **Javanese**, not Sumatran, and roughly 400–500 years too early; the standing Ayas fortress is substantially a **16th-century Ottoman rebuild**; and two V&A "merchant writing kits" are 150–220 years too late. Nothing below should be read as a finished reference sheet — it is a verification ledger, and large parts of it end honestly in "not found" or "record-only, no drawing cue," which is the deliverable, not a shortfall.

## Category 1 — Yuan and Mongol armour (I-05, II-01, II-08): PARTLY ANSWERED

**Status by scene:** I-05, II-01, II-08 are all **partly answered**. Physical evidence of Mongol military equipment exists and is credibly linked to the invasion fleets, but no researcher-viewed image and no museum-dated late-13th-century accessioned object was found for helmet, lamellar, bow, or quiver specifically.

### Takashima shipwreck finds (physical/archaeological evidence)

- **Claim:** An intact Mongol helmet and fragments of "bright red leather armor" were observed at the Takashima Kōzaki wreck site; iron arrow tips and arrow/crossbow-bolt bundles were also recovered. **Verdict:** supported, but by a single, non-cross-checked source. **Source:** James P. Delgado, "Relics of the Kamikaze," *Archaeology* magazine, Vol. 56 No. 1 (Jan/Feb 2003), archive.archaeology.org/0301/etc/kamikaze.html. **What it says:** a first-person account by the first Western archaeologist to dive the site, describing the helmet and leather armor fragments in situ/recovered, plus iron arrow tips — no mention of lamellar lacing, a bow, or a quiver. **Access level:** extract read via WebFetch (popular-magazine text, not the artifact or a photograph). **Limitations:** not a peer-reviewed site report; no measurements, no inventory numbers. **Recommended wording:** "A diver/excavator's eyewitness account (Delgado 2003) describes an intact iron helmet and fragments of red-lacquered/leather armor at Takashima; treat as credible secondary testimony, not a verified museum object record." **Drawing cue:** record-only, no drawing cue (text read, not an image).
- **Claim:** The wreck is linked to the 1281 invasion fleet specifically. **Verdict:** supported only as the article's own hypothesis — Delgado frames this as Hayashida's *suspicion*, not a settled, published dating result. **Source:** same as above. **Access level:** extract read. **Recommended wording:** "Popularly reported as part of the 1281 fleet, but the source itself frames this as the excavator's suspicion, not a confirmed dating result."
- **Claim:** Fishermen and Ryukyu University-led excavation (since 1980) at Kōzaki have recovered swords, helmets, weapons, arrows, a ~12 m ship's keel, lacquerware, and anchor stones; many finds are held at a Matsuura municipal museum. **Verdict:** record-only, not independently verified against a primary excavation report — this is an encyclopedia tertiary source whose own underlying Japanese-language citations (Agency for Cultural Affairs, Matsuura City, Nagasaki Prefecture) were not opened. **Source:** Wikipedia, "Takashima Kōzaki Site," en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takashima_Kōzaki_Site. **Access level:** full page text read via WebFetch; primary citations not opened. **Limitations:** no lamellar, bow, or quiver mentioned anywhere on this page; no per-object 1274-vs-1281 date given. **Drawing cue:** record-only, no drawing cue.
- **Claim:** A KAKENHI-funded conservation project (project 17300293) radiocarbon-dated lacquer and analyzed metal finds (swords, helmets) from the Takashima underwater site, with results reported (in the accessible abstract) as "confirming" a link to the second Mongol invasion. **Verdict:** supported as to what the abstract states; not checked against the full Japanese report. **Source:** KAKEN grant-report summary, kaken.nii.ac.jp/en/report/KAKENHI-PROJECT-17300293/173002932007kenkyu_seika_hokoku_gaiyo. **Access level:** English abstract read via WebFetch; full Japanese report not accessed. **Limitations — important:** the abstract does not state whether the radiocarbon-dated lacquer represents original manufacture or a later repair/re-lacquering layer; this distinction is simply unresolved and must not be assumed either way. No lamellar, bow, or quiver is named in the abstract at all. **Recommended wording:** "A KAKEN-funded project reports radiocarbon dating of lacquer and metal finds (swords, helmets) consistent with the second Mongol invasion; do not assume this dates original manufacture rather than a repair layer." **Drawing cue:** record-only, no drawing cue.
- **Bibliographic lead, not yet readable content:** Kimura, J. (2006), "Recent survey and excavation on the Mongolian fleet sunk off Japan: the Takashima underwater site," *Bulletin of the Australasian Institute for Maritime Archaeology*, vol. 30, pp. 7–13 — citation confirmed via the Western Australian Museum maritime archaeology bibliography (museum.wa.gov.au/maritime-archaeology-db/...); **access level: bibliographic metadata only**, no abstract or content accessed. This is the most promising lead for a genuine peer-reviewed excavation report and should be obtained with journal access before use.
- **Explicit negative finding:** no lamellar-armor lacing fragment, composite-bow fragment, or quiver fragment from Takashima was described in any source reached in this research pass. This is reported as a real evidentiary gap (it may exist in Japanese-language prefectural reports not accessed), not as proof such finds don't exist.

### Museum accession search (physical evidence, late 13th c.)

- **Claim:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art's "Ceremonial Armor" (commonly surfaced by "Yuan dynasty armor" searches) is genuinely Yuan/Mongol in style. **Verdict: CONTRADICTED.** **Source:** Metropolitan Museum of Art object record, metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/35831, accession numbers **16.118.29b–i,l,m; 32.75.301; 36.25.5a,2061**. **What it says:** the Met itself dates this object to the **18th century**, Chinese culture, and notes it closely resembles an armor set given to a Tibetan monastery in 1757; its helmet bears a gold Lantsa-script Buddhist mantra. **Access level:** catalog record/page text read; image not viewed. **This object post-dates the 1260–1295 window by roughly 450–500 years and must be explicitly excluded**, not used as Yuan/Mongol reference under any caption. **Drawing cue:** none — explicitly excluded.
- Ten other Met search-result URLs (pages 26917, 32076, 27805, 32641, 24963, 32077, 35961, 26531, 25112, 27399) were **not opened** in this pass; their dates and cultures are unknown and must not be assumed Yuan. No Tokyo National Museum, British Museum, or National Palace Museum Taipei accession for a securely late-13th-century helmet, lamellar armor, bow, or quiver was located or verified. **Overall verdict for this question: NOT FOUND.** No museum-dated (by the institution itself) c.1260–1295 helmet, lamellar armor, bow, or quiver accession record was found in this session. Any accession number used in a prior draft of this project that is not one of the Met numbers confirmed above (which is explicitly an 18th-century object) should be treated as unverified until someone opens the actual museum page.

### Pictorial evidence: Yuan court painting

- **Claim:** Liu Guandao's hanging scroll "Khubilai Khan Hunting" ("Kublai Khan Hunting"), held at the **National Palace Museum, Taipei**, is dated by the artist's own inscription, per the museum's reading, to **1280** — squarely inside the window — and depicts Kublai Khan and Empress Chabi with attendants, one bending a bow, another with a falcon, another with a hunting leopard. **Verdict:** supported, with the dating source clearly identified as an artist-dated inscription as reported by the museum (not an independent scientific dating). **Source:** National Palace Museum, Taipei, exhibition page, theme.npm.edu.tw/exh105/GatheringPrincess/en/page-5.html. **What it says:** hanging scroll, ink and colors on silk, 182.9 × 104.1 cm; mentions a bow being drawn but does **not** describe any helmet, armor, or quiver. **Access level:** museum webpage text read via WebFetch — **the image itself was not viewed** in this research pass. No accession/catalog number and no rights/credit statement appear on this page. **Corroboration:** Wikipedia's "Liu Guandao" article independently gives ~1280 and NPM as the holding institution, and raises no dating controversy for this specific painting (it does flag unrelated dating problems for two *other* works attributed to Liu Guandao, which must not be conflated with this one). **Recommended wording:** "The National Palace Museum dates this Liu Guandao hunting scroll to 1280 based on the artist's own inscription; the museum's text mentions a bow being drawn but no helmet, armor, or quiver — do not invent such details without someone viewing a high-resolution reproduction." **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue** — this is the single most promising contemporary pictorial lead in the entire armour category, but because no researcher actually viewed the image, no cue can be given yet. Someone must view a reliable high-resolution reproduction before any specific visual detail (helmet shape, lamellar pattern, quiver design) is drawn from it.
- **Claim/correction:** A separate, anonymous NPM scroll, "元人射雁圖" (Yuan Ren She Yan Tu, "Yuan Dynasty Painting of Shooting Wild Geese"), carries an old title-slip Yuan attribution but **no artist signature or seal**; the museum's own catalog essay proposes an **early-14th-century** date, based on resemblance to Emperor Chengzong (r. 1294–1307) — i.e., after the project's 1295 cutoff. **Verdict:** supported as to what the catalog page states; this is a **different painting** from the 1280 Liu Guandao scroll and must not be confused with it. **Source:** Taiwan Union Catalog of Digital Archives, catalog.digitalarchives.tw/item/00/03/fa/f9.html, item identifier 故畫000872N000000000. **Access level:** catalog page text read; image not viewed. **This is flagged explicitly as the kind of date-conflation trap the brief warns about** — an "old title slip" Yuan attribution that the museum's own scholarship pushes later; it must be labeled a post-1295 comparandum if used at all, never as contemporary with Polo.
- No game, film, or modern illustration was found cited as evidence anywhere in the sources actually checked for this category (the researcher had no access to any prior project reference sheet to audit for this specific failure mode, so this check could not be completed against this project's own materials — only a general methodological flag is offered).

**Recommended cautious wording for the illustrator (armour):** "Mongol marines/soldiers of the Takashima fleets may be shown with iron helmets and leather (not confirmed lamellar) body armor and iron-tipped arrows, per a diver's eyewitness account (Delgado 2003) and a government conservation-science abstract (KAKEN 17300293) — neither source was independently cross-checked against a full excavation report, and no lamellar lacing, bow, or quiver fragment from Takashima was found in any accessible source. Liu Guandao's 1280 'Khubilai Khan Hunting' (NPM, Taipei) is a genuinely contemporary court painting and the best pictorial lead available, but no one on this research pass viewed the image itself, so no specific drawing cue (helmet shape, lamellar pattern) can yet be extracted from it — a high-resolution reproduction must be examined directly first. Do not use the Met's 'Ceremonial Armor' (18th century) as Yuan/Mongol reference."

## Category 2 — Ships (II-12, II-14, III-02, III-06, III-08–09): PARTLY ANSWERED / NOT FOUND in parts

### Chinese seagoing hulls (II-12, II-14 context): PARTLY ANSWERED

- **Claim:** The Quanzhou ship (excavated 1974, under Zhuang Weiji) is a bulkheaded, three-masted Chinese hull whose sinking is dated by coin evidence to "within a few years after 1272." **Verdict:** supported. **Source:** Wikipedia, "Quanzhou ship," en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quanzhou_ship, citing Wake (1997, *International Journal of Maritime History*) and Li Guo-Qing (1989, *International Journal of Nautical Archaeology*). **What it says:** hull divided by 12 bulkheads into 13 compartments; plank seams sealed with chu-nam resin (also used at Satingpra, Thailand — construction mixing Chinese coastal and Southeast Asian technique); interpreted as three-masted; original estimated length 34.6 m, beam 9.82 m; preserved hull section 24.02 m × 9.15 m × 2 m deep. **Access level:** full encyclopedia page read; underlying journal articles not independently fetched. **Limitations:** no rudder or nailing detail is reported in the source checked (absence, not a negative finding); the sinking date sits right at 1272 — the Song/Yuan transition — not comfortably mid-window. Scholars speculated (explicitly framed as conjecture on the source page) that the crew scuttled the ship returning to Quanzhou after the city's fall to the Mongols. The hull is displayed at the Quanzhou Maritime Museum (Kaiyuan Temple grounds); a modern reconstruction model displayed alongside it is explicitly flagged as a **modern reconstruction, not evidence**. **Recommended wording:** "The Quanzhou ship (Quanzhou Maritime Museum) is a genuine excavated hull from right at the Song–Yuan transition (c.1272), with bulkheaded construction and resin-caulked seams independently documented; rudder and nailing details are not confirmed by the sources checked." **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue** — no image was viewed; this entry describes textual/catalog description of structural features, not a viewed photograph.
- **Comparanda, explicitly out of window:** the Shinan (Sinan) wreck sank c.1323 (~30–60 years too late) and is held at the National Research Institute of Maritime Cultural Heritage, Mokpo, South Korea; the Java Sea wreck's date is under active scholarly revision and was reached only via headline/snippet, not full text — treat its date as unresolved, not as "12th century" confirmed. Both wrecks share bulkheaded construction with Quanzhou, which supports bulkheads as a reasonably safe generic cue for "Chinese seagoing ship" in this general era, but neither should be presented as "the" 1260–1295 source.
- **Not found:** no Yuan-dynasty (contemporary, 1260–1295) painted or printed illustration of a Chinese seagoing ship was located. The Wubei Zhi ship woodcuts are Ming-dynasty (~17th century), roughly 300+ years later, and must be captioned "Ming-dynasty comparanda, not period-contemporary" if used at all. The Penglai shipwrecks (mentioned in the original brief) were not researched in this pass at all.

### Western Indian Ocean sewn-plank vessels (III-02, III-08–09 context): NOT FOUND (securely dated), with a flagged, pre-window manuscript lead

- **Claim:** The key academic review of sewn-boat evidence is Staples, Eric, and Blue, Lucy, "Archaeological, Historical, and Ethnographic Approaches to the Study of Sewn Boats," *International Journal of Nautical Archaeology* 48(2), 2019, pp. 269–285, DOI 10.1111/1095-9270.12361. **Access level: abstract/metadata only** — full text was not retrievable in this pass (ZU Scholars listing, zuscholars.zu.ac.ae/works/539; open-access mirror at eprints.soton.ac.uk returned only the same abstract page). No specific finds, sites, or dates from inside the article could be verified.
- **Claim:** The Belitung shipwreck is a real, well-documented 9th-century sewn-plank Arab/Indian-built vessel off Belitung Island, Indonesia. **Verdict:** supported, but **roughly 350–400 years too early** for the target window — usable only as deep-background comparanda for sewn-plank construction technique, never as period evidence. **Access level:** search-result titles/snippets (roots.gov.sg, Smithsonian, Cambridge Core); exact dendrochronological/radiocarbon range not independently fetched.
- **Claim/correction:** the famous "Maqamat ship" manuscript image, often cited for sewn-plank visual reference, is in the BnF's Schefer Maqamat al-Hariri, **shelfmark Arabe 5847**, with a colophon dated **9 May 1237 CE** (6 Ramadan 634 AH, a Saturday) on folio 167v. **Verdict:** supported as to the manuscript's date, but this **predates the 1260–1295 window by roughly 23–58 years** and must be flagged as such. **Source:** Public Domain Review, "Deceit and Disrobing: The Schefer Maqāmāt (BNF Arabe 5847, ca. 1237)," publicdomainreview.org/collection/maqamat/. Two folios (61r, 119v) show the protagonist boarding ships, but **no description of the ship images' actual visual content (hull, rig, sewing) was found** — the source confirms the manuscript's existence, date, and subject matter only, not a viewed description of the paintings. **Separately**, a conference abstract (Mick de Ruyter, SHA 2020 annual meeting; tDAR record core.tdar.org/document/457015) states this "Maqamat ship" image has been "the main iconographic source" for interpreting the Belitung wreck and for two full-sized replica ships, and explicitly says the image "has often been misinterpreted." **Access level: abstract only.** **Verdict: DISPUTED** — this image's reliability as a construction document for sewn-plank hulls is contested among specialists, not settled. **Recommended wording:** "The Schefer Maqamat manuscript (BnF Arabe 5847, dated 1237) is a real, dated manuscript predating the 1260–1295 window by roughly a quarter-century to half-century; its ship imagery has reportedly been used — and reportedly over-interpreted — as a model for sewn-plank hull reconstruction; no description of what the images actually show was obtained in this pass." **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue** — the image was not viewed by any researcher in this pass, and even if viewed, its reliability as construction evidence is independently disputed.
- **Not found:** no sewn-plank archaeological find specifically dated to 1260–1295 in the western Indian Ocean, Red Sea, or Gulf region was identified or confirmed. A candidate (an Al-Balid/Dhofar, Oman JASREP paper) was found in search results but not fetched or read — flagged as the clearest next step. Other illustrated Maqamat al-Hariri copies (British Library, St Petersburg, Istanbul) were not checked for closer-dated ship imagery.

### Mediterranean galleys (III-02, III-06, III-08–09 context): NOT FOUND

- **Claim:** At Yenikapı (Theodosian Harbour excavation, Istanbul), four of 37 excavated wooden ships are classified as galleys (YK13, YK16, YK25, YK36). **Verdict:** supported for existence/classification. **Source:** GRISSH, "Woods of the old galleys of Yenikapi, Istanbul," grissh.gr/article/5729baee5574414d7f000008. **What it says:** planking uses black pine, Lebanon cedar, sweet chestnut; floor timbers oriental plane and elm; keels oriental plane and oak; treenails Spanish broom and oak. **Critical limitation:** the only dating given by this source is the generic "**Byzantine Period**," with no specific year range — the source itself states that because the wood species are common and widely traded, construction origin cannot be determined from wood alone. **It is therefore unresolved whether any of these four hulls falls inside, or anywhere near, 1260–1295**, and they are Byzantine-context vessels, not Venetian or Genoese, regardless of date. Resolving this would require the primary Yenikapı excavation monographs, not this wood-species study. **Drawing cue:** record-only, no drawing cue.
- **Claim:** The Porta Sant'Alipio mosaic (Basilica di San Marco, Venice facade) is dated c.1270–1275 and depicts the arrival of St Mark's relics. **Verdict:** supported for date (see Category 3 below for full treatment). **Critical limitation specific to ships:** the one description actually checked (Smarthistory) describes the coffin/relics entering the church portal and does **not** mention a ship or boat being visibly present in this specific panel; a second source (arte.it) likewise does not mention one. **This must be treated as unconfirmed, not assumed present**, until someone views the mosaic directly or finds a source that explicitly describes a vessel in this panel. Other Cappella Zen panels (the "Dream of Saint Mark," showing the saint on a boat) exist but carry no confirmed date beyond "the basilica's mosaics date from different periods."
- **Not found:** no archaeological remains of a Venetian or Genoese galley specifically dated 1260–1295 were confirmed anywhere in this pass. Portolan charts, suggested in the original brief, were not researched at all (the Carte Pisane, usually dated late 13th century, is noted as a plausible but entirely unverified lead — and is a navigational chart, not primarily a ship-image source). No check was made of San Marco's restoration history, which is a standing caution: a c.1270–75 dating for a mosaic's original design does not establish every visible detail today is unretouched from that date.

**Recommended cautious wording for the illustrator (ships):** "The Quanzhou ship (Quanzhou Maritime Museum) is the strongest single anchor for a Chinese seagoing hull near the window (sinking c.1272), with bulkheaded, resin-caulked construction independently documented — but no contemporary illustration of such a ship was found, and the displayed reconstruction model is explicitly modern, not evidence. For Indian Ocean sewn-plank vessels, no securely dated 1260–1295 find or clearly-described contemporary image was located; the Belitung wreck (9th c.) and the Schefer Maqamat manuscript (1237, and of disputed reliability even then) are both outside the window and must be captioned as such. No Venetian, Genoese, or securely-dated Mediterranean galley image or wreck from 1260–1295 was found; the Yenikapı galleys are dated only to a broad 'Byzantine Period.'"

## Category 3 — Venice, Genoa, Ayas (I-01, I-04, III-10): PARTLY ANSWERED / NOT FOUND in parts

### Scene I-01 (Venice/Genoa merchant dress, harbor): PARTLY ANSWERED

- **Claim:** The Porta Sant'Alipio mosaic (left-hand arch, San Marco facade, Venice) depicts the translation of St Mark's relics and is dated **c.1270–75**, described by one source as "the only 13th-century mosaic still outside the church." **Verdict:** supported. **Sources:** Smarthistory, "Basilica San Marco (Saint Mark's Basilica), Venice," smarthistory.org/saint-marks-basilica-venice; arte.it, "Translation of the Body of Saint Mark," arte.it/art-guide/venice/must-see/work-of-art/translation-of-the-body-of-saint-mark-972. **What it says:** shows St Mark's coffin entering the main portal, with the **doge and patricians** — a ceremonial/clerical procession, not merchants in trading dress; the mosaic illustrates the basilica's own exterior appearance at the end of the 13th century. **Access level:** webpage text read via WebFetch; image not viewed by the researcher. **This is a religious/hagiographic narrative scene (translatio of relics), not documentary merchant evidence**, and must be captioned as such — it is useful for the basilica/piazza's architectural backdrop and for elite (not working-merchant) Venetian dress. **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue.**
- **Claim/correction:** a lead for a 13th-century Genoese fresco (Palazzo San Giorgio) does **not** check out. **Verdict: CONTRADICTED.** **Source:** Ports of Genoa, "The Exterior," portsofgenoa.com/en/port-authority/palazzo-san-giorgio/the-exterior.html. **What it says:** the building dates from 1260, but its seaward facade decoration dates to **1591** (Andrea Semino), restored/repainted **1606–08** (Lazzaro Tavarone), with further restorations in 1912 and 1992; the only genuinely 13th-century element is a spolia lion's-head sculpture from Constantinople. **No Genoese fresco or manuscript illumination of merchant dress confidently dated 1260–1295 was found anywhere in this pass** — this is reported as a genuine evidentiary gap, not a search failure; specialist archival art history (e.g., Archivio di Stato di Genova material) was not reached.
- **Harbor/quay imagery: NOT FOUND.** No 13th-century visual depiction of either the Venice or Genoa waterfront was found. A Cambridge/Radiocarbon paper on early medieval hydraulic structures at Ca' Foscari, Venice, was identified by title only (not fetched) and concerns an earlier period in any case. Jacopo de' Barbari's famous bird's-eye woodcut of Venice is dated **c.1500** — roughly 225 years after the window — and was not independently re-verified in this pass but must, if used at all, be labeled explicitly as late comparanda.

### Scene I-04 (Ayas / Laiazzo departure): PARTLY ANSWERED, thin by design

- **Claim/correction:** the standing fortification at Ayas is **not** period-authentic 13th-century fabric. **Verdict: CONTRADICTED.** **Source:** castles.nl, "Ayas Castle," castles.nl/ayas-castle. **What it says:** the present curved-wall, four-towered fortification "appear[s] to date almost entirely" from a rebuilding under **Suleiman the Magnificent in the first half of the 16th century**, using older spolia; the 12th-century Armenian-era castle it replaced is not described as surviving. **Access level:** single non-specialist (castle-touring) website, no author or footnotes visible; not cross-checked against an academic source. **Recommended wording:** "Do not depict the surviving ruins as the 13th-century fortress; any fortress wall shown must be an explicit reconstruction, not a documentary copy of standing remains." **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue.**
- **Claim:** a smaller harbor-guard castle, "Kız Castle," on an island in front of the harbor, was "built around **1282**" — squarely inside the window. **Verdict: DISPUTED/unverified** — this rests on the same single, non-specialist, uncorroborated source (castles.nl), with no citation of its own and no academic cross-check performed. **Recommended wording:** "A possible c.1282 harbor-fortification detail, currently resting on one uncorroborated non-specialist source — treat as provisional, not settled."
- **Claim:** Cilician Armenian tram and kardez coinage of Hetʻum I (r. 1226–1270) and Levon/Leo III (r. 1301–1307) exists. **Verdict:** existence plausible but **not independently verified** — the only sources found were commercial dealer listings (numisquare.com, dema-coins.com), not museum or scholarly catalogs, and were not fetched/opened. **No drawing cue can responsibly be drawn from these; recommend following up via the American Numismatic Society or British Museum rather than dealer sites.**
- **Not found:** no Ayas-specific fresco, manuscript illumination, or harbor depiction from 1260–1295 was found; no archaeological excavation report for the medieval Ayas harbor itself (as opposed to the fortress) was located.

### Scene III-10 (merchants' writing furniture): NOT FOUND for the period; two later comparanda identified and correctly excluded

- **Claim/correction:** two V&A objects surfaced as strong keyword matches but are both too late. **Verdict: CONTRADICTED** as period-contemporary objects. **Source 1:** V&A Collections, "Writing case," accession **5425-1859**, collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O321951/writing-case-unknown — black stamped cuir-bouilli case for book, pen, and ink-horn, inscribed "LAUS MAE," made in **Italy, 1490–1510**. **Source 2:** V&A Collections, "Pen case and lid," accession **8386-1863**, collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O133556/pen-case-and-unknown — moulded leather (cuir bouilli), made in **Italy, 15th century (1400–1500)**. **Access level for both:** open museum catalog pages read directly; images not independently assessed beyond the catalog description. **Both are 150–220 years too late** and must be captioned, if used at all, as "later (15th/early-16th-century) Italian comparanda for the general form of a merchant's portable writing kit — not contemporary with Polo's generation." **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue** (and explicitly excluded as period evidence regardless).
- A Metropolitan Museum "Case (étui) with an amorous inscription" and a Musei di Genova account-book listing (Battista De Luco, 1472–1476, explicitly 15th century) surfaced in searches but were **not fetched or verified** — flagged as unconfirmed leads only, not usable.
- **Not found:** no pen case, writing box, ink-horn, wax-tablet set, or account book confidently dated 1260–1295 was found anywhere in this pass. Notarial/chancery material-culture scholarship (suggested in the original brief) was not directly searched.

### Later Marco Polo manuscripts: correctly flagged as non-eyewitness, 100+ years later

- **Bodleian MS. Bodl. 264** (Oxford): the Marco Polo text is in "Part B," added in England (perhaps London) **c.1400–1410** — roughly 105–140 years after Polo's travels (1271–1295) and about 90 years after his death (1324); its exemplar was itself a copy of British Library Royal MS 19 D.i, made in Paris in the second quarter of the 14th century, meaning intermediate copying already occurred. **Source:** Bodleian medieval manuscripts catalog, manuscript_1315, medieval.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/catalog/manuscript_1315. **Access level:** catalog page read directly.
- **BnF Fr. 2810, "Livre des merveilles":** made in Paris **c.1410–1412** by workshops associated with the Mazarine Master, Boucicaut Master, and Bedford Master — roughly 115–141 years after Polo's travels. **Source:** Wikipedia, "Livre des merveilles (BNF Fr2810)." The folio noted (fol. 45r, trade/paper money at the Khan's court) does **not** depict Venice, Genoa, or merchant ships; any "merchants at a port" composition from this manuscript would need its own folio-level verification, not an assumption based on this folio.
- **Verdict on both:** these are real, dated, 14th/15th-century French courtly illuminations — useful only for compositional inspiration, and only if explicitly captioned as "later illustrated copy, not contemporary with Polo's own lifetime." They show how 15th-century Parisian illuminators imagined Polo's world, not how 13th-century Venice, Genoa, or Ayas looked.

**Recommended cautious wording for the illustrator (Venice/Genoa/Ayas):** "Venice: use the Porta Sant'Alipio mosaic (c.1270–75) only as architectural/elite-dress backdrop, captioned as a religious-narrative scene, not a merchant scene. Genoa: no verified 13th-century visual material of any kind was found for merchant dress, harbor, or writing furniture — state this plainly rather than substituting the (verified too-late) Palazzo San Giorgio frescoes. Ayas: the standing fortress is confirmed not to be 13th-century fabric; the c.1282 Kız Castle claim is a single, weak, uncorroborated lead. Writing furniture: no period object was verified; the two V&A pieces are real but 150–220 years too late and must be labeled as such."

## Category 4 — Dadu, Shangdu, Hangzhou, Quanzhou streets (I-13, II-02–03, II-10–14): PARTLY ANSWERED / NOT FOUND in parts

### Dadu (Beijing): PARTLY ANSWERED, and explicitly unresolved as to specific halls

- **Claim:** ongoing Palace Museum archaeology beneath the Forbidden City (led by Xu Haifeng, director of the Palace Museum's archaeology department) reports a "first Yuan-period layer" of foundations near the Longzong Gate, reported found in 2015. **Verdict:** supported as reported, but explicitly **unconfirmed/theoretical** as to whether this is Kublai Khan's palace — the source itself calls the case "tantalizing" with "experts still looking for more proof," and gives no stated scientific dating method (no radiocarbon or stratigraphic detail reported). **Source:** China Daily, "The riddle of the missing palaces," Sept. 2, 2025, chinadaily.com.cn/a/202509/02/WS68b628f3a3108622abc9e558.html. **Access level:** article text read via WebFetch. An earlier China Daily feature (March 10, 2022, govt.chinadaily.com.cn/.../palaces-hidden-glories-unearthed_7.html) quotes archaeologist Xu Jian explicitly framing the Yuan-city identification as still a "theory," and its own photo captions report **Ming**-dynasty roof tiles, not Yuan, at the same site. **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue** — and in this case the underlying archaeological attribution itself is still disputed by the excavators' own public statements, not merely unviewed.
- **Claim/correction:** the well-known "Yuan Dadu Site Park" along Beijing's north wall preserves **rammed-earth wall sections**, not palace halls. **Verdict:** supported for what it preserves, but the official municipal English page gives no dating method or excavation history, and is explicitly a managed/landscaped heritage-leisure site, not a raw excavation. **Source:** english.beijing.gov.cn, Yuan Dynasty Capital City Wall Relics Park page.
- **Not found / not independently checked:** the Houyingfang (后英房) excavation site, a commonly cited historical locus for Yuan Dadu residential/palace-adjacent architecture in older literature, could not be verified via a primary source in this pass — Chinese-language leads (Beijing Cultural Relics Bureau documents, a Guangming Daily series, a Chinanews.com.cn article) were found but not fetched. **Do not cite Houyingfang details without further verification.** No museum diorama or reconstruction model of Dadu's halls was identified, confirmed, or described by any source read — any such model referenced elsewhere (including in a prior project report) needs independent fresh verification.
- A genuinely relevant, verified academic book exists: Nancy Shatzman Steinhardt, *Yuan: Chinese Architecture in a Mongol Empire* (Princeton University Press, Dec. 12, 2023) — confirmed as a real, recent, illustrated survey covering Yuan palaces and cities including Shangdu, via the publisher's page (press.princeton.edu/node/59535). **Access level: publisher description only** — its specific Dadu/Shangdu content was not checked and must not be assumed; this is a high-value follow-up, not yet a usable source.

### Shangdu (Xanadu): ANSWERED for what survives; NOT ANSWERED for specific hall appearance

- **Claim:** Shangdu is UNESCO World Heritage Site "Site of Xanadu" (ID 1389); planned 1256 by Kublai Khan's Chinese advisor; functioned as Mongol capital 1263–1273, then Yuan summer capital 1274–1364; abandoned 1430. **Verdict:** supported — this is the official UNESCO list page, a primary institutional source. **Source:** UNESCO World Heritage List, whc.unesco.org/en/list/1389. **What it says:** wall lines of the Palace City, Imperial City, and Outer City "are still visible in the grassland"; mounds mark former palace/temple building sites; **some mounds have been excavated, recorded, and then reburied** — meaning the excavated material is not currently exposed. Conservation/repair work has been limited to the Mingde Gate and the east wall of the Imperial City. **Access level:** official UNESCO summary page read directly; the full nomination dossier was not read. **Important nuance for the illustrator:** because excavated hall foundations were reburied after recording, a present-day photograph of the site shows a reburied mound, not the structure the excavation actually documented — these are two different things. **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue** — no specific photograph of the Mingde Gate or Imperial City east wall was independently viewed or verified in this pass, so even the plausible inference that these two elements are the best-documented cannot yet be backed by a specific sourced image.

### Hangzhou: NOT FOUND (no contemporary image), with an explicit, important correction

- **Claim/correction:** the Qingming Shanghe Tu (清明上河圖) is frequently proposed as pictorial comparandum for a Chinese city street scene, but it **does not depict Hangzhou at all** — it depicts **Kaifeng**, attributed to Zhang Zeduan, conventionally Northern Song (roughly 150+ years before the 1260–1295 window), predating the Mongol conquest of Hangzhou entirely. **Verdict:** the Kaifeng-not-Hangzhou identification is well-established art-historical consensus, but it was **not independently re-verified against a primary/authoritative source within this research session** (the researcher flags this explicitly per the no-reliance-on-memory rule) — a Bridgeman Images stock-catalog listing gave Zhang Zeduan's life dates as "1085–1145," which is itself a secondary commercial source, not a museum catalog. **This should be re-confirmed from a museum source (e.g., the Palace Museum Beijing, which holds the Song-attributed version) before stating it as settled fact in a caption.** Later Ming/Qing copies of the scroll exist but are further removed still and reflect their own era's conventions. **Overall verdict: no verified period (1260–1295) visual source for Hangzhou's streets or markets exists in the sources checked.** Marco Polo's own famous description of "Kinsai" is a **text**, not an image, and must not be treated as visual reference.
- Southern Song textual sources such as the *Mengliang Lu* or *Wulin Jiushi* are named as potential textual cross-references but were **not checked** in this pass.

### Quanzhou: PARTLY ANSWERED for standing fabric; NOT FOUND for period imagery

- **Claim:** the Qingjing Mosque, part of the 2021 UNESCO-inscribed "Quanzhou: Emporium of the World in Song-Yuan China," was founded in **1009** (Northern Song) — well before the window, so the building existed and was in active use throughout 1260–1295. **Verdict:** supported by two independent sources. **Source 1:** Quanzhou municipal government website, UNESCO heritage page, quanzhou.gov.cn/gastronomy/en/about/unesco-heritage/202412/t20241228_3124003.htm. **Source 2:** UNESCO news page on the 2021 inscription, whc.unesco.org/en/news/2317 (independently gives "11th century AD"). **Critical correction:** the municipal page states a major renovation occurred in **1310** — 15–50 years **after** the window — and that "the site's current layout began to take shape" at that renovation. **This means the building's current/inscribed-site appearance cannot be assumed identical to its 1260–1295 appearance** without further checking; some visible features may postdate the target scenes. **Access level:** both official pages read directly. **Drawing cue: record-only, no drawing cue** (no photograph independently assessed for pre- vs. post-1310 features).
- **Not found:** no surviving Quanzhou city-wall section from the Song-Yuan period was mentioned by any source checked, and the UNESCO component list for the inscribed site (which explicitly names Islamic tombs, administrative buildings, stone docks, ceramic/iron production sites, the transportation network, bridges, pagodas, inscriptions) **notably omits city walls** — this is reported as a meaningful negative finding from the inscription's own official list, not merely unsearched. No period (13th-century) visual depiction of Quanzhou's streets or port area was identified. The "stone docks used for commerce and defence" named by UNESCO are a promising lead for port-scene reference but no specific photograph, excavation report, or precise dating detail for them was located or verified. The Quanzhou Maritime Museum's own publications (suggested in the brief, and which also hold the ship in Category 2) were not checked in this pass.

**Recommended cautious wording for the illustrator (cities):** "Dadu: base any palace-hall imagery on the explicit caveat that current Palace Museum excavation is ongoing and not yet conclusive — 'exact appearance of Yuan imperial halls is not established by current published archaeology.' Shangdu: 'based on UNESCO-documented wall lines and reburied building-mound excavations; no standing palace architecture survives above ground.' Hangzhou: 'no contemporary visual source identified; imagery is inferred from textual accounts including Marco Polo's own description; the Qingming scroll depicts a different city (Kaifeng) in an earlier period (Northern Song) and must not be used as Hangzhou evidence.' Quanzhou: 'Qingjing Mosque founded 1009, renovated 1310 — just after the target window; no period image of Quanzhou's streets or port was identified; UNESCO-listed stone docks exist but were not independently viewed in this research pass.'"

## Category 5 — Sumatra and South India (III-03, III-05–06): NOT FOUND for period figural/visual sources; textual and physical evidence only

### Sumatra (III-03): NOT FOUND for dress or boat imagery; one dated physical find for hull reasoning only

- **Claim:** the Samudera Pasai Sultanate (first ruler Malik al-Salleh, d. 1297, within/just after the window) is marked by a carved-gravestone tradition ("batu Aceh") beginning in the 13th century. **Verdict:** supported for the textual/tradition claim, but the largest surviving concentration (Kandang Aceh, ~200 graves) is dated **16th–17th century** — three-plus centuries later — and the decoration recorded is **aniconic**: botanical/geometric motifs framing Arabic calligraphy, with **no human figures and no dress depiction**. **Source:** Archnet, "Kandang Aceh," archnet.org/sites/4006. **Access level:** secondary summary page; no accession numbers or object-level dates for any single 13th-century stone were available. **This is a structural absence, not a survival gap** — Islamic funerary art in this tradition does not depict human figures at all, in this period or the better-documented later one.
- **Claim:** the Kota Cina trade settlement (North Sumatra) was occupied from the end of the 11th century to the end of the 13th/beginning of the 14th century (per McKinnon), with Miksic placing abandonment in the second half of the 13th century; a boat timber excavated 2011–2016 was AMS radiocarbon-dated to the **mid-12th to mid-13th century CE**. **Verdict: supported — this is the single best-dated piece of material evidence inside or adjacent to Polo's window for Sumatra.** **Source:** NUS ePress, Kota Cina site report, epress.nus.edu.sg/sitereports/samis/content/5.%20Kota%20Cina. **Limitation:** it is a physical ship timber, not an image of a boat — useful for hull-construction reasoning, not a ready-made pictorial composition. The same site yielded roughly a dozen stone/bronze religious sculptures (Cōḷa-style seated Buddhas, a headless four-armed Viṣṇu, a female deity fragment) — devotional icons, explicitly not genre scenes of dress or boats. Chinese copper coin finds are mostly Northern Song (Renzong, 1023–1063), useful only as trade-contact evidence.
- **Explicit correction — a common error flagged:** the Borobudur ship relief (double-outrigger sailing ship with bipod/tripod mast, canted square sail, bowsprit with spritsail, rowing gallery, deckhouse, oculi, quarter rudders) is **Javanese**, dated 8th–9th century, and is attributed by the source to Javanese Austronesian shipbuilding, which explicitly rejects a Srivijaya/Malay (Sumatran) origin. **Verdict: this is a double gap — geographic (Java, not Sumatra) and chronological (roughly 400–500 years too early) — and must be flagged on both counts, not elided.** **Source:** Wikipedia, "Borobudur ship."
- **Textual, not visual:** Marco Polo's own account places him passing through "Java Minor" (Sumatra, naming sub-kingdoms including Ferlec/Samudra) on the return voyage c.1292. His description of inhabitants is **text**, not an image, and must not be treated as pictorial evidence; the exact wording was not independently pulled from a critical edition (Yule–Cordier or Moule–Pelliot) in this pass — only paraphrase sites were consulted, and the report writer/illustration team should obtain the exact wording directly from a vetted edition.

### South India pearl fishery (III-05–06): NOT FOUND for period sculptural/relief evidence; one popular claim explicitly debunked

- **Claim:** Wikipedia's survey of Pandyan art and architecture covers mainly the 8th–9th centuries and gives no specific 13th-century ("Second Pandya Empire") sites or any maritime/boat imagery. **Verdict: NOT FOUND** (no boat/diving/dress imagery in Pandya sculpture per this source). **Source:** Wikipedia, "Pandyan art and architecture." **Access level:** tertiary encyclopedia source — a starting point only, not a substitute for specialist literature (e.g., journal articles on Pandya sculpture), which was not reached. Fish imagery does appear on Pandyan punch-marked coins and the double-fish flag/emblem, and pearls/gold were used for ornaments "sold to overseas buyers" — heraldic/numismatic and jewelry-trade material, not a depiction of pearl diving or fisherfolk.
- **Claim explicitly debunked:** a popularly-asserted "Chola ship coin" (Rajendra Chola I, r. 1014–1044, Biddulph #25/Mitchiner #741-745) does **not** show a ship. **Verdict: CONTRADICTED.** **Source:** coins.lakdiva.org.lk, Rajendra Chola gold coin, coins.lakdiva.org.lk/medievalindian/rajendra_chola_au.html. **What it actually shows:** a seated tiger facing two upright fish, with a bow and umbrella, and "Rajendra Chola" in Nagari script. **This is reported as an important negative finding** so this claim is not recycled into the final narrative — and even a genuine Chola ship-motif coin, if one exists elsewhere, would still be Chola (not Pandya) and roughly 200–250 years before the window.
- **A specialist lead, not yet checked:** a journal article, "Early Pandya Simhavahini and Sapta Matrka Sculptures in the Far South of India" (journal.equinoxpub.com/ROSA/article/view/11025), exists but was not accessed; its title suggests goddess iconography, not boats/fisherfolk — low likelihood of relevance, flagged for further digging only if wanted.
- **Textual, not visual:** Marco Polo's own account of the Maabar/Coromandel pearl fishery is a genuine, contemporary textual source but is not visual; its exact wording was not independently verified from a critical edition in this pass (same caveat as Sumatra above).

### Explicitly flagged later comparanda, and sources explicitly excluded

- The clearest available pearl-fishery illustration is an 1887-or-1889 engraving, "Day on the dhow," published in the British periodical *The Graphic* — **the source's own caption date and its image-filename date disagree**, and the report flags this internal inconsistency rather than silently picking one. **Source:** Himal Southasian, "The Paars of Mannar," himalmag.com/the-paars-of-mannar/. **Access level:** article text read; **the image itself was not viewed.** This is roughly **595–597 years** removed from Polo's c.1292 passage and must be captioned as colonial-era method-comparanda only, never as costume or vessel-type evidence. The article's own accompanying operational detail (15-ton Persian Gulf dhows, crews of 14 plus 10 divers, fleets of ~400 boats, a gun signal) is specifically **18th–19th-century colonial-period detail and must not be back-dated** to the 13th century, even though the general diving-pair/weight/tender method may represent a longer-duration practice.
- Ibn Battuta visited the Jaffna/Puttalam pearl coast in **September 1344** — a genuine independent textual witness, but roughly 50–85 years **after** Polo, and not to be merged into a single account with Polo's description. **Source:** Arab News, "How Arabs brought luster to Sri Lanka's historic pearl trade."
- **Explicitly excluded as sources:** a set of search results from a site called "temporiscope.com," with titles like "Tamil pearl divers in the 12th century Gulf of Mannar," labeled by invented era categories ("Renaissance," "High Middle Ages," "Classical Antiquity"), was identified and **deliberately not used** — its structure (genre-labeled, gallery-style URLs assigning named historical scenes to arbitrary period categories) is consistent with AI-generated illustrative content, not a verifiable historical artifact or scholarly source. This is flagged explicitly so this material is never mistaken for genuine period evidence by anyone working from this report.

**Recommended cautious wording for the illustrator (Sumatra/South India):** "No 13th-century Sumatran image of dress or boats is known to survive; base costume on Marco Polo's own textual description (verified against a critical edition, not a paraphrase site), and base boat/hull reasoning on the Kota Cina boat-timber archaeology (a physical find, not a picture); use the Borobudur ship relief only as distant, clearly-labeled Javanese/8th–9th-century comparanda. No Pandya-period sculptural or relief depiction of pearl diving, diving boats, or fisherfolk dress is known; treat Marco Polo's own account of the Maabar pearl fishery as the primary period source, textual only; colonial-era (1880s) illustrations may be used explicitly as comparanda with a ~500–600-year gap stated plainly. Do not use the 'Chola ship coin' claim — it does not hold up on inspection."

## Fallback approaches where period evidence is thin or absent

For every gap above, invented composite imagery is the wrong fix. The defensible fallback in each case is to go narrower, not wider:

- **Armour (I-05, II-01, II-08):** until someone views a high-resolution reproduction of Liu Guandao's 1280 "Khubilai Khan Hunting" directly, do not attempt specific helmet/lamellar/quiver detail from it. Fall back to the archaeologically-attested elements alone — an iron helmet and leather/lacquered armor fragments, per Delgado 2003 — without inventing lamellar lacing, bow profile, or quiver design, which are not attested anywhere in the sources reached.
- **Mediterranean galleys (III-02, III-06, III-08–09):** no defensible period image or securely-dated wreck was found. Either omit specific galley detail and keep the vessel generic/distant in the composition, or base it narrowly on the Yenikapı galley timbers' known wood species and general hull classification, captioned as "Byzantine-period, date within the hull's broad range not established," rather than inventing Venetian/Genoese decorative or rigging detail with no basis.
- **Ayas harbor and fortress (I-04):** do not depict the standing ruins as 13th-century fabric — they are a 16th-century Ottoman rebuild. Either show a plausible, explicitly-labeled reconstruction, or minimize specific architectural detail and rely on the general historical fact of Ayas as a Genoese/Venetian trading port without inventing wall profiles.
- **Writing furniture (III-10):** no period object exists in what was checked. The safest approach is to base the scene on known 13th-century notarial/mercantile practice in general terms — wax tablets, quill, ink-horn, loose parchment or paper rolls — without copying the specific decorative form of the V&A's 15th/16th-century cases, and to caption any such case explicitly as a later comparandum if shown at all.
- **Sumatra dress and boats (III-03):** no period figural image survives. Base costume strictly on Marco Polo's own text (sourced from a critical edition), and base hull reasoning strictly on the Kota Cina boat-timber archaeology — a timber find, not a picture — without inventing a composite "Sumatran ship" image. If a visual silhouette is needed, the Borobudur relief may be used only as explicitly-labeled distant Javanese comparanda, never presented as Sumatran.
- **South India pearl fishery (III-05–06):** no period sculptural or relief evidence survives. Base the scene on Marco Polo's own textual account of the Maabar fishery, and if a visual anchor is wanted, use the 1880s *Graphic* engraving explicitly captioned as colonial-era comparanda with the ~590-year gap stated, never as 13th-century evidence. Do not use the "Chola ship coin."
- **Hangzhou streets (II-10 to II-14 context):** no contemporary image survives, and the Qingming scroll is the wrong city and the wrong dynasty. Rely on Marco Polo's own textual description plus general, explicitly-labeled Song-Yuan urban painting convention, not a copy of the Kaifeng scroll relabeled as Hangzhou.
- **Dadu's imperial halls (I-13):** current archaeology is explicitly unresolved and partly contradicted by the excavators' own public caution (Ming tiles found at the same site). Caption any hall imagery as "based on ongoing, inconclusive excavation; exact appearance not established," rather than presenting a confident reconstruction.

Taken together, the five categories show the same shape: real finds and real dated images exist at the edges (Takashima, Quanzhou ship, Kota Cina timber, Liu Guandao's 1280 scroll, the Porta Sant'Alipio mosaic, Shangdu's wall lines), but almost none of them have been visually inspected by a researcher in this project, and several plausible-looking shortcuts — the Met's Yuan-style armor, the Qingming scroll, the Borobudur relief, the Ayas ruins, the V&A writing cases, the "Chola ship coin" — fail specifically because someone finally checked the date or the attribution against the holding institution's own record. The next concrete step for every "record-only, no drawing cue" entry above is the same: obtain and view a verified, high-resolution reproduction directly from the holding institution before drawing anything from it, and treat every unchecked lead listed in the gaps section as exactly that — a lead, not a source.
