## Substantive Disagreements and Limits

**Catalan Atlas provenance.** Montserrat Galera i Monegal’s **NEW (2016)** abstract describes the atlas as a commission from the Catalan dynasty to the Cresques workshop, intended as a gift to Charles V (Monegal, 2016). Muhammed Ali Budak’s **NEW (2025)** account instead describes a commission by the future John I and a gift from Peter IV to Charles VI in 1381 (Budak, 2025).

A third **NEW (2021)** chapter, “Pluricultural Sources of the Catalan Atlas,” places the atlas in Charles V’s library by 1380 (, 2021). Its attribution to approximately 1375 and to Cresques Abraham is presented as conventional rather than as a reproduced commission record (, 2021). These descriptions cannot establish that the surviving object is the map mentioned in a particular royal dispatch. The inventory and correspondence must be inspected before choosing between the narratives.

**Toscanelli.** The 1920 review identifies Henry Vignaud as challenging the traditional discovery narrative and Hermann Wagner and Carlo Errera as his principal opponents (D., 1920). This establishes a historical controversy, not the present balance of specialist opinion. Giancarlo Truffa’s **NEW (2014)** “Toscanelli dal Pozzo, Paolo” is identified by metadata, but no readable argument is supplied; consequently, it cannot establish transmission, authenticity, or the dates of surviving copies.

**Return voyage.** Lin and Ran’s **NEW (2017)** literature review reports that defenders of Polo invoke Chinese and Arabic accounts as historical corroboration of his return journey (Lin & Ran, 2017). This is explicitly a **scholar’s description of other sources**, not direct inspection of those sources. It does not establish a departure year, distinguish an imperial order from embarkation, or show that a particular notice names the Polos. Assigning precise positions to Cleaves or Haw would exceed the available evidence.

**Engineers and manuscript transmission.** Wood’s supplied excerpt supports only a broad attribution to Persian engineers. It does not identify their hometowns or demonstrate correspondence between Chinese and Persian names. Likewise, no inspected passage establishes Ahmad’s presence in Z: textual affinity elsewhere cannot substitute for locating the episode itself.

**Family evidence.** Olshin’s daughter identification is secondary testimony. His discussion expressly acknowledges unresolved questions surrounding the purported family-associated maps and documents (Olshin, 2014). Bolognari’s **NEW (2020)** archival discovery concerns Marco Polo’s relationship with Dominican friars, not the requested wife-and-daughters proof (Bolognari, 2020). Neither item verifies Agnese or documents a marriage date.
