Heng (2009)HighIII-02, E54Scholarly Synthesis and Disagreement AnalysisE35: Chandrabhānu of Tāmbraliṅga and the Thirteenth-Century Maritime Invasions of Sri LankaThe historical trajectory of Chandrabhānu, the Javaka monarch of Tāmbraliṅga (centered at Nakhon Si Thammarat / Ligor on the Malay Peninsula, corresponding to Henry Yule’s preferred identification for Marco Polo’s Locac), represents a central pivot in thirteenth-century trans-Indian Ocean geopolitical dynamics. The epigraphic baseline for Chandrabhānu’s reign is established by the Sanskrit Chaiya (Jaiya) Inscription, discovered at Wat Hua Veng in Chaiya. This inscription is explicitly dated to the Śaka year 1152, which converts directly to 1230 AD. In the text, Chandrabhānu is adorned with the royal titles of Śrī Dharmarāja and identified as the ruler of the kingdom of Tāmbraliṅga.Chronological and narrative accounts of Chandrabhānu’s military campaigns in Sri Lanka derive primarily from the Cūlavaṃsa (chapters 83 and 88, translated by Wilhelm Geiger) and the critical epigraphic syntheses of George Coedès and Amaradasa Liyanagamage (based on his 1963 SOAS doctoral dissertation). The primary historical sources outline two distinct invasions:First Invasion (c. 1247 AD): According to Cūlavaṃsa 83:36–51, Chandrabhānu landed a naval force of Javaka troops on the southern coast of Sri Lanka during the 11th regnal year of King Parākramabāhu II of Dambadeniya. Posing initially as a fellow Buddhist ruler seeking the sacred Tooth and Alms Bowl relics, Chandrabhānu launched an offensive across the island. His forces were eventually repulsed by the Sinhalese prince Vīrabāhu at Yapahuwa (Siriyābāla). Coedès, Geiger, and Liyanagamage unanimously fix the date of this initial expedition to c. 1247 AD.Second Invasion (c. 1260–1262 AD): Cūlavaṃsa 88:62–75 documents a second invasion launched by Chandrabhānu, who assembled a formidable coalition comprising Javaka troops and Tamil mercenaries recruited from the Pāṇḍya and Chola regions of South India. Chandrabhānu fortified positions in northern Sri Lanka (Yapahuwa and Subhagiri) and demanded total submission from Dambadeniya. This second campaign precipitated a major geopolitical realignment when the Pāṇḍyan empire intervened.The resolution of chronological discrepancies surrounding Chandrabhānu’s death and legacy relies on reconciling the Cūlavaṃsa with South Indian Pāṇḍyan prasastis (such as the Kudumiyamalai and Tiruppullani inscriptions of Jaṭāvarman Sundara Pāṇḍya I and his co-regent Jaṭāvarman Vīra Pāṇḍya). While Cūlavaṃsa narratives credit Prince Vīrabāhu with defeating the Javaka forces, Pāṇḍyan epigraphy confirms that Jaṭāvarman Vīra Pāṇḍya invaded Sri Lanka around 1262 AD, slew Chandrabhānu in battle, and exacted tribute from the Javaka kingdom.As Liyanagamage established in his SOAS thesis, Chandrabhānu’s son subsequently assumed power over the northern Javaka territories of Sri Lanka (centered in the Jaffna Peninsula at locations still bearing Javaka toponyms, such as Chavakachcheri). This son ruled as a vassal under Pāṇḍyan suzerainty into the 1270s. He launched a subsequent assault against Dambadeniya with Pāṇḍyan support before being finally overthrown when the Pāṇḍyan general Ārya Cakravarti established direct Tamil dominance over the Jaffna kingdom in the late 1270s and early 1280s.E37a: Epigraphic and Material Authenticity Debate of the Ram Khamhaeng InscriptionThe authenticity debate concerning Sukhothai Inscription No. 1 (the Ram Khamhaeng Stele, dated in its text to 1292 AD) remains one of the most intense historiographical controversies in Southeast Asian studies. The debate centers on whether the stele is an authentic 13th-century monument erected during the reign of King Ram Khamhaeng of Sukhothai or a 19th-century literary composition created after its discovery at Sukhothai in 1833 by Prince Mongkut (later King Rama IV).Scholarly Polarization and Gedney's NomenclatureDuring the height of the controversy at international symposiums in the late 1980s and early 1990s, the academic community split into two well-defined camps. The revisionist skeptics were led by art historian Piriya Krairiksh and historian Michael Vickery, who argued that the inscription's unique orthography (placing all vowel signs on the baseline alongside consonants), its advanced economic vocabulary, and its depiction of an idealized Buddhist monarchy reflected 19th-century Bangkok intellectual concerns rather than 13th-century realities.Opposing the revisionists was an alliance of Thai and Western philologists and epigraphists, including A. B. Griswold, Prasert na Nagara, David K. Wyatt, Anthony V. N. Diller, Barend Jan Terwiel, Hans Penth, and Hiram W. Woodward Jr., who maintained the stele's 13th-century origin. In his keynote commentary at the 1989/1991 Siam Society symposium, the distinguished Tai linguist William J. Gedney famously referred to these opposing factions as the "Angels" (the defenders of authenticity) and the "Demons" (the proponents of the 19th-century forgery hypothesis).Vickery's Contributions to the Chamberlain VolumeIn the canonical anthology edited by James R. Chamberlain, The Ram Khamhaeng Controversy: Collected Papers (Bangkok: The Siam Society, 1991), Michael Vickery published two major essays asserting the forgery hypothesis:"The Ram Khamhaeng inscription: a Piltdown skull of Southeast Asian history?" (pp. 3–52)."Piltdown skull: installment 2" (pp. 333–418).In these essays, Vickery explicitly likened the stele to the famous "Piltdown Man" paleoanthropological hoax, arguing that the text was fabricated to provide historical legitimacy for 19th-century Thai state formation and royal lineage.Scientific Surface Analysis (1990)To resolve the debate through empirical physical data, Chiraporn Aranyanak (in collaboration with Srisopa Maranate), a conservation scientist at the National Museum of Thailand, conducted a comprehensive archaeometric investigation of the stone stele in 1990 using Optical Microscopy, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectrometry (EDX).The petrographic analysis established that the stele consists of a natural calcareous siltstone composed of quartz, feldspar, and a calcite (calcium carbonate) matrix, a rock type native to northern Thailand. The SEM/EDX investigation examined the micro-structure of the exposed outer surface versus the internal unexposed stone and the carved letter grooves.The analysis revealed that centuries of outdoor chemical weathering had dissolved calcite and feldspar from the exposed outer surfaces, leaving a porous, micro-cavity-filled texture with depleted aluminum and calcium levels. Crucially, examination of a thin section cut across an inscribed character stroke on the third face demonstrated a calcite-depleted alteration layer measuring 0.20 to 0.25 mm deep directly inside the carved grooves. This proved scientifically that the inscription was carved shortly after the stone was cut and polished, long before it underwent centuries of outdoor weathering. Because the weathering rind inside the letter grooves matched control samples from authentic 14th-century Sukhothai stelae (such as Inscription No. 3 and Inscription No. 45), the 1990 SEM/EDX study demonstrated that the inscription could not have been cut in the 19th century.[Calcareous Siltstone Matrix: Quartz, Feldspar, Calcite]

Monograph Synthesis, UNESCO Inscription, and Current ConsensusIn 2010, Barend Jan Terwiel published a definitive monograph titled The Ram Khamhaeng Inscription: The Fake That Did Not Come True (Reihe Gelbe Erde 5, Ostasien Verlag, Gossenberg, 2010, ISBN 978-3-940527-39-4).
