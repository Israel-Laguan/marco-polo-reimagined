## Part 2 — Disagreements explained

**Frances Wood vs. Rachewiltz/Rossabi/Morgan/Haw/Vogel on Polo's "silences" and overall authenticity (rows 18–22, 37, 50–51, 70).** Frances Wood's *Did Marco Polo Go to China?* (1995) argues from a cluster of omissions — the Great Wall, tea-drinking, foot-binding, chopsticks, Chinese/Mongol writing, woodblock printing, cormorant fishing — plus Polo's absence from Chinese gazetteers and several apparent errors (miscounted bridge arches, inaccurate Suzhou terrain, the impossible Xiangyang-siege claim) that Polo likely never went beyond Persia and compiled his account from Persian/Arabic intermediary sources and family lore. Igor de Rachewiltz (1997) rejected this as methodologically unsound; Morris Rossabi argued Polo's descriptions of the postal system, Dadu, Hangzhou, and the court show firsthand knowledge; David Morgan found basic errors in Wood's own scholarship; Stephen Haw (2006) dismantled the "search for Marco Polo by name in Chinese records" argument (medieval Europeans lacked fixed surnames) and was judged by reviewer Peter Jackson to have "likely settled" the controversy; Hans Ulrich Vogel (2012/13) added a detailed positive case from currency, salt, and revenue data that no other contemporary non-Chinese writer matches. None of these four primary works — Wood, Rachewiltz, Haw, or Vogel — was read directly in any research-notes file; every characterization above is paraphrased from secondary summaries, reviews, or Wikipedia. The debate should be presented as genuinely live rather than resolved: post-2006 specialist opinion has shifted toward accepting Polo's China sojourn, but Wood's case still has at least one sympathetic reviewer (Laxman Satya, 1999), and two of her most commonly cited silences — tea-drinking and foot-binding — have **no located rebuttal at all** in any file, which should be flagged to the reader as an open gap rather than smoothed over as "answered."

**Aung-Thwin's revisionism on the "fall of Pagan" vs. the traditional (Yule/Phayre-adjacent) narrative (rows 55–57).** The traditional narrative — inherited from 19th/early-20th-century historiography, including Yule's own notes and G.E. Harvey's 1925 *History of Burma* — holds that the 1287 Mongol campaign resulted in the conquest and sack of Pagan, precipitating the Pagan Empire's collapse. Michael Aung-Thwin's revisionist scholarship (*Pagan: The Origins of Modern Burma*, 1985; *Myth and History in the Historiography of Early Burma*, 2002 2nd ed.) argues this is largely a colonial-era historiographical construction: no contemporary Mongol or Chinese record documents the actual conquest of Pagan city, "recent research" holds the Mongols probably stopped roughly 160 km north of it, and even granting they reached it, damage was likely minimal — a position an 1293 inscription by three Burmese ruling brothers (claiming they, not the Mongols, were victorious) also supports. This matters directly for Polo's "Battle of Vochan" (II-08): the battle itself (dated by Yule to 1277, correcting Polo's 1272) is treated by Yule as real and corroborated in Chinese annals, independent of whatever happened or didn't happen at Pagan a decade later in 1287 — so the revisionist Pagan thesis, if accepted, narrows but does not eliminate the plausibility of a real frontier engagement. Crucially, **Aung-Thwin's own primary texts were not read in any research-notes file** — this entire picture rests on a Wikipedia synthesis of his work, which is a significant verification gap given how load-bearing this revisionist claim is for the retelling's framing of the Mongol-Burmese wars.

**Yule vs. Pauthier vs. Cordier on the toponyms Caugigu, Anin, Coloman, Cuiju (rows 60–64).** This is a disagreement internal to the critical-edition tradition itself, not something later scholarship introduced. For Caugigu, Yule confidently reads it as (part of) Laos and explicitly rejects Pauthier's rival identification with Chiangmai, while Cordier (Yule's own later collaborator/reviser) goes further and reads it as Tungking/Kiao-chi (northern Vietnam) instead. For Anin, Yule leans toward the Ho-nhi/Ngo-ning tribes of southeastern Yunnan (with the French explorer Francis Garnier's own caution about the unreliability of local-name identification noted), while Cordier reads "Aniu" as a transcription of "Nan-yué." For Coloman, Yule favors the Kolo (proto-Lolo/Yi) tribes and rejects Pauthier's Kwangsi placement as "impossible," while Garnier doubted any resemblance to "Kolo" at all, and Cordier instead proposed "To-lo-man." For Cuiju, Yule's "most probable" identification with Kwei-chau (Guizhou) — following Marsden, endorsed more firmly by Pauthier — is undercut by Yule's own admission that a subsidiary place-name in the same passage ("Fungul") remains unexplained. No modern (post-1920s) scholarship was located in any research-notes file that resolves or meaningfully supersedes this three-way Yule/Pauthier/Cordier dispute; Paul Pelliot's posthumous *Notes on Marco Polo* (1959–1973) is almost certainly the key missing source and should be consulted before the retelling asserts any of the four identifications as settled.

**The Vochan battle's historicity: Yule/Baber vs. Phayre (row 55).** Within Yule's own 1903 edition, two of his own contributors disagree: H.A. Baber treats the battle as historical fact (a "dire and parlous fight"), while Sir Arthur Phayre — author of the standard 19th-century *History of Burma* — was inclined to reject its historicity altogether; Yule sided with Baber but noted the disagreement respectfully. No modern (post-1920s) source was found reconciling this dispute with new evidence, so it remains open.

## Part 4 — Unresolved questions (by book section)

**II-01.** Whether Polo's elephant-borne-command-platform detail for the 1287 Nayan battle is attested in the *Yuan shi* itself, independent of the Polo-derived narrative tradition — not found. No Chinese-language (CNKI-indexed) scholarship on Nayan's rebellion was located. No post-2013 peer-reviewed article specifically re-examining the 1287 campaign (beyond Jackson 2021, which does not focus on it specifically) was located.

**II-02.** Direct verification of Polo's Kublai-appearance passage and the "4 wives"/sons passage against the original 1903/1920 Yule-Cordier print edition (only a secondary web aggregation was used). Rossabi's 1979 chapter and 1988 biography were never read directly — all Chabi/Zhenjin claims rest on Wikipedia's citation of them. A specific peer-reviewed Kaogu/Wenwu excavation report on Dadu's palace-city foundations (e.g., Houyingfang) was not located. Nancy Shatzman Steinhardt's own publications on Dadu's planning were never consulted directly. No rebuttal was found addressing Polo's silences on tea-drinking or foot-binding specifically. None of Wood, de Rachewiltz, Haw, or Vogel's primary texts were read directly — direct access to all four is strongly recommended before finalizing annotations, especially for exact quotations.

**II-03.** Allsen's "Guard and Government" (1986) and Hsiao Ch'i-ch'ing (1978) — the two key specialist works on keshig structure — were never retrieved; the "150 vs. 1000" initial keshig-size discrepancy in the secondary source used was not resolved. No modern reconstruction of the "White Feast"/birthday feast against *Yuanshi* ritual-treatise material was found. Polo's specific "thirteen feasts" figure and a formal institution named "Festival of the Cloth of Gold" were not confirmed, and the relationship between 詐馬宴 (zhama yan) and 質孫宴 (zhisun yan) terminology was not resolved. The 100,000-white-horses birthday gift claim has no located source at all.

**II-04.** Allsen's *Royal Hunt* (2006) was never read for its specific cheetah/lynx/tiger content. No source on gyrfalcons or the Yuan 鷹坊 (yingfang) falcon-bureau institution was found. Station-count figures for the Yuan-heartland zhanchi postal system were not verified (a commonly cited "1,400+" figure was explicitly NOT confirmed and should not be used without further verification). Endicott-West (1989) and CHC vol. 6's xingsheng chapter were never read directly. No source on changpingcang/yicang famine-relief granaries specific to the Yuan/Kublai was found (only Qing-era material surfaced). No source on Kublai's "road trees" claim was found at all.

**II-05.** The exact 1287 zhongtongchao→zhiyuanchao conversion ratio and an itemized Zhiyuan denomination list were not found. The present holding institution of the surviving "1287" printing plate was not confirmed beyond a secondary image caption. **Vogel's book itself was never read**, and the more rigorous BSOAS review of it was blocked from access — this is flagged as the single most consequential gap in the entire project, since Vogel is the central modern authority for the "Polo was in China" case built on economic-history evidence. Hartwell's coal/iron article could not be fetched. The Islamic Astronomical Bureau (回回司天監) at the Yuan court and the specific nature of Jamal al-Din's collaboration with Guo Shoujing remain under-documented here. Allsen's chapter on Bolad (in *Culture and Conquest*) was never read. No peer-reviewed article specifically on "Wang Zhu's 1282 conspiracy" was located. A direct, passage-by-passage comparison of Polo's own Ahmad-assassination narrative against the modern reconstruction was not performed (the base text was not in hand).

**II-06.** No peer-reviewed archaeological/architectural-historical study of Lugou Qiao itself was found (Chinese-language academic-database search was not attempted). No modern scholarship on Taiyuan/Pingyang's Yuan-era urban condition was located. The claimed continuity between Polo's Chengdu "great bridge" and the modern (historically wooden) Anshun Bridge was not archaeologically verified and should be treated as a tourism-tradition claim, not an established identification.

**II-07.** Polo's Tibet "devastation and bamboo forest" passage was never verified against the primary text. The specific administrative-history claims (Wylie, Petech, Rossabi, Franke) were cited only via a Wikipedia mirror, not read directly. No source bridges Dian-era and Yuan-era cowrie-currency continuity in Yunnan. No source ties couvade specifically to the Zardandan/Jinchi (Doja 2005 is the most promising unread lead). Detailed primary/secondary scholarship on the 1253 Dali campaign's route and immediate aftermath was not located.

**II-08.** Aung-Thwin's own primary texts (1985, 2002) were never read — the "fall of Pagan is a myth" argument rests entirely on a Wikipedia synthesis and should be verified with real page numbers before publication. G.E. Harvey's 1925 *History of Burma* (the explicit baseline comparator requested) was never consulted. No modern (post-2013) source specifically revisiting the "Battle of Vochan" identification, or specifically engaging with Polo's "gold and silver towers" passage against current Pagan-period stupa chronology, or specifically revisiting Polo's Bangala passage, was located.

**II-09.** No modern (post-1920s) toponymic scholarship resolving Caugigu, Anin, Coloman, or Cuiju was found. **II-10.** The original Yule-Cordier footnote on "Bayan Hundred Eyes" could not be retrieved in this pass (needs direct consultation of the physical/full text). No scholarly monograph on Song-Yuan foundling institutions, 舉子倉 (juzicang) charity granaries, or the 慈幼局 network specifically covering the Yuan period was found.

**II-11.** Vogel's full text and the Zhu Jiang/Peng Hai Chinese-language scholarship underlying his Yangzhou-post reassessment were never read directly — only via the Na Chang review; the "1282–84" Yangzhou dating was not cross-verified against a second source. The current physical location/custody of the Vilioni tombstones was not confirmed. Whether Pelliot's posthumous *Notes on Marco Polo* discusses the Vilioni tombstones (discovered 1951, after Moule & Pelliot's 1938 edition) was not checked. Stephen Haw's 2006 book — the most relevant monograph by a scholar favorable to Polo's authenticity — was never directly consulted regarding the Xiangyang-siege-engine claim specifically.

**II-12.** No Yuan-dynasty customs/toll records or modern quantitative study of Yangtze/Grand Canal vessel counts at Yizhen/Zhenjiang was found. "Sinju" = Yizhen (儀真) was not independently re-verified against a modern toponymic source. Specific Yuan canal-construction stages/dates (e.g., the Huitong Canal) were not verified. No peer-reviewed modern treatment of Yuan-era Suzhou's canals/bridges, or any comparison of Polo's Suzhou claims against the Pingjiang Map, was located. The exact bibliographic identity (author, journal, year) of the single source used for the Mar Sargis/Zhenjiang-Christians material was not confirmed — flagged for re-verification before citation. No modern monograph-level treatment of Yuan Nestorianism (in the tradition of Pelliot, Moule's *Christians in China Before the Year 1550*, or Li Tang) was read in full.

**II-13.** No modern source gives an agreed actual bridge count or wall circumference for Song-Yuan Hangzhou to set against Polo's "12,000 bridges"/"100 miles." The five population-estimate scholars (Morris, Elvin, Modelski, Bielenstein, Gernet) were accessed only through one secondary synthesis, never in their own original works. No rigorous modern scholarly assessment of Polo's West Lake description against Song-Yuan evidence was found. A.C. Moule's own argument and conclusions about the "Queen of Kinsai" letter (*Quinsai*, 1957) were never verified (the book and its reviews are paywalled). Specific excavation findings at the Southern Song imperial-city site (Fenghuang Mountain) were not retrieved. Whether any Yuan-period household-registration/fire-watch/bathhouse institution specifically existed in Hangzhou (as opposed to the Song-era baojia system, which the sources consulted do not extend to the Yuan at all) remains unconfirmed — flagged explicitly as an anachronism risk.

**II-14.** No dedicated modern monograph or archaeological study on Yuan-era Fuzhou (as opposed to Quanzhou) was found; Chinese-language regional gazetteers/archaeology reports almost certainly exist but were not located. The full UNESCO nomination dossier for Property 1561 (which would give page-level specificity and the exact inscription criteria) was not retrieved. No dedicated modern study of Quanzhou's Syriac/Nestorian or Manichaean inscriptions (as opposed to the well-documented Islamic and Tamil/Hindu corpora) was found. The identification of Kaiyuan Temple as the specific repository of reused Hindu/Tamil carvings was not confirmed in a dedicated source. The comparative claim about Quanzhou vs. Alexandria pepper-import volumes ("the hundredfold" figure sometimes attributed to Marignolli or to Polo's own text depending on edition) has no located modern economic-historical treatment at all. The philological identification of Polo's toponym "Tyunju" with either Dehua or Cizao by name was not confirmed against Yule's own notes or against Vogel (2013).

**II-15.** Exact day-dates for several Ilkhanate succession transitions (notably Gaykhatu's death) vary unresolved across modern secondary sources. Whether "House of Ögedei vs. House of Tolui" is genuine specialist terminology for the Kaidu-Kublai war (as opposed to a looser popular framing) was not confirmed. **Most importantly: no source — de Rachewiltz, Biran, Jackson, or otherwise — was found anywhere in this research project that directly states a scholarly position on whether Polo's Book IV Kaidu/Ilkhanate war narratives are reliable reportage, garbled oral tradition, or literary set-pieces.** This is the single most important open question flagged across the entire project and should be pursued with direct access to Biran's monograph itself, de Rachewiltz's 1997 article in full, and Peter Jackson's 2017 book before the retelling asserts a scholarly consensus either way. No Chinese-language scholarship on 窩闊台汗國/海都 (the Ögedeid khanate/Kaidu) was located.

**II-F1.** None of the five recommended overview works (Franke & Twitchett 1994; Brook 2010; Rossabi's Khubilai Khan biography; Atwood 2004; Biran & Kim 2023) was read in full by any researcher — their recommendation rests on bibliographic/review-listing confirmation only. A second standalone Rossabi title and the exact year/content of Biran's *Along the Silk Roads in Mongol Eurasia* were not confirmed.
