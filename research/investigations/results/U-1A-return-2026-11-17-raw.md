I'll check each of the three claims against texts I can actually open (Yule–Cordier itself, the Yuan shi, Rashid al-Din in translation, and open scholarship), then give you one ledger per topic.

Two of the three notes need correcting, and the third needs its wording tightened. The Li Tan note is sound in substance but blurs the revolt date with the siege date. The Kaidu note rests on a statement in Yule that Pelliot calls wrong. The funeral note overstates Rashid al-Din, and nothing I could open supports the 20,000 figure.

- **OCR excerpt**: text of that same OCR returned by the search index; I did not open the full page.
- **Wikisource excerpt**: passages of the Chinese Wikisource Yuan shi transcription returned by the search index. Direct fetches of those pages failed, so I did not read any chapter in full.

## 1. Li Tan's revolt

**1a. Polo's year and commanders**
- Status: supported as a reading of Yule, with a variant.
- Source says: Yule's text gives 1273 and names the two barons Aguil and Mongotay. The Moule–Pelliot translation prints 1272, with a footnote on the date.
- Locator: Yule–Cordier 3rd ed., vol. II, p. 136 (Book II, ch. LXI); Moule–Pelliot, *Description of the World*, vol. I, scan page 311.
- Access: OCR excerpt for both.
- Limits: I did not see the Moule–Pelliot footnote, so I cannot say which manuscripts read which year. The retelling's "Note 6" is Yule's Note 5 to this chapter.
- Safe wording: "Polo's text dates the revolt to 1272 or 1273, depending on the manuscript."

**1b. Chinese annals date the revolt to 1262**
- Status: supported.
- Source says: The annals record under day jichou that Li Tan rebelled, handed Lian and Hai to the Song, killed the Mongol garrisons and marched on Yidu; under renzi that he occupied Jinan. Pelliot places the revolt in the 2nd moon of 1262 and says the manuscripts are wrong by ten years.
- Locator: Yuan shi, juan 5 (Shizu benji 2), Zhongtong 3; Pelliot, *Notes on Marco Polo* II, pp. 762–763 ("Liitan sangon").
- Access: Wikisource excerpt; OCR excerpt.
- Limits: the month heading for jichou was not in the excerpt I saw; "2nd moon" is Pelliot's statement.
- Safe wording: "The Yuan shi places the outbreak in the second month of the third Zhongtong year (early 1262), about a decade before Polo's date."

**1c. A siege at Jinan — and its date as distinct from the revolt's**
- Status: supported; the retelling should separate the dates.
- Source says: On guiyou Shi Shu and Aju were sent to Jinan, defeated Li Tan's force (4,000 heads) and he fell back into the city; on the first day of the 4th month the army built palisades and trenches around Jinan. The biography adds a ring wall begun on gengshen of the 5th month and the encirclement closed on jiaxu. On jiaxu (7th month) Li Tan entered Daming Lake, failed to drown, was taken and executed with the Mongol officer Nangjia, and his body was dismembered for display. Pelliot converts the execution to 6 August 1262.
- Locator: Yuan shi juan 5; juan 206 (Li Tan biography); Pelliot II, p. 763.
- Access: Wikisource excerpts (one via a mirror, quanxue.cn); OCR excerpt.
- Limits: there is also a field battle before the siege; Polo's single "battle" could be either.
- Safe wording: "The revolt began in early 1262; Jinan was invested from the fourth month and fell in the seventh, when Li Tan was executed (6 August 1262 by Pelliot's reckoning)."

**1d. "Different commanders"**
- Status: supported.
- Source says: Li Tan was brought bound to the tent of Prince Hebichi, and the minister Shi Tianze urged immediate execution. Juan 131 has officers following Prince Hebichi (and Kököchü-dai) against Li Tan at Jinan. Yule's note names Prince Apiché and General Ssetienché.
- Locator: Yuan shi juan 206; juan 131 (biographies of Nangjiadai, Wanzhedu, Huaidu); Yule II, p. 138, Note 5.
- Access: Wikisource excerpts; OCR excerpt.
- Safe wording: "Chinese sources name Prince Hebichi as nominal commander and Shi Tianze as director of the siege."

**1e. Can Aguil or Mongotay be identified?**
- Status: Aguil tentative; Mongotay not identified.
- Source says: Pelliot writes that Qubilai ordered A-shu to attack Jinan, with a cross-reference to his entry "Agiul". Aju's biography says that in Zhongtong 3 he followed the princes Baichu and Tiege against Li Tan. Yule instead took Agul to be a cousin of Kublai and father of Nayan. On Mongatai, Pelliot says the form must be Manghutai, a name borne by some forty men in the Yuan shi, and that Yule conflated two different men; he rejects Charignon's Nangiadai, since the Nangiadai of the annals was executed with Li Tan as a rebel, not sent against him.
- Locator: Pelliot II, pp. 763, 782–783; Yuan shi juan 128; Yule II, p. 138, Note 4.
- Access: OCR page (p. 782); OCR excerpts; Wikisource excerpt.
- Limits: Aju's presence in the campaign is explicitly attested; that Polo's "Aguil" is Aju is Pelliot's identification, and I did not open his "Agiul" entry (NOT SEEN). Pelliot's preferred Manghutai is on p. 783, which I did not open.
- Safe wording: "Aguil may be the general Aju, who did fight Li Tan; Mongotay (a common name, Manghutai) cannot be identified with confidence."

**1f. Pardon for lesser participants, execution of leaders**
- Status: supported in substance; not a single order.
- Source says: The annals record an edict on dinghai (4th month) releasing from guilt the soldiers and people of Boxing and Gaoyuan who had been coerced by Li Tan, and a later edict pardoning officials, soldiers and people of Yidu who had been coerced. Executions recorded are Wang Wentong (as co-conspirator), two Taiyuan officials who circulated Li Tan's proclamation, and Li Tan with Nangjia. Polo has the Kaan order chiefs put to a cruel death and those of lower rank pardoned.
- Locator: Yuan shi juan 5; Yule II, p. 136.
- Access: Wikisource excerpt; OCR excerpt.
- Limits: the pardons cover the "coerced", and one precedes the fall of Jinan. The month of the Yidu pardon (10th) comes from the Xin Yuan shi, a twentieth-century compilation, not from the Yuan shi excerpt.
- Safe wording: "The Yuan shi records the execution of Li Tan and named associates and separate edicts pardoning those coerced into the revolt, which broadly matches Polo."

## 2. Kaidu and Nomoghan

**2a. Polo's sequence**
- Status: supported; the two battles are distinct.
- Source says: The first battle is dated 1266, with Caidu and Yesudar attacking two of the Kaan's barons, named Chibai and Chiban. Caidu then stays at peace for two years, after which he marches on Caracoron, where Nomogan and George await him with over 60,000 horse. The fight lasts till vespers without victory on either side, and Caidu rides home to Great Turkey and Samarcand.
- Locator: Yule II, pp. 459–462 (Book IV, ch. II).
- Access: OCR page (p. 460); OCR excerpts (pp. 459, 462). The battle's outcome is from a web transcription of Yule, not the page scan.
- Limits: the text itself gives no year for the second battle; by its own count it falls about 1268. The 1266 date belongs only to the first.
- Safe wording: "Polo dates only the first battle (1266); the second follows two years later in his telling and is undated."

**2b. The Chinese record of Nomoghan's command and capture**
- Status: supported as an event; dates need adjusting.
- Source says: Pelliot has Nomoghan sent to Almaliq in 1271, and Antong sent to assist him. The annals, as quoted in the Qing critical work Yuan shi benzheng, record that in the 7th month of Zhiyuan 14 Prince Shiregi seized the Prince of Beiping at Almaliq, put Antong in fetters and drew other princes into revolt. Bayan's biography says the same of year 14 and has Bayan defeat Shiregi's force on the Orkhon. Pelliot adds that, per Rashid, Nomoghan and Kököchü were sent to the Golden Horde and Antong to Qaidu. Pelliot gives the release as 1283.
- Locator: Yuan shi, Shizu benji, Zhiyuan 14/7 (quoted in Yuan shi benzheng juan 35); Yuan shi juan 127; Pelliot II, pp. 795–796; I, p. 126.
- Access: excerpts only; I did not open the benji chapter itself.
- Limits: Pelliot elsewhere dates the capture "1276–1277"; the conspiracy began late 1276. The Pelliot OCR cites "YS 13" for 1271, which looks wrong for that year; unverified.
- Safe wording: "Nomoghan held the north-western command from about 1271, was joined by Antong in 1275, and was seized by mutinous princes at Almaliq in 1276–77."

**2c. Does that event match Polo's second battle?**
- Status: contradicted as a match; the battle is unresolved.
- Source says: Yule's note says that in 1277 Kaidu and Shireghi engaged Nomoghan and Antong near Almalik and took both prisoner. Pelliot states that Qaidu was no party to the plot and that Yule II, 462 is wrong. On George, Pelliot says no text connects him with Nomoghan; he was killed in 1298 fighting Qaidu and Dua, after Polo had left, though his father Ai-buqa fought Sarban, one of Nomoghan's captors. Pelliot also notes that Nomoghan later stood on the Tamir river in the Orkhon basin, where he had to deal with Qaidu.
- Locator: Yule II, p. 462, Note 3; Pelliot I, p. 128; II, "Giorge" (scan page 141) and p. 796.
- Access: OCR excerpts.
- Limits: the Shiregi affair was a seizure by Nomoghan's own companions at Almaliq, ending in his capture. Polo describes a drawn pitched battle with Kaidu near Karakorum. Place, opponent, participants and outcome all differ. A later, unrecorded clash on the Orkhon is possible but is my inference, not a source's claim.
- Safe wording: "No recorded battle matches Polo's second engagement; Nomoghan was captured in 1276–77 by Shiregi's mutiny, in which Kaidu took no part, and George is not attested alongside him."

## 3. Möngke's funeral

**3a. Polo's claim**
- Status: supported as Polo's text only.
- Source says: Polo states as certain truth that when Mangu died more than 20,000 persons who met the body were slain.
- Locator: Yule I, pp. 246–247 (Book I, ch. LI), quoted by Howorth in *Indian Antiquary* 16.
- Access: transcription excerpt (Howorth); Yule's own page for the text not opened.
- Safe wording: "Polo reports that more than 20,000 people were killed."

**3b. "Rashid al-Din confirms escort killings and horse sacrifice for Chinggis Khan"**
- Status: partly supported, partly misattributed.
- Source says: Yule's note says Rashid relates that the escort carrying Chinghiz to burial slew all whom they met, and that forty noble girls and superb horses were despatched to serve him; he cites Erdmann and D'Ohsson, and adds that carrying out the rule for Mangku would have meant great slaughter. A blog quoting Thackston's translation gives Rashid's sentence as the bearers slaying every creature they met until they reached the ordus. Howorth, following Rashid's account of Ögedei, places the forty girls and the horses after Ögedei's inauguration, not at the funeral procession.
- Locator: Yule I, ch. LI, Note 4 (Gutenberg transcription via bookrags); Rashid/Thackston (page NOT SEEN); *Indian Antiquary* 16.
- Access: transcription excerpts; another author's report for Thackston.
- Limits: Rashid wrote some eighty years after 1227 and narrates this as part of the secret return of the coffin. It concerns Chinggis only and is not stated as a standing custom. The blog author calls it legendary; that is opinion. Yule's last sentence is his inference, not evidence.
- Safe wording: "Rashid al-Din, writing decades later, says the bearers of Chinggis Khan's coffin killed all they met; the girls and horses he mentions were offered later, under Ögedei."

**3c. What Rashid says of Möngke's funeral**
- Status: Polo's figure is not supported.
- Source says: In Pelliot's translation, Möngke's son Asutai brought the coffin to the ordos; mourning was held on four successive days in the ordos of four wives, the coffin placed on a table each day; it was then carried to Burqan-qaldun, the Yeke-qoruq, and buried beside Chinggis and Tolui.
- Locator: Pelliot I, p. 338 ("Cinghis"), citing Blochet II, 335–336.
- Access: OCR excerpt.
- Limits: the excerpt has an ellipsis; I cannot exclude omitted words. Silence is not denial.
- Safe wording: "Rashid al-Din's account of Möngke's funeral, as translated by Pelliot, mentions mourning rites and burial but no killings."

**3d. Independent support for 20,000, or for a universal custom?**
- Status: unresolved; none found.
- Source says: Pelliot quotes Jourdain de Sévérac's relay-of-bearers tale and calls it a baseless story that nonetheless attests the secrecy of the tombs; he notes that Mandeville's funeral passage is copied from Plano Carpini and did not originally concern the Great Khans.
- Locator: Pelliot I, p. 334.
- Access: OCR excerpt.
- Limits: later European writers repeating the 20,000 may depend on Polo and should not be counted as independent. I did not open Carpini, Rubruck, Juvaini or the Yuan shi annals of Möngke for this (NOT SEEN). A failed search is not proof of absence.
- Safe wording: "The figure of 20,000 rests on Polo alone, and no accessible source shows that every royal funeral involved such killings."

## Concrete corrections

1. Give Polo's year as "1272 or 1273" (Moule–Pelliot versus Yule).
2. Date the revolt to early 1262 and the fall of Jinan to the seventh month (6 August 1262); do not let "1262" stand for the siege alone.
3. If the retelling follows Yule's Note 4 on "Agul" as Nayan's father, replace it with "possibly Aju"; leave Mongotay unidentified.
4. Say "edicts pardoned those coerced", not a single pardon of lower ranks.
5. Remove Kaidu from the capture of Nomoghan; Yule's Note 3 is the source of that error.
6. Change "command and capture around 1275–77" to command from about 1271, Antong added 1275, capture 1276–77.
7. Do not present the 1276–77 event as a better fit for the second battle; say no match is known.
8. Restrict Rashid to Chinggis's coffin escort; move the horses and girls to Ögedei's reign; drop "confirms".
