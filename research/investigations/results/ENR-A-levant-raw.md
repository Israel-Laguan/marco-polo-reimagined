## Political Status of Acre and the War of St Sabas

The political landscape of Acre during the late 13th century was profoundly shaped by the intense rivalry between the maritime republics of Venice and Genoa [[46]]. The War of St Sabas (1256–1270), initially framed as a dispute over a monastery, evolved into a brutal commercial war for control of the lucrative port of Acre, the capital of the Kingdom of Jerusalem [[48,50]]. The outcome of this conflict had lasting consequences for the city's urban fabric and its international relations. The available evidence confirms a permanent division within Acre following the war, but the timeline for subsequent events, such as the restoration of the Genoese quarter, remains less clear. The alliances of the Crusader military orders and the actions of key Venetian commanders like Lorenzo Tiepolo were decisive factors in shaping the conflict's outcome. Scholarly work, particularly by David Jacoby, provides a detailed topographical and economic context for these events, moving beyond simple chronicle narratives to a more nuanced understanding of the period [[47,54]].

The central claim that Acre was divided after the War of St Sabas is strongly confirmed by archaeological evidence [[50]]. Excavations in Crusader-period Acre have revealed a fortification system constructed on the northern border of the Genoese Quarter [[46]]. This defensive structure included a wall built of bossed ashlars with arrow slits, a fortified gate leading into the quarter through a vaulted passageway, and a projecting tower [[50]]. Crucially, both the gate and parts of the passageway within this new fortification were blocked off during the Crusader period [[50]]. This physical barrier represents the first concrete material evidence of the conflict's aftermath, directly substantiating historical accounts that the Genoese were expelled from Acre and their quarter walled off, effectively creating a partitioned city [[46,50]]. This tangible outcome demonstrates how the commercial rivalry between Venice and Genoa had direct and destructive consequences for the urban layout and internal cohesion of the Latin East's most important port city [[46]]. The conflict originated not just from the property dispute over the monastery of St Sabas but from the deeper struggle for control over Acre's harbor and the vast trade networks of the eastern Mediterranean [[48,50]].

However, the specific claim that the Genoese quarter was restored only in 1288 cannot be verified or refuted based on the provided sources; this information is not present in the documents analyzed [[52]]. While the division is confirmed to have been a consequence of the war, the timeline for its resolution is not addressed. This remains an unresolved question within the scope of the available research materials. The war concluded with the Peace of Cremona in 1270, brokered by Louis IX of France and Pope Clement IV, which established a truce between the two republics [[36,37]]. Yet, this treaty did not erase the physical and political scars left in Acre itself. The expulsion of the Genoese and the subsequent blocking of their quarter indicate that the underlying tensions persisted long after the formal end of hostilities [[49,50]]. The fall of Acre to the Mamluks in 1291 ultimately destroyed the city, leaving behind a largely empty town with damaged walls, but the partitioned nature of the city during its final decades is a historically significant fact derived from both textual and archaeological sources [[46,47]].

The alignment of the military orders with the Italian maritime powers is a well-documented aspect of the conflict. The sources consistently confirm that the Templars and the Teutonic Knights supported Venice, while the Hospitallers backed Genoa [[49]]. This pattern of alliance reflects the complex web of loyalties and interests within the Crusader states, where military power often intersected with commercial rivalries. The Venetians, allied with the Templars and Teutonic Knights, decisively defeated the Genoese fleet in a pivotal naval engagement in June 1258 [[49]]. The Genoese commander, Rosso della Turca, requested land-side assistance from Philip of Montfort and the Knights Hospitaller to launch a combined attack against the Venetians blockading their ships in the harbor [[49]]. However, this attempt failed when the Venetian fleet, led by Lorenzo Tiepolo, broke the harbor chain and engaged the Genoese fleet in open combat, resulting in a crushing victory for Venice [[36,49]]. Half of the Genoese fleet was captured, and an estimated 1,700 men were killed or captured, leading to the immediate expulsion of the Genoese from Acre [[48,49]]. This event solidified Venetian dominance in the city and cemented the existing patterns of allegiance among the military orders.

Regarding the role of Pisa, the lead's claim that Pisa changed sides to support Venice in 1257 is incorrect. The sources clarify that in June 1257, it was the Venetian war fleet under Lorenzo Tiepolo that entered Acre's harbor and seized or destroyed all Genoese ships present [[36]]. Pisa's shift in allegiance occurred much later, during the second major phase of the Venetian-Genoese wars (1291–1299), prompted by the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf Khalīl capturing Acre in 1291 [[36]]. In response to Genoa's successful capture of the city, Venice formed an alliance with Pisa to challenge Genoese dominance [[36]]. Therefore, Pisa's support for Venice was a reaction to a later event, not a development in the initial stages of the War of St Sabas. The early conflict was primarily a direct confrontation between Venice and Genoa, with their respective allies among the military orders.

The actions of Lorenzo Tiepolo are confirmed with some precision. He commanded the Venetian fleet that broke the harbor chain and defeated the Genoese admiral Rosso della Turca in June 1258 [[36,49]]. The sources specify the month as June 1258, which aligns with the lead's statement but highlights the need for chronological accuracy [[49]]. His victory was decisive, securing Venetian control over the port and forcing the Genoese out of Acre [[48,49]]. This action was part of a broader Venetian strategy to protect its merchant convoys, which had led to the implementation of a strict convoy system (*carovana*) limiting trade to the Levant [[36]]. The entire sequence of events, from the initial dispute to the naval battles and the final expulsion of the Genoese, underscores the violent nature of commercial competition in the medieval Mediterranean. The work of scholars like David Jacoby has been instrumental in providing a detailed framework for understanding this period, focusing on the economic life and topography of Crusader Acre [[47,51]].

## Venetian-Genoese Naval Conflicts and Marco Polo's Capture

The protracted conflicts between Venice and Genoa were defined by a series of dramatic naval engagements that shaped Mediterranean trade routes and political alliances for centuries [[35]]. It is described as a crushing Venetian victory, showcasing the superior naval capabilities of the Venetian fleet during this initial phase of the conflict [[35,37]]. Similarly, the Battle of Trapani in Sicily in 1266 is also confirmed as another significant Venetian victory [[37]]. These victories were part of a larger pattern where Venice, characterized by Frederic C. Lane as having overwhelming superiority in fleet actions, repeatedly bested its rival [[35]]. Following a truce signed in 1268, a period of relative calm ensued until hostilities resumed in the second phase of the war [[36]]. The claim of a battle at Acre known as the "Tower of Flies" in 1267 is unsubstantiated and appears to be a fabrication or misinterpretation. The core of Lead E32 concerns Marco Polo's capture. This story forms the basis of the book's prologue, which mentions Marco deciding to recount his travels while imprisoned with Rustichello in 1298 [[31]]. The Battle of Curzola was indeed a catastrophic disaster for Venice [[39]]. Fought on September 7, 9, or 10, 1298, in the channel between the island of Curzola (Korčula) and the Dalmatian mainland, the Genoese fleet, commanded by Lamba Doria, decisively defeated the Venetians led by Admiral Andrea Dandolo [[29,39,79]]. The Venetian losses were immense: approximately 83 of their 95 galleys were destroyed, and between 7,000 and 9,000 men were killed [[29,39]]. Dandolo was either killed or committed suicide upon his capture [[36,77]]. It is within this context of massive Venetian defeat and widespread captivity that Marco Polo's alleged capture fits logically.

While the Ramusio tradition is dominant, the provided sources introduce a degree of historiographical nuance. It is noted as "unclear" whether Marco Polo was actually captured at the Battle of Curzola or at a previous minor engagement near Laiazzo (Ayas) in 1294 [[77,78]]. The Battle of Laiazzo was the opening engagement of the second phase of the Venetian-Genoese war and resulted in the destruction of a Venetian fleet [[37]]. This competing theory suggests that Marco could have been captured in this earlier, smaller-scale conflict rather than the more famous Battle of Curzola [[77,78]]. However, the consensus among scholars and the weight of tradition still favor the Battle of Curzola as the site of his capture [[80]]. The sources do not name any specific modern historians who explicitly reject the Curzola capture story, indicating that the debate is more about minor details than the core premise itself [[77]]. The lack of contemporary documentation means the story rests almost entirely on Ramusio's 16th-century account, which itself may have drawn upon earlier oral traditions [[80]].

In summary, the chain of major naval battles is confirmed for the periods around 1263 and 1266, but the "Tower of Flies" engagement is not found. ## Art-Historical Origins of St Mark's Basilica Spoils

The decorative elements adorning St Mark's Basilica in Venice, particularly the Porphyry Tetrarchs and the Pilastri Acritani, have long been celebrated as spoils of war, trophies looted from the enemy [[28]]. For centuries, Venetian tradition held that these magnificent artifacts were brought back from the Holy Land, specifically plundered from the Genoese stronghold of St Sabas at Acre after a Venetian victory in 1258 [[24,28]]. However, modern scholarship, driven by meticulous archaeological work and art-historical analysis, has definitively overturned this cherished narrative. The evidence overwhelmingly confirms that both sets of spoils originated not in the Crusader States but in the heart of the Byzantine Empire, Constantinople. The Porphyry Tetrarchs were looted during the Fourth Crusade in 1204, while the Pilastri Acritani come from the church of Hagios Polyeuktos, also taken during that same sack [[28,62]]. This revisionist view is now the scholarly consensus, replacing the older Acre-based theory with a more accurate and ancient history for these iconic objects [[28,66]].

The traditional attribution of the Porphyry Tetrarchs to Acre is explicitly corrected by modern scholarship [[24]]. The popular theory linking them to Acre gained traction from 14th-century chronicles referring to stonework being looted from the city after the 1258 Venetian victory [[24]]. However, this narrative is now understood to be a powerful piece of Venetian propaganda, celebrating their rivalry with Genoa [[28]]. The definitive refutation of this theory began with Marilyn Perry's influential 1977 article, "Saint Mark's Trophies," and has since been solidified by numerous studies [[62,64]]. The current scholarly consensus, supported by figures like Otto Demus and Arne Effenberger, is that the Tetrarchs were plundered by the Venetians from Constantinople during the Sack of 1204 [[53,60,62]]. They were originally imperial monuments, carved from precious red porphyry reserved for Roman emperors, dating to the early 4th century AD [[24]]. Their original context was likely a public square in Constantinople, possibly the Philadelphion, a ceremonial center associated with the imperial cult [[23,24]].

Further evidence supporting their Constantinopolitan origin comes from a significant archaeological discovery in Istanbul in the 1960s [[24]]. During excavations, a fragment of a porphyry heel and base was recovered [[24]]. This piece was later confirmed to be the missing foot of the fourth figure in the group [[53]]. When placed with the sculptures in Venice, the fragment fit perfectly, proving that all four figures were carved as a single unit and were always meant to be seen together [[24]]. This discovery provides conclusive proof that the Tetrarchs were not dismembered spoils from a distant crusade but were integral components of a pre-existing monument in Constantinople [[24]]. The 2023 article in *Pyrenae* by Arne Effenberger further argues for their placement at the Philadelphion, reinforcing the Byzantine connection [[60]]. The suggestion that their mutilation was caused by Venetian vandalism is downplayed in favor of alterations made during their recontextualization in Byzantium itself [[23]].

Similarly, the origin of the Pilastri Acritani, the beautiful carved piers in front of the south wall of St Mark's, has been conclusively revised [[66]]. For a long time, they too were believed to be spoils from Acre, a belief notably held by Frederic C. Lane in his classic work *Venice: A Maritime Republic* [[28]]. However, modern scholarship has established that they originate from the church of Hagios Polyeuktos in Constantinople [[28,64]]. This conclusion was reached following excavations of the church site in Istanbul in the 1960s, which unearthed fragments of decorative stonework [[28]]. Matching intricate ornamentation, particularly grape motifs, was found on both the stones inside St Mark's and the architectural fragments excavated from Hagios Polyeuktos, providing direct and undeniable evidence of their shared origin [[28]]. This finding replaced the traditional Acre narrative with a more accurate account tied to the wealth and artistic legacy of the Byzantine capital. The shift in scholarly opinion highlights the importance of archaeological evidence in challenging long-held historical traditions, even those as deeply ingrained as the spoils of Acre [[28]].

The body of evidence concerning the origins of these two famous sets of spoils is robust and consistent. For the Porphyry Tetrarchs, the verdict is that they were plundered from Constantinople in 1204, not Acre in 1258 [[53,62]]. Their original context was a high-status imperial monument, likely the Philadelphion [[24,60]]. The discovery of the matching foot fragment in Istanbul serves as definitive proof of their unified creation and Byzantine provenance [[24,53]]. For the Pilastri Acritani, the verdict is that they came from Hagios Polyeuktos in Constantinople, a fact established by the excavation of the church and the matching of decorative motifs [[28,66]]. Both cases serve as excellent examples of how material culture can rewrite textual traditions and provide a more accurate picture of Venice's relationship with the Eastern Mediterranean, rooted in the cataclysm of the Fourth Crusade rather than the commercial rivalries of the 13th century.

## Historiography of Sultan Baybars's Death in 1277

The death of Sultan Baybars in July 1277 has been the subject of considerable historiographical debate, reflecting the political instability of the Mamluk succession crisis [[27,83]]. The provided sources outline three primary competing narratives: death from illness, accidental consumption of poisoned kumis, and deliberate assassination orchestrated by a successor, Qalāwūn [[68,69,73]]. While the poisoning story is the most detailed and widely cited, the possibility of murder introduces a layer of intrigue that challenges the simpler accounts. The positions of modern historians, including Thorau, Holt, Amitai, and especially Hans-Ulrich Kühn, demonstrate the ongoing scholarly effort to interpret the available Arabic primary sources critically [[25,45,73]]. Presenting these conflicting interpretations provides a nuanced understanding of both Baybars's final days and the methods of medieval historical inquiry.

The most popular and detailed account attributes Baybars's death to the accidental consumption of poisoned kumis (fermented mare's milk) [[68,83]]. According to multiple 14th-century Arabic chroniclers, including Ibn Kathīr, al-Maqrīzī, and al-Yūnīnī, a guest at a banquet hosted by the Sultan was intended to be poisoned [[68,69]]. In one version, Baybars, perhaps confused by identical drinking vessels or misled by an attendant, accidentally drank from the poisoned cup instead [[83]]. His condition reportedly deteriorated rapidly, and he died in Damascus on June 30th or July 1st, 1277 [[27,69]]. This narrative, emphasizing a tragic accident, became the standard account and is frequently repeated in secondary sources [[27,68]]. Peter Thorau's critical biography, *The Lion of Egypt*, translated by P. M. Holt, is a key modern source engaging with these events [[25,26]]. Holt's translation of Thorau's work, along with Thorau's own text, provides a sophisticated analysis of Baybars's character and reign, and would necessarily involve a close reading of these primary sources [[25,82]].

A competing, and more sensational, theory posits that Baybars was deliberately murdered. This interpretation is most forcefully advanced by Hans-Ulrich Kühn in his chapter "Qalāwūn als Mörder von Sultan Baibars? – Eine These" ("Qalāwūn as the Murderer of Sultan Baybars? – A Thesis"), published in his 2019 monograph *Sultan Baibars und seine Söhne* [[73]]. Kühn's thesis suggests that Qalāwūn, who succeeded Baybars as sultan, may have orchestrated the poisoning to eliminate a rival and secure his own accession [[73]]. This hypothesis transforms the incident from a tragic accident into a calculated political assassination, placing it within the context of the factional struggles that immediately followed Baybars's death [[83]]. While Kühn's work is identified as a source, it is flagged as paywalled or print-only, making it inaccessible through open-access channels [[73]]. However, its inclusion in the list of preferred sources indicates its relevance to the historiographical debate [[73]]. The existence of this theory shows that scholars continue to question the straightforward narratives provided by medieval chroniclers, searching for political motives behind seemingly accidental events. Other historians, such as Reuven Amitai-Preiss, whose expertise lies in Mongol-Mamluk relations, may not focus on the specifics of Baybars's death but their broader works provide essential context for understanding the pressures facing the Sultan in his final years [[43,51]].

A third, less detailed possibility mentioned in the sources is that Baybars died from an illness or a wound sustained during his last campaign against the Mongols [[27,69]]. In April 1277, shortly before his death, Baybars invaded the Seljuq Sultanate of Rûm and defeated a Mongol force at the Battle of Elbistan [[69,70]]. While victorious, the campaign was arduous, and it is conceivable that he suffered injuries or fell ill as a result [[27]]. However, none of the provided sources attribute a specific primary source or modern historian to an "illness-only" theory. The poisoning narrative is presented as the main alternative to the illness theory, suggesting that the illness account lacks strong contemporary support or detailed elaboration in the surviving chronicles [[27]]. The ambiguity surrounding his death is itself a significant historical point; the very multiplicity of explanations—accident, murder, illness—reflects the turbulent political climate of the Mamluk court and the difficulty of establishing a single, definitive truth from biased or incomplete sources [[83]].

In synthesizing these claims, it is clear that while the poisoning narrative is the most prominent, it is not the only one. The historiographical landscape presents at least two main competing views: the popular story of the mistaken cup of poison and the more speculative theory of assassination by Qalāwūn. The positions of the specified scholars can be mapped as follows: Thorau and Holt are associated with a critical examination of Baybars's life, which would include analyzing these sources [[25,26]]; Amitai provides crucial contextual knowledge of the era [[43]]; and Kühn explicitly proposes the assassination theory [[73]]. This allows for a presentation of both sides of the debate, fulfilling the user's request for a nuanced view of the historiography.

## Mongol Succession Wars: Hulagu's Defeat at the Terek

The winter of 1262–63 marked a turning point in the history of the Mongol Empire, culminating in a decisive defeat for Hulagu Khan, ruler of the Ilkhanate in Persia, at the hands of his cousin Berke, the Muslim Khan of the Golden Horde [[11,13]]. This clash, a major episode in the Toluid Civil War, was not merely a dynastic squabble but a conflict with profound geopolitical consequences, reshaping the balance of power in the Middle East and Central Asia [[11]]. The provided sources confirm that Hulagu's army was severely defeated by forces loyal to Berke on the banks of the frozen Terek River in January 1263 [[11,84]]. The retreating Ilkhanate troops were slaughtered by pursuing Mongol horsemen as they fled across the ice, which cracked under their weight, causing many to drown [[11,13]]. This military catastrophe forced Hulagu to abandon his plans for a full-scale invasion of Egypt and instead divert his attention to defending his own territories from Berke's attacks, effectively halting the westward expansion of the Mongol Empire [[11,42]].

The leadership of the Golden Horde forces in this critical engagement was entrusted to Nogai, who is identified as Berke's nephew [[11,84]]. This corrects a vague characterization in some summaries, providing a specific familial relationship. Nogai led a vanguard of 30,000 men dispatched by Berke in early 1262, marking the beginning of the open conflict between the two cousins [[11]]. The reasons for this civil war were multifaceted. The primary cause was religious: Berke, who had converted to Islam, was enraged by the destruction of Baghdad and its massacre of Muslims in 1258, which he viewed as an assault on the Islamic world [[11]]. A secondary cause was economic, related to trade disputes and the hoarding of wealth in North Iran [[11]]. The conflict escalated rapidly, with Berke's forces defeating Hulagu's vanguard near Shamakhi in mid-October 1262 [[11]]. Hulagu retaliated by crossing the Terek River and taking the strategic city of Derbent in December 1262, but this success was short-lived [[11]].

The climactic battle took place on January 13, 1263, on the banks of the Terek River [[11]]. After a fierce fight, Berke's reinforcements overwhelmed Hulagu's army. As the remnants of the Ilkhanate force attempted to retreat across the frozen river, the pursuing Golden Horde soldiers drove them onto the ice, which then broke apart [[11]]. Many thousands of Hulagu's soldiers drowned in the icy waters before Berke's forces withdrew to Derbent [[11,13]]. This devastating defeat crippled Hulagu's military strength and forced him into a defensive posture for the remainder of the conflict's first phase [[10]]. Marco Polo's account of the war contains a numerical discrepancy, claiming Hulagu commanded 300,000 men against an army of 350,000 led by Berke, a figure questioned by modern scholars like Thomas Allsen due to its implausibility [[10]]. Nonetheless, the historicity of the defeat itself is not in doubt. The war continued intermittently for years, but the Battle of the Terek was the defining moment of the initial phase, demonstrating the limits of Mongol military power and paving the way for a strategic alliance between the Mamluks of Egypt and the Golden Horde against the Ilkhanate [[10,11]].

## The Genoese Prison as a Literary Milieu and the Rialfri Repertory

The collaboration between Marco Polo and Rustichello da Pisa is traditionally framed as a simple act of dictation in a Genoese prison [[31]]. However, a deeper analysis reveals a more complex socio-cultural context. The prison itself, particularly the detention camp for Pisan prisoners in Genoa after the Battle of Meloria in 1284, functioned as a vibrant literary milieu [[32,45]]. This environment, characterized by the intensive copying and production of manuscripts, provides a compelling backdrop for the creation of the *Devisement dou monde* [[32]]. Alvise Andreose's 2015 research explicitly links the linguistic and literary characteristics of Rustichello's work to this specific milieu, arguing that the Franco-Italian language of the original text reflects the speech and writing habits of the Pisan prisoners [[6,32]]. Furthermore, the Rialfri repertory offers a digital gateway to the rich literary output of this period, documenting the extensive manuscript tradition of Rustichello's Arthurian compilations and providing resources for studying the unique language of the *Devisement*'s F-text [[2,3]].

The phenomenon of manuscript production in the Genoese prison is confirmed by multiple sources [[32,45]]. After their decisive naval victory at Meloria in 1284, the Genoese held thousands of Pisan captives, including skilled artisans and scribes [[45]]. Rather than being passive inmates, these prisoners formed a productive community, copying and composing texts in the local Franco-Italian dialect [[32]]. This activity created a distinct literary environment, separate from the courts and scriptoria of the Italian peninsula. Alvise Andreose's article, "La scrittura in carcere a Genova dopo Meloria e la 'Compilatio arthuriana' di Rustichello da Pisa," makes the direct and significant claim that this prison milieu influenced the creation of Rustichello's works [[32]]. Andreose's work connects the dots between the Pisan prisoners' activities and the authorship of the *Devisement du Monde*, suggesting that the book's unique linguistic features are a product of this specific historical moment [[32]]. This perspective enriches the traditional narrative by situating the collaboration between Polo and Rustichello within a wider intellectual and cultural movement occurring in a wartime prison camp.

Andreose's article, published in the journal *Francigena 1* in 2015, is accessible via the journal's website, which is part of the RIALFrI project [[6,32]]. The full title of the article is "La scrittura in carcere a Genova dopo Meloria e la 'Compilatio arthuriana' di Rustichello da Pisa" [[32]]. This work is a key piece of scholarship for understanding the social context of the *Devisement*'s production. Other scholars mentioned in the sources who contribute to the study of this period include Fabrizio Cigni, who has worked on the manuscripts produced by the Pisan prisoners, and Marta Materni, whose work on an unpublished version of a manuscript related to the Arthurian romance 'Guiron le Courtois' is published online by RIALFrI [[4,6]]. The collective work of these scholars helps to reconstruct the literary culture of the Genoese prison, highlighting it as a center of production rather than mere incarceration [[32]]. The idea that the *Devisement*'s unique blend of factual narration and chivalric romance might reflect the literary tastes and linguistic environment of these prisoners is a compelling and well-supported hypothesis [[18,32]].

The Rialfri repertory, formally known as the Repertorio Informatizzato Antica Letteratura Franco-Italiana, is an invaluable tool for researchers of this period [[1]]. Directed by Francesca Gambino at the University of Padua, its homepage is `https://www.rpia.unipd.it/` [[1,3]]. Rialfri provides comprehensive data on the literary works of the Franco-Italian tradition, including those of Rustichello da Pisa [[3]]. It documents his "Compilation arthurienne," a significant prose romance collection based on French originals like *Guiron le courtois* and *Roman de Meliadus* [[4,21]]. The repertory catalogs the numerous manuscripts containing his work, providing scholars with a detailed overview of its transmission and reception [[3,5]]. For the *Devisement dou monde*, Rialfri identifies the language of the sole complete surviving manuscript (Version F) as "franco-italiano" and provides access to a digital edition of a fragment of the text [[2,7]]. This confirms the linguistic hybridity of the original work, which blends Marco Polo's Venetian background with Rustichello's use of French literary conventions [[18]]. Rialfri thus serves as both a repository of information about Rustichello's literary career and a practical resource for analyzing the linguistic features of the *Devisement*'s original text, directly supporting the claims made by Andreose and others about its unique origins [[2,32]].

In conclusion, the Genoese prison was not merely the setting for Marco Polo's imprisonment but a dynamic center of literary production [[32]]. The work of Alvise Andreose convincingly ties this milieu to the creation of the *Devisement dou monde*, suggesting its distinctive character was shaped by the linguistic and cultural environment of the Pisan prisoners [[32]]. The Rialfri repertory stands as a testament to the richness of this literary tradition, offering detailed information on Rustichello's Arthurian compilations and providing critical resources for the study of the Franco-Italian language of the *Devisement*'s F-text [[2,3]]. Together, these findings provide a much richer and more nuanced understanding of the circumstances under which Marco Polo's famous book was written.
