# Five source checks resolved for Volume III annotations

Across five independent verification passes, three claims hold up with precise, re-locatable sourcing, one rests on a genuine three-way scholarly dispute with no consensus, and several supposedly settled attributions turn out to be secondhand or simply absent from the cited text. Pelliot's **FERLEC**, **LAMBRI**, and **PENTAN** entries in *Notes on Marco Polo* are confirmed at their stated printed pages once the Tōyō Bunko scan numbers are converted with a verified **scan+596=page** offset, but **SEILAN has no standalone headword at all** — likely one of the entries Pelliot never finished before his death. The Pasai gravestone question is not a case of "replacement vs. original" with a clear winner: Lambourn's later dating (c. 1480s–1520s) and Ismail, Ramli & Ahmad Zakaria's 2022 rebuttal (contemporary with 1297) are both in print, and Guillot & Kalus's own verdict on this specific stone could not be recovered. The oft-repeated claim that **Taddesse Tamrat identifies Polo's Abash king as Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon is false** — his only Marco Polo citation attaches to Yǝkunno Amlak, not his son — while the Trimingham/Pankhurst attribution survives only as an unverified Wikipedia-sourced claim. The Takashima excavation record supports a predominantly Chinese-built hull assemblage dated by artifact association (a 1277 CE bronze seal) to the 1281 campaign, but shows no itemized storm-damage signature — the one specific damage pattern found is burning, not typhoon — and the 2019 *Current Science* paper remains unread. Orlandini's document 6 (Maffeo Polo's will) names "the lord Comnenus of Trebizond" as responsible for the loss but never specifies a mechanism, so "robbery" remains an unsupported characterization. Every finding below is pinned to its exact locator, access level, and limitation, and no verdict from the underlying research notes has been softened.

## 1. Pelliot's Notes on Marco Polo: three entries confirmed, Seilan not found

The Tōyō Bunko scan identifiers given in the task brief are retrieval leads, not printed page numbers, and the research established a reliable conversion: within the Vol. II scan range checked (roughly scans 19–206, i.e., printed pp. 615–803), **printed page = scan number + 596**, confirmed independently at three points (scan 129→p.725, scan 165→p.761, scan 206→p.802) against the Vol. III index's own "See p. nnn" self-references. On that basis, **FERLEC is supported at Vol. II, p. 725 (entry 231)**: Pelliot identifies Polo's "Ferlec" with Perlak on Sumatra's north coast, treating the form as an Arabized rendering of the Malay name and comparing Yuan-period Chinese transcriptions (Pelliot, *Notes on Marco Polo*, Paris: Imprimerie Nationale, 1963, accessed via NII Digital Silk Road, [dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/V-2/page-hr/0129.html.en](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/V-2/page-hr/0129.html.en), access level: OCR text of the scan, not the raw page image). **LAMBRI is supported at Vol. II, pp. 761–762 (entry 270)**: Pelliot places the kingdom at Sumatra's north-western tip near later Acheh/Achin, possibly the village of Lamreh, disputes Rockhill's reading of a Chinese term as "Achin" as chronologically too early, and traces the name through Javanese, Tamil, Arabic and Chinese sources from the 11th–16th centuries ([V-2/page-hr/0165](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/V-2/page-hr/0165.html.en), same access level). **PENTAN is supported at Vol. II, pp. 802–803 (entry 313)**: Pelliot prefers "Pentan" over Yule's "Pentam," identifies the island as Bintan in the Riau Archipelago, reconstructs Polo's probable sea route north of Bintan through the Straits of Singapore, and disputes Yule, Gerini and Cordier at length on the history of the "Governor's Straits" toponym ([V-2/page-hr/0206](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/V-2/page-hr/0206.html.en)).

**SEILAN is not found**, and the evidence points beyond mere absence-from-search toward Pelliot never having written the entry. An exhaustive OCR search of the Vol. III alphabetical index — the full window from "Sdam" through "Sezaire" (where "Seilan" would sort between "Seide" and "Seilani"), plus the parallel "Ceylan/Ceylon" window in the C-section — turned up no self-standing headword, only scattered cross-references: "Seilani" (adjective, discussed under BRAZIL, p. 104), "Seyllan" (pointing to DARKNESS (PROVINCE OF), p. 622), and two bare Ceylon mentions under BETTALA (p. 95) and DAGROIAN (p. 615). Following the strongest lead, the DARKNESS (PROVINCE OF) entry (Vol. II, pp. 615–636) contains only a philological aside in which Pelliot argues that a particular toponym "Laṅkā" is *not* Ceylon, favoring the Nicobar Islands instead — the opposite of a dedicated Seilan article. This is consistent with editor Louis Hambis's 1963 Avertissement to Vol. II, which states that for the later part of the alphabet only a list of planned (not drafted) entries survived Pelliot's death; however, that specific "never written" list was not itself located, so the conclusion rests on a systematic negative search rather than an explicit confirming statement ([V-3 index pages 0251–0253, 0050–0070](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-c-104/); continuous pagination across vols. I–II independently confirmed by Peter Jackson, "Marco Polo and His 'Travels'," *BSOAS* 61.1 (1998)). Annotators should not cite a Pelliot "SEILAN" entry; if Pelliot's view on Ceylon specifically is needed, it must be sourced to these scattered, separately page-checked cross-references, not to a unified article.

## 2. The Pasai gravestone: a live three-way dispute, not a settled replacement

Background for the Ferlec/Samudra-Pasai chapter: the stone traditionally identified with al-Malik al-Salih, Pasai's semi-legendary first Muslim ruler, bears a death date read as Ramadan 696 AH (23 June–22 July 1297 CE) — roughly 15–20 years before Polo's visit (c. 1292) — but *whether the physical stone standing today is contemporary with that date* is contested in print as recently as 2022, and Polo's own text says nothing about the tomb at all. **Elizabeth Lambourn** is reported, via a secondary paraphrase in a rival 2022 article, to have argued on art-historical and epigraphic grounds that the stone was carved roughly two centuries later, c. 1480s–1520s (Lambourn, "Tombstones, Texts, and Typologies," *Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient* 51.2 (2008): 252–286) — a dating independently repeated, as settled background rather than live controversy, by Feener, Daly, McKinnon et al. in a 2021 survey article on northern-Sumatran gravestones (DOI [10.1080/13639811.2021.1873564](https://doi.org/10.1080/13639811.2021.1873564)). Lambourn also separately argues, contrary to Moquette's older claim, that there is no identifiable Gujarati/Cambay production centre to link to this particular stone — a dating dispute, not an import-origin dispute. None of Lambourn's three relevant articles (2003, 2004, 2008) were read in primary form in this research; every claim attributed to her rests on secondary paraphrase, corroborated independently once (Feener et al.) but never quote-verified against her own wording.

Directly opposing this, **Muhammad Uzair bin Ismail, Zuliskandar Ramli & Ros Mahwati Ahmad Zakaria**, "Islamic Art Influences on Several Batu Aceh Gravestones in Sumatra and the Malay Peninsular: Revisited," *The Islamic Quarterly* 66.1 (2022) — read directly via automated extraction of the PDF hosted at mail.iccuk.org — explicitly rejects Lambourn's redating on calligraphic and decorative grounds and concludes the stone "is indeed contemporary with his date of death." The same article also surveys and rejects a third, older position: Othman Mohd. Yatim (cited 1985) held that the present stone replaced an earlier, possibly damaged original while preserving the recorded date — a middle position between "original" and "much later fabrication" that was not independently verified in this research. **Claude Guillot and Ludvik Kalus**, authors of the major book-length epigraphic study of the whole Pasai funerary corpus (*Les monuments funéraires et l'histoire du sultanat de Pasai à Sumatra*, Cahier d'Archipel 37, Paris: Association Archipel, 2008, ISBN 978-2-910513-53-5), classify Pasai gravestones into six types and trace one class ("ogival") to Gujarat/Cambay — but this is a general typological claim, and **their specific verdict on the Malik al-Salih stone could not be recovered**: the only accessible material, a book review by Dick van der Meij (*Studia Islamika* 17.1 (2010): 183–193, read in full), does not discuss Malik al-Salih's tomb by name. No source accessed states or implies any consensus either way; the recommended framing is that this is a live, multiply-attested scholarly disagreement, with Ismail et al. 2022 explicitly a minority rebuttal published to contest an otherwise more settled-sounding later-dating view, and Guillot & Kalus's actual position an open gap pending direct access to their 402-page book.

## 3. Abash and the "1288" bishop story: Taddesse Tamrat does not make the attribution claimed

Marco Polo's own text — Henry Yule (tr.), *The Book of Ser Marco Polo*, 3rd ed. rev. Cordier (1903), Book III ch. XXXV, read in full via Project Gutenberg EBook #12410 — dates the bishop-and-circumcision-and-war episode to "the year of Christ, 1288" and names no king, bishop, or sultan; every identification discussed below is later scholarly inference, not anything in Polo. **Taddesse Tamrat**, *Church and State in Ethiopia 1270–1527* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1972), was read in full (Internet Archive, identifier `church-and-state-in-ethiopia`, unrestricted OCR, 932,377 characters, searched exhaustively). The word "Polo" occurs exactly once in the entire book, as a footnote citing Conti Rossini's 1940 article "Marco Polo e l'Etiopia" (*Atti della Reale Istituto Veneto* 99.2: 1024–8, itself not located online) — and that footnote is attached to a discussion of **Yǝkunno Amlak**, not his son Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon. Taddesse discusses Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon's reign (1285–94) in detail elsewhere but never connects it to Marco Polo, Aden, Adal, Zeila, or any war. **The claim that Taddesse Tamrat identifies Polo's king with Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon is therefore contradicted by his own text and should not be attributed to him.**

The Trimingham and Pankhurst attributions could not be verified at all against primary text. **J. Spencer Trimingham**, *Islam in Ethiopia* (Oxford, 1952), and **Richard Pankhurst**, *History of Ethiopian Towns from the Middle Ages to the Early Nineteenth Century* (Äthiopistische Forschungen 8, Wiesbaden: Franz Steiner, 1982) — title independently confirmed via Cambridge/BSOAS review metadata — are both reported, via the English Wikipedia article "Yagbeʾu Seyon" and its mirrors (en-academic.com, Wikiwand, Grokipedia), to identify Polo's king with Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon and to read "Aden" as Adal, with Zeila as the sultan's capital, at specific pages (Trimingham pp. 69f., Pankhurst p. 55). All three Internet Archive copies of Trimingham are access-restricted, no Pankhurst copy was located anywhere, and no library/JSTOR access was available in this research, so **these attributions rest entirely on secondhand, Wikipedia-sourced citations and were not independently verified.** What the primary edition *does* establish directly is that **Henry Yule himself**, writing in 1871/1903, already raised and partly endorsed the Aden/Adal(Zeila) conflation — decades before any 20th-century Ethiopianist — while explicitly rejecting Polo's own 1288 date and instead assigning the episode to Yǝkunno Amlak with the date pushed back to c. 1278, on the ground that Amda Ṣeyon's comparable wars are independently dated to 1315–16 and could not have been known to Polo by 1298. The chronological logic for why a modern scholar might prefer Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon instead — taking Polo's stated 1288 at face value, which falls within his reign (1285–94) rather than his father's — is the research's own inference, not something stated outright in any source read. Moule & Pelliot's relevant glossary entry was sought but not located in the time available and remains an open lead.

## 4. Takashima shipwreck: Chinese-built hull material, artifact dating to 1281, no itemized storm evidence

Randall Sasaki's 2015 book, *The Origins of the Lost Fleet of the Mongol Empire* (Texas A&M University Press, 2015, ISBN 9781623492304), was read in full (archive.org item "Book_1137," ~364,000 characters across three passes) and is the strongest-evidenced source in this research. It supports that a substantially intact lower hull was excavated at Takashima/Kozaki by a University of the Ryukyus team led by Yoshifumi Ikeda (first reported 2011, further finds 2015), that the broader timber assemblage (502 catalogued pieces) is overwhelmingly fragmentary (only nine pieces ≥200 cm, nearly 60% heavily degraded), and that wood-species analysis (camphor and China fir predominating among hull components, e.g. "camphor wood does not grow in Korea, and thus these timbers most certainly came from China") combined with diagonally-driven iron nails and bulkhead construction points to a southern Chinese origin, likely Fujian/Quanzhou, for much of the hull material. A single Goryeo-style Korean Buddha statue was also recovered, but it was raised in the 19th century under uncontrolled conditions with no secure findspot, and Sasaki's own 2008 thesis abstract (accessed only via a Medievalists.net summary, not the full thesis) explicitly states "no conclusive statements can be made regarding the origins of the vessels" — a notably more hedged position than the 2015 book, which the annotation should flag as an evolving scholarly judgment rather than a settled fact.

Dating rests chiefly on **artifact association, not absolute dating of the wood**: a bronze official seal bearing a date corresponding to 1277 CE in Phags-pa script is Sasaki's key artifact linking the site to the Yuan invasion forces and situating it within the historical window of the 1281 campaign, alongside ceramic storage jars and cast-iron bomb casings ("tetsuhau"). Radiocarbon evidence is thin: only a single uncalibrated figure (864±18 BP) for one sample was located, with fuller results explicitly deferred by Sasaki to an un-located primary site report; no dendrochronology was found in the sources accessed. Critically, **the storm/"kamikaze" damage narrative is not supported by any itemized physical-damage evidence in the sources reviewed.** Sasaki attributes the site's general fragmentation to "turbulent conditions, including currents and heavy silt" — a taphonomic explanation, not a one-time storm-sinking signature — and is explicitly skeptical of the storm's centrality to the invasion's military outcome ("perhaps there was really no need for the storm"), arguing Japanese shore defenses mattered more. The one specific, itemized damage pattern he documents — at least 35 charred/burned timbers and a heavily burned windlass stand — points toward **fire**, not storm, though Sasaki notes "there is no direct evidence to prove this point" for a specific cause. Construction-defect evidence is also inconclusive; Sasaki leans toward adequate (not hasty or faulty) construction while separately noting Yuan-dynasty Chinese sources that describe pre-storm logistical problems (rotting ships, food shortages) independent of any storm. The task's top-priority source, **Ikeda, Sasaki & Kimura, "Recent Underwater Investigations at Takashima," *Current Science* 117.10 (25 November 2019): 1635–1639, DOI [10.18520/cs/v117/i10/1635-1639](https://doi.org/10.18520/cs/v117/i10/1635-1639)**, could not be accessed beyond its confirmed bibliographic citation — the journal's own PDF was robots-blocked and no abstract was recoverable via secondary indexes — and no specific finding from it should be cited pending direct access. No source accessed connects any specific excavated vessel to any specific passage or figure in Polo's own account, and none should be implied.

## 5. The Trebizond loss: a named responsible party, but no specified mechanism

This episode belongs to the Prologue of the *Travels* (the Polos' return journey), not Book III proper, though it is the episode the annotation project tracks as III-09/10 — worth flagging for the locator. A.C. Moule & Paul Pelliot, *Marco Polo: The Description of the World*, vol. I (London: Routledge, 1938), p. 15 and List of Abbreviations (read in full via archive.org OCR, item `descriptionofwor01polo`), cite **G. Orlandini, "Marco Polo e la sua Famiglia," *Archivio Veneto-Tridentino*, vol. IX (1926), pp. 1–68**, with the relevant will-text pinpointed to "Or. p. 27, d.6." Orlandini's full first name was not found anywhere, and **the article itself remains inaccessible**: no digitized copy turned up on Internet Archive, HathiTrust, Google Books, or Internet Culturale, and no library catalog record was independently opened, so its existence and citation are known only secondhand, through Moule-Pelliot's reference — the task brief's locator of "document 6, pp. 25–31" could not be independently verified against the original journal.

What Moule & Pelliot's English translation of document 6 (Maffeo Polo's will, dated 6 February 1309 Venetian style = 1310 modern, settling accounts with his nephew Marco) actually says is that 1,000 pounds were received from the Doge and Commune of Venice "for part of the loss inflicted on us both by **the lord Comnenus of Trebizond**... and in the territory of the same lord Comnenus and also in other affairs of ours," alongside jewels and gold paiza tablets from the Great Khan treated as a settlement between Maffeo and Marco. This is secondhand access only — Moule-Pelliot's English rendering of Orlandini's edition, not the Latin/Venetian original and not even Orlandini's own Italian commentary — so no original-language passage can be quoted here. **The document names the Trapezuntine ruler as responsible for the loss and locates part of it within his jurisdiction, but at no point in the accessible translation does it specify a mechanism** — no word corresponding to robbery, theft, piracy, toll, or forced exaction appears. Moule & Pelliot's own narrative voice is in fact vaguer than the document they quote, using only "trouble" and "(serious) loss" ("the party got into trouble and suffered serious loss"), and their footnotes state their entire account is "based on Orlandini's article... especially on d.6" with no independent corroborating source cited. The prior finding that this is a loss, not an established robbery, is confirmed and should stand: the document is somewhat more specific than Moule-Pelliot's narrative (it names the responsible party) but neither source, as accessed, establishes "robbery" as the mechanism. No source consulted states or implies that Marco Polo personally witnessed the Trebizond events; the will establishes only that he had a financial stake in the shared loss.

## Unresolved questions and best safe fallback wording

Several gaps recur across all five checks and should be treated as standing limitations on the annotation, not as resolved by omission. **Trimingham (1952) and Pankhurst (1982) primary texts were never accessed** (all Internet Archive copies of Trimingham are access-restricted; no copy of Pankhurst was found at all) — fallback: present the Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon/Adal-Zeila identification as "reported by Wikipedia-derived secondary sources with precise page citations (Trimingham pp. 69f., Pankhurst p. 55), not independently verified against the primary texts," and recommend a library or JSTOR check before stating it as settled. **Guillot & Kalus's specific verdict on the Malik al-Salih stone was not recovered** from their 402-page book (only a review that does not mention the stone by name was accessible) — fallback: cite Guillot & Kalus only for their general six-type typology of the Pasai corpus, and state explicitly that their position on this specific stone requires direct consultation of the book. **The Ikeda, Sasaki & Kimura (2019) Current Science article (DOI 10.18520/cs/v117/i10/1635-1639) was never accessed beyond its bibliographic citation** — fallback: do not cite any specific finding from it; note only that it exists and addresses "recent underwater investigations at Takashima." **Orlandini's original Italian/Latin text (1926) was never accessed**, so no original-language quotation of the will passage or confirmation of the "pp. 25–31" locator for document 6 is possible — fallback: attribute all content of document 6 to "Moule & Pelliot's English translation of Orlandini's document 6," never to Orlandini directly, and do not assert document 6 establishes a specific loss mechanism. **Moule-Pelliot's Abash glossary entry was sought but not located**, so their position (if any) on the Yagbeʾa Ṣeyon identification or the Aden/Adal/Zeila question remains unknown — fallback: silence on this point in the annotation, or a flagged "not checked" note, rather than an assumed alignment with either Yule or the later Ethiopianists. Finally, Lambourn's three primary articles (2003, 2004, 2008) were read only through secondary paraphrase (principally by Ismail et al. 2022, an opposing scholar) and one independent corroborating citation (Feener et al. 2021) — fallback: attribute her dating argument explicitly to "Lambourn (2008), as summarized in later literature," and flag that her own wording has not been quote-verified. In every one of these cases, the safest annotation posture is to name the source making the claim, state the access limitation plainly, and avoid presenting a secondhand or partially-sourced attribution as settled scholarly fact.
