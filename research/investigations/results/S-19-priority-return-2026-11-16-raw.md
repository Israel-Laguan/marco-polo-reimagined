# Five endnote clusters checked against primary witnesses

Across all five numbered clusters, the pattern is consistent: Marco Polo's narrative is never *named* in any surviving Chinese or Persian document consulted in this research pass, and every "corroboration" that exists is either a circumstantial convergence of independent records (the Kököchin mission) or a general-culture parallel (Rubruck on ger-carts and kumis) rather than a verbatim eyewitness match to Polo's specific claims. The Yuan shi/Rashid al-Din material on Nayan and Ahmad Fanakati (cluster 1) could not be opened in the original or in a cited English translation in this pass, so the comparison with Polo's text remains at the level of modern secondary paraphrase, not a primary-source check. Cleaves's 1976 article (cluster 2) was likewise never obtained directly; what the notes establish, via Peter Jackson's 1998 BSOAS paraphrase and a 2025 overview by Ma Xiaolin, is that neither the Chinese nor the Persian document names a Polo, that the underlying Chinese document was first identified by Yang Zhijiu in 1941 (not "discovered" by Cleaves), and that the arrival/departure dates are themselves a reconciliation across two silent-on-Polo sources, not a reading off either one directly. Rubruck (cluster 3) was fully read in Rockhill's 1900 public-domain translation and gives genuine, page-cited, quotable parallels for ger-carts (p. 55) and kumis production (pp. 66–67, 85), but only as general Mongol-steppe corroboration, decades before and geographically short of anything Polo describes in China or the ocean. Ibn Battuta's ship and port material (also cluster 3) is comparably strong on Hormuz but could not be pinned to a verified page in the in-copyright Gibb translation, and the Aden/Kish material in the one accessible excerpt is affected by a source-text conflation (horse-export belongs to Dhofar, not Aden, in the text read) that must not be smoothed over. Juvaini, Zhou Daguan, Wang Dayuan, and al-Qazwini (cluster 4) range from solidly confirmed at the topic level (Juvaini's silence on the hashish-garden legend) to simply inaccessible in this pass (Wang Dayuan/Rockhill, al-Qazwini's rukh passage). Finally, the rights audit (cluster 5) clears four English translations for verbatim commercial quotation — Yule-Cordier 1903, Rockhill's two Hakluyt/T'oung Pao translations, and Samuel Lee's 1829 Ibn Battuta — while firmly blocking Gibb/Beckingham, Thackston, Boyle, and Harris, and leaving Pelliot's 1951 posthumous Zhou Daguan volume and Karl Jahn's death date as open, unresolved legal and biographical questions respectively.

## Cluster 1 — Nayan and Ahmad: Polo's text is solid, the Chinese and Persian comparanda are not yet in hand

The only part of this cluster resting on direct primary-source reading is Polo's own account. The Yule-Cordier 1903 text (read via the Project Gutenberg edition of volume 1, cross-referenced against the archive.org page-image scan for verbatim use) gives, for Nayan: Book II, Ch. II "Concerning the Revolt of Nayan, who was Uncle to the Great Kaan Cublay" (pp. 332–334), describing Nayan as a "great Tartar Chief" commanding "300,000 horsemen" who conspired with Kaidu; Ch. III (pp. 335–336) on Kublai's secret, rapid march; Ch. IV (pp. 336–339), ending "Nayan lost the battle and was utterly routed... taken prisoners"; and Ch. V (p. 343), where Nayan is executed by being "wrapt in a carpet, and tossed to and fro so mercilessly that he died," with a Yule/Cordier editorial footnote (p. 344) citing a parallel from "Friar Ricold." For Ahmad, Book II, Ch. XXIII, "Concerning the Oppressions of Achmath the Bailo" (pp. 415–419), gives Achmath's tyranny (p. 416), his "five-and-twenty sons" (p. 417), and the assassination narrative naming the conspirators "Vanchu" and "Chenchu" and the impersonated prince "Chinkin" (p. 419) ([Gutenberg #10636, vol. 1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10636/old/10636-h/10636-h.htm); verbatim use should be checked against the archive.org page-image scan, identifier `bookofsermarcopo001polo`).

What the notes could **not** do is open the Yuan shi itself. A Chinese Wikisource link for juan 205 (the traditional "biographies of treacherous ministers" juan commonly associated with Ahmad) exists but the fetch failed — this is catalog/search-snippet evidence only, not a read of the text, and no juan number for Nayan's revolt (plausibly in the Basic Annals of Shizu for 1287) was confirmed from any source actually opened. No English translation of either Yuan shi passage, by Cleaves, Franke, or anyone else, was located and read. On the Rashid al-Din side, the one page citation found — Boyle's *Successors of Genghis Khan* (1971), p. 12, cited by Wikipedia's "Ahmad Banakati" article in connection with Polo's naming of "Bailo Acmat" — is, on its structure (page 12, very early in the volume, explicitly comparative), almost certainly Boyle's own editorial footnote comparing Rashid al-Din to Polo, not Rashid al-Din's primary narrative naming Polo; this was not independently confirmed either way because p. 12 itself was never opened. Whether Boyle's volume (which covers the Ögedei–Möngke period) even extends into the Khubilai-era material needed for Nayan or Ahmad is also unresolved.

On the specific, carefully worded question the project most needs answered — do the Yuan shi or Rashid al-Din name Marco Polo? — the notes find no evidence that either does, but every supporting statement found is a *general* null-result claim (Rossabi, writing for Columbia's Asia for Educators site, that Polo's name is absent from "the massive documentation" of the Mongol period; an Association for Asian Studies teaching document stating "absolutely no record of Marco Polo in the Yangzhou gazetteers") rather than a source that says "I checked Yuan shi juan X / Rashid al-Din page Y and he is not there." Igor de Rachewiltz's 1997 article "Marco Polo Went to China" — the piece most likely to contain that granular check — was never obtained in full text, only located at a catalog/abstract level (ANU repository, Project MUSE). Stephen Haw's *Marco Polo's China* (2006) and Hans Ulrich Vogel's *Marco Polo Was in China* (2013) were likewise not opened.

A secondary, partial corroboration worth flagging cautiously: the names "Vanchu" and "Chenchu" in Polo's assassination narrative plausibly correspond to the modern-scholarship names "Wang Zhu" (王著) and "Gao Heshang" (高和尚) given in English Wikipedia's summary of Ahmad's death, and the general shape of the story (two-man plot, impersonation of the arriving crown prince, decapitation) matches. But this match is drawn from a tertiary Wikipedia summary of modern scholarship, not a Yuan shi passage actually read, and should not be elevated past that.

## Cluster 2 — Kököchin: Cleaves links, he does not discover, and neither source names a Polo

The project's own prior reading (Thackston pp. 593 and 606; Jahn's Persian edition p. 40) already established that Rashid al-Din's narrative of Kököchin's marriage to Ghazan names no Polo and describes no sea route. This research pass could not obtain Cleaves's 1976 *Harvard Journal of Asiatic Studies* article (vol. 36, pp. 181–203, JSTOR 2718743, DOI 10.2307/2718743 — bibliographic details confirmed independently via Ma Xiaolin 2025 and Wikipedia's "Kököchin" article) in full text; no open-access PDF, JSTOR full text, or archive.org copy was found. Everything reported below about Cleaves's own argument is therefore **secondhand paraphrase**, drawn principally from Peter Jackson's "Marco Polo and his 'Travels,'" *BSOAS* 61.1 (1998), pp. 82–101 (read via an Amherst-hosted PDF), from Ma Xiaolin's 2025 overview "Chinese Research on Marco Polo's *Devisement dou monde*" (read via a Firenze University Press PDF), and from an NYU student research page paraphrasing the Chinese scholar Yang Zhijiu. No sentence of Cleaves's own prose is quoted anywhere in the notes, and the report below is written on that basis — citations to "Cleaves" throughout this section mean "per Jackson 1998, paraphrasing Cleaves" or "per Ma Xiaolin 2025, paraphrasing Cleaves," never a reading of Cleaves's own text.

On the Chinese source: secondary accounts disagree about exactly which document is at issue. Ma Xiaolin's 2025 overview (pp. 82–83) calls it "a hitherto-overlooked passage in the *Yuanshi*," while a more textually specific NYU-hosted page, quoting Yang Zhijiu directly, identifies it instead as a passage in the ***Yongle Dadian*, juan 19418**, quoting the Chinese line "上言今年三月奉㫖遣兀魯䚟阿必失呵火者" — an internal administrative document (consistent with the *Jingshi Dadian* postal-relay/"Zhan chi" section), not a narrative history. Jackson's own article does not name either text explicitly, citing Cleaves 1976 in footnote 87 only for the conclusion that the mission "is confirmed by both Chinese and Persian sources." Both the "Yuanshi" and "Yongle Dadian" namings should be flagged as **unresolved** pending direct access to Cleaves's own citation — this is exactly the kind of discrepancy the project asked not to be smoothed over.

Crucially, per Ma Xiaolin (2025, p. 83) and a listing in her reference list (p. 95), this Chinese document was **first identified and connected to Marco Polo by the Chinese historian Yang Zhijiu (Yang Chih-chiu) in 1941**, who published an English-language note on it in 1944 titled (as transcribed secondhand) "A New Discovery referring to Marco Polo's Departure from the Chinese Source." Cleaves's 1976 contribution, as every secondary source agrees, was to bring Yang's already-published Chinese find to wider Western/Mongolist attention by joining it rigorously to a Persian source — not to discover the Chinese document himself. Any endnote must not call the Chinese source "newly discovered by Cleaves"; if "newly discovered" belongs to anyone, it is Yang Zhijiu, in 1941.

On the Persian side, every secondary source agrees the source is Rashid al-Din's *Jami' al-Tawarikh*, recording the arrival in Persia of an envoy named Qoja/Khwāja (火者) escorting princess Kököchin — and, again per Jackson's explicit statement, "the Europeans are not mentioned in either of these eastern accounts." The NYU-hosted page, paraphrasing Yang Zhijiu, adds that Rashid al-Din names only one of the three envoys (Qoja/Khwāja), explained by Yang's claim that the other two died en route. It remains unconfirmed from secondary sources whether Cleaves's Persian-text citation is to Karl Jahn's edition (the same edition, at p. 40, already read by the project) or a different edition/pagination current in 1976 — this is plausible but not established.

The logical structure of Cleaves's argument, as relayed through Jackson, Ma Xiaolin, and the NYU page, is explicitly triangulation, not a single-document naming: the Chinese record dates and names three envoys (transliterated across sources as Uladai/Uludai 兀魯[歹/䚟], Abishqa/Abisqa 阿必失呵, and Qoja/Khwāja 火者); Polo's own book names the same three men ("Oulatai, Apousca, Coja") as his fellow-travelers; and Rashid al-Din's Persian record independently confirms one of the three (Qoja) arriving in Persia escorting Kököchin. No single document in Chinese or Persian names Polo; Polo's presence is an **inference** built by combining the three threads — Jackson's own words are "the Europeans are not mentioned in either of these eastern accounts." The project's suggested name "Uluget" does not match any transliteration found in any source (the closest is "Uladai"/"Uludai") and appears to be a garbling.

On dating, secondary sources converge loosely but not exactly: Ma Xiaolin (per Yang Zhijiu) gives orders dated to "the eighth lunar month of 1290," with departure "at the very end of 1290 or the first days of 1291"; Jackson gives "April–May 1290" for the order to prepare, and "c. 1292–93" for arrival in Persia, while his own footnote 87 clarifies that Rashid al-Din's text gives **no explicit arrival date**, only that arrival was clearly before the winter of 1293–4. Wikipedia's "Kököchin" article gives a still fuller timeline (betrothal 1291, departure from Quanzhou spring 1291, sailing from Zayton early 1292, arrival in Persia about 1293) without making clear how much of that granularity is Cleaves's own versus later harmonizing by Barnes & Singleton (2008) or de Rachewiltz. The "8th lunar month" vs. "April–May 1290" discrepancy is flagged as unresolved rather than silently reconciled — it may simply be a lunar-to-Gregorian conversion difference somewhere in the paraphrase chain, but this was not verified.

## Cluster 3 — Rubruck gives genuine quotable parallels; Ibn Battuta's are real but harder to pin down

This is the strongest-evidenced cluster in the whole research pass, because Rockhill's 1900 Hakluyt Society translation of Rubruck was read directly in both an archive.org page-image-backed scan and a plain-text OCR mirror, with page numbers confirmed from body text rather than a table of contents. On ger-carts (endnote I-12), **printed page 55** of Rockhill's translation reads: "And they make these houses so large that they are sometimes thirty feet in width... I myself once measured the width between the wheel-tracks of a cart xx feet, and when the house was on the cart it projected beyond the wheels on either side v feet at least... The axle of the cart was as large as the mast of a ship... I have counted to one cart xxii oxen drawing one house" ([archive.org djvu text](https://archive.org/stream/journeywilliamr01ruysgoog/journeywilliamr01ruysgoog_djvu.txt); page-image copy at [archive.org/details/journeyofwilliam00ruys](https://archive.org/details/journeyofwilliam00ruys)). On kumis (mare's milk; endnote III-01), **pages 66–67** give the production process ("This cosmos, which is mare's milk, is made in this wise"; churning with a stick "as big as a man's head"; fermentation "boil[ing] up like new wine"), while **page 85** gives a separate, later passage recording Rubruck's own visceral first reaction ("I broke out in a sweat with horror and surprise"). These are two distinct passages from different points in the narrative, and an endnote citing "production" should point to pp. 66–67, not p. 85.

The scope limitation is equally important and must not be blurred: Rubruck's embassy ran 1253–1255, roughly two decades before Polo's own travels (c. 1271–1295), and was confined to the Pontic-Caspian steppe route and Karakorum — he never reached China, Persia proper, or the Indian Ocean. An essay by Paul Kahn ("Reading The Book of Marco Polo") gives the standard view that Rubruck, Carpini, and Polo were "entirely unaware of his predecessors," so there is no established textual dependency in either direction — but no source found gives a specific, named ruling from Larner, Haw, or de Rachewiltz on whether Polo could have known Rubruck's text; this remains an open gap, not a settled "no." Rubruck's testimony therefore corroborates general Mongol steppe material culture that Polo would also have encountered — it is **not** eyewitness corroboration of anything Polo describes specifically in China or at sea, and an endnote must say so explicitly rather than imply Rubruck confirms Polo's China material.

Ibn Battuta's evidence is real but more fragmented in what was actually accessed. On ships (endnote III-04), the only body text read directly was one page of Henry Yule's *Cathay and the Way Thither* (a separate, public-domain Hakluyt Society compilation, not Ibn Battuta's own translation), which states on printed p. 176 that there were "three classes" of Chinese vessel — "Junk," "Zao," and "Kakam" — but the visible text breaks off before any attribution footnote, so it is **not confirmed** whether this sentence is Yule's own synthesis, a quotation of Ibn Battuta, or a quotation of Polo ([dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko](https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/toyobunko/III-2-F-b-2/V-2/page/0176.html.en)). Popular secondary summaries (a Berkeley ORIAS-sourced page reproduced at factsanddetails.com) attribute to Ibn Battuta details of five-deck junks, "interior cabins and even private lavatories," and crews "up to 1,000" at Calicut — but these are explicit paraphrase, without a Gibb volume/page citation, and the "watertight compartments" detail specifically was **not confirmed** in any primary-source body text read. The in-copyright Gibb/Beckingham translation (vols. I–IV, 1958–1994) was never obtained in full; a public-domain Fordham "Medieval Sourcebook" excerpt of (likely) the 1829 Samuel Lee translation was read in full and contains **no** junk/deck/crew passage at all — only a forward-reference promising a description "later," which does not appear in that particular excerpted page. This specific claim should therefore be reported as **access-level: not found in any primary text actually read**, with the Gibb volume IV (completed by Beckingham, covering India/China) flagged as the needed next source.

On ports (endnotes III-07–08), the same Fordham excerpt (translator unidentified on the page, likely but not confirmed to be Lee 1829 or a paraphrase of it) gives real, specific lines: for Hormuz, "Hormuz is a town on the coast, called also Mughistan," and separately "New Hormuz, which is an island" roughly nine miles offshore — directly confirming Ibn Battuta visited and distinguished both Old (mainland) and New (island) Hormuz, consistent with Polo's own account. For Aden, the same text calls it "the port of the Indians" and describes its merchants and water supply, but **contains no frankincense or horse-trade passage for Aden itself** — horse export is instead attributed, in this text, to **Dhofar**, under a separate heading. If the retelling's draft endnote currently pairs Aden with a frankincense/horse-trade detail from Ibn Battuta, that pairing should be checked against the actual passage, because in the text read here that detail belongs to Dhofar, not Aden — this is exactly the kind of inferred-from-contents error the project asked to guard against. For Kish/Qais, the Fordham text never uses the name "Kish" at all; it describes a town called "Qays," which this particular text additionally (and confusingly) equates with Siraf — historically two distinct Gulf ports. Whether this is a genuine Ibn Battuta statement, a translation quirk of this specific older rendering, or an editorial compression in the excerpted sourcebook was **not resolved**. A separate secondary source (ORIAS Berkeley) cites a pearl-diving passage to "Gibb, vol. II, pp. 408–409" without stating whether it concerns Hormuz, Kish, or a generalized description of the Gulf — so that citation cannot be used to confirm a Kish-specific passage either.

## Cluster 4 — Juvaini confirmed at the topic level; Zhou Daguan, Wang Dayuan, and al-Qazwini largely not found

Juvaini's *Tarikh-i Jahangushay* (trans. Boyle, *The History of the World-Conqueror*, Manchester UP, 1958) is a genuine eyewitness/participant account — Juvaini personally drafted the proclamation of Alamut's 1256 surrender and selected which books from its library to save before the rest were burned ("I burnt them all," per a secondary quotation of his own words). Only the Translator's Introduction (pp. xxi–xxii) was actually opened via an archive.org scan; the body-text siege chapters themselves, where exact page numbers for the fall of Alamut would sit, were **not accessed**, so no specific page range can be given for that narrative in Boyle's 1958 pagination. What is well supported, via a secondary synthesis explicitly citing the historian Farhad Daftary (an Institute of Ismaili Studies encyclopedia article, and a related blog post citing Peter Willey's description of Juvaini's eyewitness report), is that Juvaini's account "made no mention of a garden or palace," and that the garden/hashish/trained-assassin legend is understood by modern scholarship to have reached its fullest, most influential form specifically in **Marco Polo's own account** — a late synthesis built on looser Crusader-era "hashishi" rumors, not something found in Juvaini or in contemporary hostile Muslim sources generally (Daftary, per the IIS article: "contemporary non-Ismaili Muslim sources... remain silent on this subject"). This is Juvaini's silence, not contradiction — the distinction the project asked to be preserved — and Polo should be characterized as a vector/synthesizer of a pre-existing legend, not as someone corroborated by Juvaini or any other near-contemporary Persian historian. Neither Rashid al-Din nor Hamd-Allah Mustawfi's specific treatment of Alamut (if any) could be located in this pass.

Zhou Daguan's *Customs of Cambodia* (真臘風土記, composed c. 1296–97, making him a genuine first-person eyewitness roughly contemporary with the very end of Polo's own travels) was accessed only in fragments: a Wikipedia-quoted line from Harris's 2007 translation (p. 70) about Chinese men taking local wives, and two passages from a different, 1992 Darcy Paul English edition (pp. 15, 17–19) on young Khmer men currying favor with Chinese visitors and a note that Chinese are barred from witnessing a local deflowering ceremony. **None of these is the dedicated merchants/trade passage the endnote apparently anticipates** — this should be reported as an access-level gap (extract/preview only), not as a confirmed absence of such a passage in Zhou Daguan's full text. Neither Peter Harris's full 2007 translation nor Pelliot's 1902 French original (available in page-image PDF, per the rights notes) was read in full in this pass.

Wang Dayuan (汪大淵), author of the *Daoyi Zhilüe* (c. 1349), is confirmed by general reference material as a genuine first-person eyewitness-traveler through Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean in the 1330s — but his voyages and book postdate Polo's own travels (final return 1295; death 1324) by several decades, so even if his testimony on Hormuz, Malabar, or Sumatra could be read, it would corroborate general, continuing early-14th-century trade conditions, not Polo's own moment. In any case, W. W. Rockhill's English rendering/study of Wang Dayuan ("Notes on the Relations and Trade of China with the Eastern Archipelago and the Coast of the Indian Ocean during the Fourteenth Century," *T'oung Pao*, 1914–15) was **never located and read** in this pass despite its existence being confirmed bibliographically (a Western Australian Museum shipwreck-archaeology bibliography entry, and the page-image PDF identified in the rights notes at archive.org identifier `2tungpaotoungpao15corduoft`). This entire sub-cluster should be treated as **not found**, not as negative evidence.

Al-Qazwini's rukh passage (*'Aja'ib al-Makhluqat*, c. 1260s) was similarly not located in any quotable English rendering. The rukh is confirmed, via general reference sources, to belong to a much older Arabic/Persian cosmography and marvels-literature tradition (with antecedents toward pre-Islamic and Indian Ocean sailors' lore and parallels in the Sindbad cycle) rather than a claim tied to a contemporary 13th-century sighting by al-Qazwini himself — consistent with the project's own framing — but this was **not confirmed from al-Qazwini's own wording**, which remains inaccessible. A targeted check of one folklore blog (abookofcreatures.com) returned no al-Qazwini material at all, citing instead the later author al-Damiri — a genuine negative finding, logged honestly rather than substituted with a different source.

## Cluster 5 — Rights and editions: four texts cleared, four blocked, two open legal/biographical questions

The rights audit treats "freely readable online" and "public domain" as entirely separate questions — a point worth stating plainly because several sources in this project are hosted on archive.org or HathiTrust while remaining in copyright (Gibb/Beckingham, Thackston), and conversely some public-domain texts (Yule-Cordier, Rockhill) happen to be hosted the same way. Status is determined by the US 95-year rule (as of 1 January 2026, works published through 1930 are US public domain) or the UK/EU life+70-post-mortem-auctoris rule, never by where a file is hosted ([Cornell Copyright guide](https://guides.library.cornell.edu/copyright/publicdomain); [Library of Congress, "Lifecycle of Copyright: 1930 Works in the Public Domain"](https://blogs.loc.gov/copyright/2025/12/lifecycle-of-copyright-1930-works-in-the-public-domain/)). Separately, an OCR text file or plain-text transcription (a Gutenberg `.htm`, an archive.org `_djvu.txt` dump) is a derivative that can silently corrupt wording (long-s substitutions, ligatures, hyphenation errors); any verbatim quotation should be checked against the actual page-image scan before use, and this image-vs-OCR check was **not personally performed** for any specific sentence in this research pass — it still needs to be done by whoever finalizes the exact wording.

## Unresolved questions and recommended follow-up research

The single highest-value follow-up is obtaining Cleaves 1976 directly (HJAS 36, JSTOR 2718743) to confirm, in his own words, which Chinese compilation he cites (Yuanshi vs. *Yongle Dadian* juan 19418), his own translation of both passages, his own stated dates, and how he credits Yang Zhijiu's 1941/1944 priority — everything in cluster 2 currently rests on paraphrase of Cleaves by Jackson (1998) and Ma Xiaolin (2025), not on his own text. Second, the Yuan shi passages on Nayan (likely in the Basic Annals of Shizu, juan unconfirmed) and on Ahmad (plausibly juan 205) should be opened directly, ideally alongside Rossabi's *Khubilai Khan* (1988, located at UC Press but not read) for an English-language narrative with page numbers, and Rachewiltz's 1997 "Marco Polo Went to China" should be tracked down in full to get a source-specific (not merely general) statement that Polo is absent from both the Yuan shi and Rashid al-Din. Third, Gibb vol. IV (completed by Beckingham, covering India and China) needs to be consulted directly for the Calicut junk and Aden/Hormuz/Kish passages, since the only freely accessible excerpt found (Fordham's unlabeled "Medieval Sourcebook" page) is of uncertain edition and already shows one likely error (the Dhofar/Aden horse-trade conflation). Fourth, Wang Dayuan via Rockhill's *T'oung Pao* articles (1914–15) and al-Qazwini's rukh passage both need a direct read — a page-image PDF exists for the former and was simply not opened in this pass, while the latter may genuinely lack an accessible English translation. Fifth, two specific legal/biographical items need a primary-source resolution before the rights clearance can be called complete: Karl Jahn's actual death date (library authority record, e.g. GND/VIAF, not yet checked), and whether Pelliot's 1951 posthumous Zhou Daguan book underwent US copyright restoration under the URAA.

Five cautious, fully-supported fallback phrasings for the endnotes, each staying inside what was actually verified in this pass:

**Cluster 1 (Nayan/Ahmad):** "Polo's own account of Nayan's rebellion and Ahmad Fanakati's fall (Yule-Cordier, 1903) can be compared with the Yuan shi and Rashid al-Din's history, though the relevant passages in those sources were not independently examined for this note; no Chinese or Persian source consulted names Marco Polo."

**Cluster 2 (Kököchin):** "Chinese and Persian administrative records — brought together by Francis Woodman Cleaves in 1976, building on an identification first made by the Chinese historian Yang Zhijiu in 1941 — independently confirm a Mongol mission escorting a princess to Persia around 1292–93; neither record names any member of the Polo family, and their connection to Polo's own account rests on matching three traveling companions' names across the Chinese and Persian documents and Polo's own narrative."

**Cluster 3 (Rubruck/Ibn Battuta):** "William of Rubruck (1253–55) independently describes Mongol wheeled dwellings and fermented mare's milk in terms consistent with Polo's account, though decades earlier and confined to the steppe, not China; Ibn Battuta (1325–54) independently describes Hormuz's two sites and, per later secondary summaries, large Chinese trading vessels, though the specific wording for the ship passage and for Aden's trade goods could not be confirmed against a named edition in this research pass."

**Cluster 4 (Juvaini/Zhou Daguan/Wang Dayuan/al-Qazwini):** "Juvaini's eyewitness account of Alamut's fall (1256) does not include the garden-and-hashish legend; modern scholarship treats that legend's fully developed form as originating with Polo's own account. Zhou Daguan's eyewitness record of Angkor (c. 1296–97), Wang Dayuan's account of Indian Ocean ports (c. 1349), and al-Qazwini's account of the rukh were not available in sufficiently accessible form in this research pass to quote or cite at the passage level, and should not be presented as confirmed parallels pending further access."

**Cluster 5 (rights):** "Quotations in this edition drawn from Yule and Cordier (1903), Rockhill's translations of Rubruck (1900) and of material on Wang Dayuan (1914–15), and Samuel Lee's 1829 translation of Ibn Battuta are used under public-domain status in the US and UK/EU; all other cited translations (Gibb and Beckingham's Ibn Battuta, Thackston's Rashid al-Din, Boyle's Juvaini and Rashid al-Din, and Harris's Zhou Daguan) are presented in paraphrase with attribution, not as direct quotations, because they remain in copyright."
