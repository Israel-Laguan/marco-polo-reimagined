# S-21 — natural history and marvels: follow-up deep research prompt

```text
I am preparing an English retelling of Marco Polo, based on Yule–Cordier (1903/1920). A first research pass covered 59 topics but often used evidence that a species or custom exists to confirm Polo's particular description, location, chronology, or personal observation. I need a critical, source-led follow-up for chapter Notes, trivia, captions and commodity tables. Prioritize the questions below, deliver partial results if necessary, and provide a coverage ledger for every numbered group and subtopic so unfinished work is visible.

METHOD — read before answering:
• Anchor each finding to the exact passage: Yule volume/book/chapter and, where possible, F or another named witness/edition and its variant. Our project IDs are routing hints, not independent proof of location. Differentiate the Book's claim, a modern identification, and what modern evidence can or cannot establish. Distinguish a species' present distribution from evidence of its distribution, cultivation, extraction or trade at that specific place around 1270–1300. Separate eyewitness, reported information and editorial inference; do not infer personal observation merely from accuracy.
• Prefer directly inspected modern peer-reviewed scholarship, critical commentaries (especially Moule and Pelliot, Marco Polo: The Description of the World, 1938; Pelliot's Notes on Marco Polo; later editions), authoritative museum/botanical/zoological records, archaeometric research and original historical texts. Search non-English scholarship and translate or summarize passages with the source language identified. Older and non-peer-reviewed specialist accounts are useful as attributed leads. Archive/library restrictions are acceptable: say what was inaccessible and provide exact bibliographic and catalogue details, not imagined content.
• For each source, identify what you actually read (full text, specified pages, abstract, catalogue, snippet, or another author's report) and give a page, section, folio or other precise locator where available. A citation to a text, or an abstract, does not mean its relevant argument was read. Never invent a quotation, page, journal, date, DOI, URL, consensus or species. Attribute interpretations and dissent to named authors; mark inference. “Earliest located” is not absolute priority, and a similar motif is not evidence of direct transmission. Label scholarship published after 2013 NEW.
• Break bundles into independently assessed claims. Identify organisms to genus or species only when the evidence supports that precision; current scientific names and synonyms should be sourced. Avoid speculative diagnoses and physiology stated as fact. Do not repeat stereotyped claims about named peoples as ethnographic reality; describe the Book's language as a claim and consult relevant Indigenous/region-specific scholarship where possible.

ALREADY CHECKED — do not spend time re-establishing these except to address an open question: in 1841 Blyth printed the Pamir sheep name and cited Polo (project S20-E17). Siriwardana et al. (2024, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10761-024-00739-5) report *Pinctada fucata* as 81% of their excavated assemblage, cite early textual evidence for the fishery, and date an Arippu midden to the 15th–17th centuries; they do not establish uninterrupted use of the settlement from the fifth century BCE. Brito, Jordão and Pierce (online 2015; journal 2016, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0025315415000910), abstract, place ambergris in sperm-whale intestines and treat irritation by cephalopod beaks as a possible mechanism, not a demonstrated “biliary secretion.” Oxford University Herbaria distinguishes Asian sappanwood from American brazilwood and notes that both yield brazilin; that does not alone identify Polo's plant. A review of Freedman's Out of the East cited in the previous report was written by John H. Munro, not Ronald Munro.
```
