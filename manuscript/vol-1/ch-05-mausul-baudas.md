# Chapter I-5
## Mausul, Baudas, and the Mountain That Moved

> *“Now I will tell you of a great miracle that befell at Baudas, wrought by God on behalf of the Christians.”*
> — Marco Polo, Book 1, ch. 6 (Yule–Cordier)

---

Step across the south-eastern frontier of Greater Hermenia (Greater Armenia) and you walk straight into a fabric whose name outlasted empires. The Book calls the country Mausul (Mosul), gives its great traders the name *Mosolins*, and stamps that same name on the fine cloth they ship west [1]—the ancestor of our own *muslin*. Packhorses haul local cottons to the next town, while the Mosolins’ richer wares—pearls, spices, silk, and cloth of gold—travel all the way to the Levant. One word heard in a European drapery shop opens the gate to the region, and a Venetian listener knows at once what sort of cargo is piled on the counter.

See [the map of the outbound road, I-3](ch-03-second-setting-forth.md#map-of-the-outbound-road).

Turn that bale over in your hands for a moment. What was the stuff actually woven from? The Book speaks of silk and gold, though those richer threads may well have slipped up from the merchants’ other wares on the line below [2]. Out in the surrounding towns of Mus and Merdin, weavers under Mongol overlordship churned out cotton and buckram in abundance [3]. Around Mausul itself, the old order had already fallen: the city surrendered in the summer of 1262, and its last ruler al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl was carried off and killed [4].

A merchant riding in with silver coin never asks only what the local looms weave. He asks whose tax-collectors sit at the gate and whether the citadel’s garrison keeps the mountain passes open. Around Mausul, Mongol governors now gave those answers.

Alongside the Muslim Arabi, the Book finds Nestorian and Jacobite Christians living under their Jatolic (Catholicos), who dispatches bishops and priests as far as India and Cathay—much as the Pope does from Rome [5]. For a Venetian trader wondering who might look after his nephew at the far end of the Gulf, a Christian church with reach matching his own ships was worth remembering. Up in the steep hills, though, live Kurdish bands—both Muslims and Christians—whom travellers are warned to watch as fearless robbers [6]. A merchant trudging beside loaded mules through a narrow defile has one view of the passes; the hillmen watching from the ridge above have another. Follow the river south instead, and the highway turns to water.

At Baudas (Baghdad), bales go straight aboard Tigris river-craft bound for the sea. Downstream lies Bastra (Basra), ringed by palm groves yielding the finest dates on earth [7], and from Baudas the Book reckons eighteen days to Kisi (Kish) and the Indian Sea. That brisk merchant’s summary leaps clean across a long stretch of open Gulf water between the river-mouth and the island [8].

Even so, a factor loading a barge on the Tigris quay had plenty to tempt him: silk and cloth of gold woven under names European buyers knew by heart—*nasich*, *nac*, and *cramoisy*—worked with birds and beasts [9]. When a rich Baudas brocade was strung overhead as a ceremonial canopy for a prince or a bishop in Italy, the canopy itself took the city’s name: *baldachin* [10]. Picture a crowd craning their necks at a feast-day procession in Venice, standing beneath the far end of the same loom-trade as a clerk tallying oiled bales onto a Tigris barge.

Pause on that riverbank to feel what those cloths actually were. *Nasich* and *nac* were silk brocades shot with stiff gold thread that caught torchlight across a banquet hall; *cramoisy* was silk steeped in deep crimson dye. Packing them into waxed tarpaulins against river-spray and river-pirates was a gamble, yet one chest landed safely at a Mediterranean port could pay a crew’s wages for a year.

Imagine a Venetian buyer running his thumb across a bolt of *nasich* in a sunlit loggia back home. He sees only the shimmer of gold thread and the price scrawled in his ledger. Behind that single bolt stand weavers at Baudas looms, Tigris boatmen poling past palm groves, factors bargaining on the Bastra quays, and camel-drivers hauling chests across the desert to the Mediterranean. Every luxury fabric in a European palace carries half a continent of sweat in its weave.

---

To explain how Baudas fell, the Book throws open the doors of a palace vault. Alaü (Hulagu) stormed the Abbasid capital in 1258—dated 1255 in the Book’s telling [11]—against a garrison the story swells to over a hundred thousand horsemen plus infantry besides. How could such a host lose? The answer stands inside a tower packed to the rafters with gold, silver, and precious stones [12].

Look at the two men facing each other across those gleaming heaps: the sovereign who hoarded metal, and the conqueror who brought steel. Alaü demands to know why a ruler sitting on a mountain of coin refused to spend a penny hiring soldiers to hold his walls. The Calif has no answer. Then let him eat his gold, Alaü replies, and shuts the heavy treasury door upon him without bread or water. Four days later, the story opens the door again, and the Calif lies dead among his treasure [13].

It is pure chivalric theatre—a moral fable staged inside a locked vault where every coin mocks the man who hoarded it. Listen closely to that fable, because once Alaü delivers his speech at the treasury door, the ending feels decided before the iron bolts are drawn. Imagine a crowded Venetian dining hall listening to that moment: every merchant in the room knows that gold locked in a cellar earns no interest and hires no watchmen, and that a ruler who starves his own garrison for silver ends up starving beside it.

Behind the stagecraft, chroniclers across the Islamic world and Christendom argued for generations over how the last Abbasid caliph, al-Mustaʿṣim, actually met his end. Some repeated the starvation-and-gold taunt; others pictured Alaü confronting him with a golden tray before having him rolled inside a carpet and trampled to death so no royal blood would spill upon the ground [14].

In reality, part of the plunder went east to the Great Kaan and part stayed with Alaü [15], and while the caliphate died in Baudas, a surviving Abbasid line kept the title alive in Cairo under Mamluk protection [16]. By the time young Marco could have passed this way, the siege smoke had long cleared. Neither the assault nor the opening of the tower could have met his eyes; what he gathered was the dinner-table legend still echoing through the caravanserais. A merchant listening over a bowl of lentils remembers the moral about hoarded gold far longer than the exact fate of a palace.

Now raise a hill outside Baudas’s walls and look down at the city below. Here sits the Calif of the Book’s second great drama—placed in 1275 in some copies and 1225 in others [17]—bent on forcing his Christian subjects to convert or die. Seizing on the Gospel promise of *“faith as a grain of mustard seed”*, he summons the Christians, points to the hill beyond the ramparts, and gives them ten days to move it by prayer—or take his law or the sword [18].

For eight days and nights, men, women, and children fast and weep inside their churches while the clock runs down. Then an angel visits a bishop in a dream and names their unlikely champion: a one-eyed cobbler [19]. Picture the bishop ducking into that cramped lane-side workshop, surrounded by leather scraps and wooden lasts. The cobbler splits his daily copper earnings between his own table and the poor, fasts, and hears Mass. Years before, while fitting a woman’s shoe, he felt a flash of stray desire; after she walked out, he picked up his stitching awl and put out his own eye [20].

There is no marble basilica in this scene, no gilded altar—just a cobbler’s stool, a sharp awl, and a tradesman who takes the Gospel at its word. If the Calif thought he was challenging the proud bishops of Baudas, the story sends him to the humblest bench in the alley.

Listen to the rhythm of the story as an audience in a medieval hall would have heard it. First comes the grand political threat from the palace throne; then eight days of weeping and fasting while the clock ticks down; then a quiet knock on a poor man’s door in a back lane. Every listener knows that the bishop in silk vestments cannot move the mountain, and that the man who already gave away his eye for his faith is the only person in the city light enough in spirit to lift a hill.

The cobbler protests that he is a sinner unworthy of such grace, until the weeping congregation wears him down [21]. On the tenth morning, after Mass, a vast procession—over a hundred thousand strong, the Book claims—marches out behind a lifted cross singing hymns. Those at the tail of the column cannot even see the Calif’s waiting battle-lines drawn up across the plain until the whole crowd halts beneath the hill [22].

The bishop gives his blessing. The cobbler drops to his knees and lifts his hands to heaven. Before the terrified eyes of the sovereign and his court, the hill heaves up and shifts to the exact spot the Calif had named [23]. Only years later, when the ruler’s body is washed for burial, does the sequel come to light: a small cross hangs secretly around his neck, and he is laid to rest apart from his forebears [24]. To this day, Eastern Christians keep the deliverance with an annual feast and a strict vigil fast the night before, refusing even a sip of water until the liturgy ends [25]. Walk into that service for the first time and you ask what it commemorates; start from the fast, and the miracle explains how the congregation gained another day on its calendar.

Walk round the hill for a moment, and you hear how widely that story travelled. Other tellings set the same moving mountain near Tauris (Tabriz), or across the desert in Egypt at the hill of Muqaṭṭam—proof of how easily a great folk-miracle packed its bags along the caravan routes [26]. The Book closes the episode with a sweeping polemic against Muslims everywhere, turning a tale of Christian deliverance into a blanket indictment [27]. Yet the two Baudas stories ask very different things of a visitor. A merchant who missed the 1258 siege could still inspect broken walls and talk to Tigris boatmen; a traveller hunting for the moved hill needed a local guide to point at empty ground and swear a miracle once stood there.

Whether the Polos ever walked the lanes of Mausul and Baudas in person remains an open question [28]. A merchant could handle *mosolin* and *baldachin* silk in half a dozen bazaars without ever boarding a Tigris barge. If you want to catch up with the caravan now, follow the dealers riding east out of the river plain into the covered jewel and silk market of Tauris (Tabriz), where gold-bird brocades gleam beside handfuls of rubies and pearls.

## Notes

[1] ✓ Polo derives the merchants and the cloth from Mausul. Pelliot finds forms of the name of the people of the city in papal lists of 1244, 1258 and 1291 [@yc, 1.5 n. 3; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Mosul”].

[2] ~ Yule connects the word for the cloth to muslin. Pelliot suspects the fibre and metal in the description of the cloth have been copied from the next line, on the goods the merchants sell [@yc, 1.5 n. 3; @pelliot1959notes, vol. 2, “Mosul”].

[3] ✓ Shared by the V witness and Ramusio. Yule brackets the passage, and V also names Mus and Merdin and describes their cotton, buckram and trade under the Mongol king [@yc, 1.5 n. 5; @simion2019devisement, 13.9].

[4] ✓ The surrender belongs to July–August 1262, a year later than Yule gives. Yürekli reports the capture and subsequent death of al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl, whose precise date and manner of death remain open [@yurekli2013musul; @jackson2017mongols; @yc, 1.5 n. 1].

[5] ~ The name Jatolic stands for Catholicos. Yule describes the reach of the Church of the East across Asia, while Polo compresses the distinctions among Eastern churches and explains them to a Latin Christian audience [@yc, 1.5 n. 2].

[6] ~ Yule compares Polo with Ricold of Montecroce on the Kurds and on the dangers to travellers. The description of a whole people as robbers is Polo’s and Ricold’s generalisation [@yc, 1.5 n. 4].

[7] ✓ Bastra stood near the head of the Gulf and was known for its dates. Yule compares medieval notices of its exports and groves [@yc, 1.6 n. 3].

[8] ✗ Polo’s reckoning leaves the impression that the river leads straight to Kisi. Yule points out how much of the Gulf a ship would have to traverse to reach the island [@yc, 1.6 n. 2].

[9] ✓ Yule compares the textile names with the forms listed by Pegolotti and other writers. He also cites evidence on the long trade in gold cloth patterned with animals [@yc, 1.6 n. 4].

[10] ✓ Yule traces “baldachin” through a name for the cloth of Baudas. The name subsequently passed to the canopy the cloth served to make [@yc, 1.6 n. 4].

[11] ✓ The sack of the Abbasid capital took place in February 1258. Yule’s translation keeps the Book’s 1255 and Cordier supplies the historical date [@yc, 1.6 nn. 1, 5].

[12] ~ The Book gives the garrison more than a hundred thousand horsemen and has Alaü discover exceptional riches in a tower. The number and single store of fabulous wealth belong to Polo’s siege story [@yc, 1.6].

[13] ~ Polo makes the Calif’s inability to feed himself with his treasure the answer to his failure to spend it on the defence. Yule records related tellings in Hayton and other writers [@yc, 1.6 n. 7].

[14] ? Neggaz quotes Hayton and the account attributed to Ṭūsī, discusses Rashīd al-Dīn’s starvation episode and execution, and reads the recurrence of the treasure taunt across versions. Cordier quotes Le Strange’s report of Ibn al-Furāt, whose account is later, and gives the execution at Waqf in Yule’s account of Rashīd. Confinement and execution can occur in the same narrative [@neggaz2020deaths; @yc, 1.6 n. 7].

[15] ✓ Yule’s note records one portion of the plunder sent away and another placed in safekeeping towards Lake Urmia. A division of the spoils is a separate claim from the treasure tower tale [@yc, 1.6 n. 6].

[16] ✗ The Book ends the caliphate everywhere with the taking of Baudas. Yule notes a surviving Abbasid caliphate under Mamluk rule in Cairo [@yc, 1.6 n. 8].

[17] ? The date 1275 is in the Geographic Text and Pauthier, and 1225 is Ramusio’s reading. Cordier lists two caliphs for 1225, while the city’s fall in 1258 rules out 1275 for a reigning Abbasid in Baudas [@yc, 1.7 n. 1].

[18] ~ The mustard seed is the Gospel image the Calif takes up for his ultimatum. The ten days and the three choices are given in the miracle story [@yc, 1.7].

[19] ~ The community spends eight days and nights praying in Polo’s telling. The answer is delivered in the bishop’s vision, which recurs before he tells the Christians about it [@yc, 1.8–9].

[20] ~ The cobbler’s gifts, fasting and Mass shape his reputation in the story. He acts on a saying about the offending eye in Matthew 5:29 when he uses the awl [@yc, 1.8].

[21] ~ The persuasion of the cobbler is a separate stage of the legend. His doubts about his own goodness come before his agreement to pray [@yc, 1.9].

[22] ~ The early Mass, high count, cross and armed men make up the Book’s account of the procession. The stated number belongs to the tale [@yc, 1.10].

[23] ~ In the legend the cobbler is blessed by the bishop and prays before the cross. The hill moves with both communities there to see it [@yc, 1.10].

[24] ~ Polo has the Calif converted in secret. The cross becomes known at his death and explains the separate burial within the logic of the story [@yc, 1.10].

[25] ~ Ramusio-only. Yule quotes the description of the continued observance with the fast on the eve of the feast [@yc, 1.10 n. 1].

[26] ? Yule traces the relocation to Latin copies and to Gravina between Tauris and Nakhchivan. He compares Gregory Thaumaturgus, reports of travelling mountains near Mecca, and a tale heard about the Egyptian Muqaṭṭam. Neggaz also makes the comparison with the Egyptian story. The route by which any of these tales reached the Book remains open [@yc, 1.10 n. 1; @neggaz2020deaths].

[27] ✗ The Book uses the Christian victory to support a general claim that Islam commands hostility to Christians. That is the polemic in this telling of the miracle [@yc, 1.10].

[28] ? The account shows knowledge of city names, merchandise and stories on the caravan routes. Neither it nor the comparisons here establishes a personal visit, and the date of the conquest rules out the younger Polo seeing it [@yc, 1.5–10].
